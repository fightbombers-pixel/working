"We voted, and you're not family anymore."

My wife stood up at her mother's Thanksgiving table and said it with a glass of white wine raised in the air, in front of twenty-three people, two turkeys and a ham, and the whole room cheered like the Chiefs had just scored.

Her brother whistled through his fingers. Her cousin's husband banged the table so hard the cranberry sauce jumped.

I was sitting with a dinner roll in my hand that I'd bought at Dillons that morning, because her mother asked me to, and I remember thinking that I had been the license on the wall of this family's business for sixteen years, and not one person at that table had ever asked me what that cost.

So I laughed. Softly. I couldn't help it.

Then I reached under my chair, picked up the blue folder I'd brought in from the truck, and set it on the table in front of her plate, right next to the gravy boat.

"Then I guess you won't need this," I said.

Forty seconds later, her brother was reading page one out loud in his mother's dining room, and his hands were shaking so hard the paper rattled.

What none of them knew was why the City of Wichita's building department had called me at work on the Tuesday before Thanksgiving.

My name is Willis Duarte. I'm 57 years old, and for twenty-four years I've been the lead HVAC mechanic at St. Francis hospital in Wichita, Kansas. I keep the air moving in the operating rooms, the NICU and the cancer ward, and if I do my job right, nobody ever knows my name.

In a hospital, everything you do gets written down. Every work order, every badge swipe, every valve you touch has a time next to it. The old-timers have a saying: if it isn't in the log, it didn't happen. Hold on to that.

Rosa looked at the folder like it was a casserole somebody brought that she didn't trust.

"What is this?"

"Open it."

"Is this some kind of joke?"

"You're the one who held a vote."

She flipped the cover open, and the cheering died off table by table, the way it does when people in the back realize the people in the front have stopped.

She read the first page. Then she read it again. Then she started screaming.

"No. No, no, no. You can't do this. You can't do this!"

"What is it?" her mother said. "Rosa, what is it?"

Rosa threw the page across the table at her brother. Ray Carrillo caught it against his chest, and he read it, and that's when the paper started rattling.

"To the Metropolitan Area Building and Construction Department," he read. "Effective Monday, November 30, I, Willis Duarte, holder of Master Mechanical License number..." He stopped. He looked up at me. "You're pulling your license?"

"I'm withdrawing as the qualifier for Carrillo Heating and Air. Yes."

"On Monday?"

"On Monday."

"Willis, I've got forty jobs open. I've got eleven guys on payroll. I've got a hospital wing in Derby we're bidding on."

"Then you'll want a licensed qualifier by Monday."

"Nobody can get licensed by Monday!"

"I know," I said. "That's why I'm telling you on Thursday."

Let me explain, because most people outside the trade don't know this. In Wichita, you can't pull a permit for heating and air work unless the company has a licensed master mechanic standing behind it. The qualifier. His name goes on the license board, his number goes on every permit, and if somebody puts in a furnace wrong and a family gets carbon monoxide poisoning, it's his license and his name in the newspaper. Back in 2009, Ray wanted to start his own shop and couldn't pass the master's test. Rosa asked me to put my license behind him. I did. For sixteen years, I've signed off on his work, checked his worst jobs on my Saturdays, and never taken a dime.

Rosa ran the office. Payroll, invoices, scheduling. Sixty-two thousand a year. From her brother's company. That only existed on paper because of me.

"There's a second page," I said.

Rosa grabbed the folder back, flipped the page, and stopped screaming. That was worse. She just stared.

"You filed," she said.

"Wednesday morning. Sedgwick County District Court. You'll be served next week. I thought you'd want to see it first."

"You filed for divorce the day before Thanksgiving."

"You held a vote on Thanksgiving. I figured we were both busy."

At the end of the table, Malcolm Vinson set down his glass of iced tea. Malcolm was 44, Ray's business partner for the last two years, a big good-looking guy who chewed his ice like it owed him money. He'd been sitting at Rosa's right hand all afternoon, which is where I used to sit, and nobody had said a word about it.

"Willis," he said, in that calm way he had, like a salesman closing a deal. "Let's not do this in front of the family."

"You're not my family," I said. "I just got voted out. You must've been the deciding vote."

A couple of people at the kids' table actually laughed. Rosa's mother, Consuelo, did not.

"You ungrateful man," Consuelo said. "After everything this family has done for you."

"What's that, Consuelo?"

"We took you in!"

"I was 25 and I had a job. You took in my paycheck. There's a difference."

Ray stood up, still holding the letter.

"Willis. Brother. Come on. We can talk about this. Whatever's going on with you and Rosa, that's you and Rosa. The business is separate."

"It's not separate. It's got my name on it."

"So we'll get our own guy. Malcolm's been studying for the master's test."

"Malcolm failed the master's test twice," I said. "In March and in August. The city keeps a list."

Malcolm stopped chewing.

Ray turned around slowly and looked at his partner.

"You told me you passed the written."

"Ray, sit down," Malcolm said.

"You told me you passed the written in August."

"I said sit down."

I stood up, pushed my chair in, and took my coat off the back of it.

"There's a third thing in the folder, Ray," I said. "But it's not for Thanksgiving. Ask your partner about it before Monday."

Then I walked out of Consuelo Carrillo's house with twenty-three people staring at my back, and the last thing I heard before the storm door shut was my wife's voice, high and wild, yelling, "You'll regret this, Willis! You hear me? You'll regret this!"

My son Mateo was sitting in my truck in the driveway.

I hadn't seen him leave the table. He was 17, a junior at Northwest High, six-foot-one and still growing, with his mom's dark eyes and my big hands. He'd been quiet all day. He'd been quiet for about three weeks, actually, and I'd put it down to being 17.

"Hey," I said, climbing in.

"Hey."

"You okay?"

"You filed for divorce?"

"Yeah."

He looked out the windshield at his grandmother's porch light.

"Good," he said.

That was all. I started the truck. It was already sleeting, little needles of ice coming down sideways across the headlights, and the radio said the whole county was under an ice storm warning until morning.

"Dad," he said, when we were halfway home on Maize Road. "Did you know about Malcolm before today?"

I kept my eyes on the road.

"Three weeks ago, I went to Applebee's on Rock Road to pick up a to-go order. Your mom and Malcolm were in the corner booth. She was holding his hand across the table. She didn't see me."

He nodded slowly.

"I knew too," he said. "I didn't know how to tell you."

"You don't have to tell me anything, Matty. That's not your job."

He looked at me, and for a second he seemed like he wanted to say more. Then he looked away.

"Okay," he said.

We got home a little after seven. Rosa came home at nine-thirty, with Malcolm's black Silverado idling at the curb until she got inside, and that told me plenty. She came straight into the kitchen, where I was ironing my work shirts for the week like I always do on Thursday nights, creases sharp enough to cut paper, because my old man was an Army cook and he ironed everything, including his jeans.

"You humiliated me," she said.

"You stood up and announced a vote."

"That was a family decision!"

"About me. Without me. I've been married to you for twenty-six years, Rosa. When did my family start voting on me?"

"When you stopped being a husband!"

"When was that?"

She opened her mouth and nothing came out. Then she went to her favorite weapon, which was always the scorecard. Rosa kept score of everything. Who paid for dinner in 2004. Who forgot whose birthday.

"You missed my birthday dinner last year for a chiller."

"The chiller was for the cancer ward, and it was 104 degrees outside."

"You missed Mateo's homecoming."

"I didn't. I was in the third row. You were in the parking lot on your phone."

"You work every holiday."

"I work every holiday because somebody has to keep the air on in a hospital. You know that. You knew that when you married me." I put the iron down. "Rosa. How long with Malcolm?"

She lifted her chin.

"Since June."

"June."

"He makes me feel alive. You make me feel like an old woman waiting for a furnace to break."

"Okay."

"Okay? That's it? I tell you I've been with another man since June and you say okay?"

"I found out three weeks ago, at Applebee's. I've already said everything else to myself in the truck."

She came around the ironing board and pointed at me.

"You pull that license on Monday, and Ray loses everything. I lose my job. Malcolm loses his investment. Do you understand that?"

"I understand that Malcolm loses his investment."

"He put eighty thousand dollars into that company!"

"Then he should've put in a license. That's the one thing it didn't have."

"You are going to regret this, Willis. I am going to make sure you regret this. You won't sleep another night in this house. I promise you that."

That was the second time she'd said it. I should have written it down. In a hospital, you write everything down.

At twenty to eleven, my phone rang. It was the hospital.

"Willis, it's Bernadine on NICU," the charge nurse said. "Air handler six just tripped. The ice took out a damper motor on the roof, we're losing pressure in the isolation rooms, and we've got nine babies in here. The on-call guy can't get out of his driveway in Andover."

"I'm coming."

"Roads are bad."

"I'll drive slow."

I grabbed my coat and my tool bag. Mateo was standing at the top of the stairs in his socks.

"Where are you going?"

"Roof of the NICU. Babies need air."

"In this?"

"In this."

Rosa was standing in the living room doorway with her phone in her hand, already typing something. She didn't look up.

I drove to St. Francis at fifteen miles an hour with my hazards on, badged in through the loading dock a little after eleven, and climbed up to the east tower roof in an ice storm to fix a damper motor while nine babies breathed underneath me.

I didn't come home until almost six in the morning.

And by noon the next day, my wife had sworn to a judge that I'd been at home at one in the morning, drunk, throwing a lamp at her head.

The deputy found me in the St. Francis parking garage at two-thirty Friday afternoon, as I was walking back to my truck after a four-hour nap in the boiler room break chair and another shift checking the roof.

He was young and polite and clearly hated this part of his job.

"Willis Duarte?"

"That's me."

"Sir, I'm with the Sedgwick County Sheriff's Office. I need to serve you with a temporary Protection from Abuse order." He held out an envelope. "You're ordered to stay away from the residence on Ridgeport Drive and from the petitioner, Rosa Duarte, until a hearing on December 17th. You can arrange with the sheriff's office to collect personal belongings with an escort."

I took the envelope.

"Can I read it?"

"Yes, sir. I'd recommend it."

I opened it right there next to my truck. The petition was four pages, and the part that mattered was in Rosa's own handwriting, in that tight little cursive she used for invoices.

"On the night of November 26, at about 1:00 a.m., my husband came home drunk after leaving the house angry. He yelled at me in the living room and threatened to ruin my family. He threw a lamp at my head. It missed me and broke against the wall. I am afraid for my safety and my son's safety."

At the bottom, above her signature, it said she swore under penalty of perjury that every word was true.

At about 1:00 a.m., I'd been on my knees on an icy roof sixty feet in the air with a flashlight in my teeth.

"Sir?" the deputy said. "You all right?"

"I'm fine." I folded the paper. "Deputy, can I ask you something? Off the record."

"Depends."

"If somebody writes down a time on one of these, and swears to it, does that time matter?"

He looked at me for a second.

"It's the first thing a judge reads," he said.

I called Deanna Royce from the truck. She'd done my mother's will and my cousin's DUI, and she has a voice like a gravel driveway.

"A temporary PFA," she said. "Did you throw a lamp?"

"I was on the roof of the NICU."

"At one in the morning."

"From a little after eleven to almost six."

"Can you prove that?"

"I work in a hospital, Deanna. Everything I do gets written down."

She was quiet for a moment, then she laughed. Not a big laugh. Just one little puff of air, like a woman who has been waiting all week for something to go right.

"Don't call your wife. Don't text your wife. Don't drive past your house. Don't argue with anybody on Facebook. Get me every record that hospital has of you that night, and get me the names of anybody who saw you. And Willis?"

"Yeah."

"Do not let her know what you've got. Let her keep the 1:00 a.m. She wrote it down. Let it sit there."

That night I checked into the Candlewood Suites on East Kellogg with two duffel bags and my ironing board, which the deputy had let me take from the garage, because I'm not going to go to work in a wrinkled shirt just because my wife lied to a judge.

Saturday morning, I drove out to Gus Kimball's place in Valley Center.

Gus is 66. He was chief engineer at St. Francis for thirty years before me and trained me when I was a kid with a bad mustache. He lives on four acres with a pole barn full of old compressors he refuses to throw away, and he drinks his coffee out of the same chipped thermos he used on the night shift in 1987.

I showed him the petition. He read it with his reading glasses on the end of his nose, then took them off and folded them.

"One in the morning," he said.

"One in the morning."

"Well, she's either a liar or she's got the worst sense of time in Kansas." He poured me coffee. "What's the rule, Willis?"

"If it isn't in the log, it didn't happen."

"And the other rule."

I thought about it. Gus had a lot of rules.

"Say nothing."

"Say nothing. Let her put it in writing, and then let her say it again in front of people." He tapped the paper. "She's already done the first part. Your job is to make sure she does the second."

"She'll find out I was at work. Everybody saw me leave."

"Did she see you leave?"

"She was in the doorway on her phone."

"Then she's betting you won't be able to prove where you went after you left. People who don't work in hospitals don't know how many doors there are between the parking lot and the roof." He smiled. "How many doors are there, Willis?"

"Seven badge readers between the loading dock and the east tower roof hatch."

"And every one of them writes down your name and the minute."

"Every one."

"Then go get your minutes."

Monday morning, I went to the facilities office and filed a request, through the proper channel, for my own badge access history and my work orders for the night of November 26th. The security manager printed it out for me in twenty minutes and signed the bottom, because when you've fixed the man's office air conditioner three summers in a row, he doesn't make you wait.

Two pages. Seventeen badge swipes. Loading dock, a little after eleven. Service elevator. Fourth floor mechanical. East tower stairwell. Roof hatch, over and over, every time I came down to warm my hands and went back up. The last roof hatch swipe was at 5:40 in the morning. The loading dock on the way out, a few minutes later.

Then the work order. Air handler six, damper motor failure, emergency call-in, opened by charge nurse Bernadine Lucero, closed by Willis Duarte, with a note in my handwriting: "Replaced actuator, ice in linkage, NICU pressure restored." And under that, the list of people who'd signed for the emergency call-in: two NICU nurses, the charge nurse, the house supervisor, the security officer who unlocked the roof door for me, and my apprentice, Corey, who had driven in from Park City at midnight with a spare actuator because I called him.

Six people. Six signatures.

I sat in my truck in the hospital garage holding those two pages, and I laughed out loud, because it was either that or cry, and I'd already done my crying in the shower at the Candlewood on Saturday night, where nobody could hear.

Then my phone rang. It was a number from the city.

"Mr. Duarte? This is Kenny Alvarado, I'm the mechanical inspector you talked to last Tuesday. I got your withdrawal letter this morning. I need you to come look at something before the end of the day. Can you meet me at the Carrillo Heating and Air shop on West Street? Because I think somebody's been signing your name."

I'd known that since Tuesday. That was the third thing in the folder.

Here's what had happened. The Tuesday before Thanksgiving, Kenny Alvarado had been inspecting a furnace install in a new subdivision out in Goddard, done by Carrillo Heating and Air, and the install was garbage. Wrong flue pipe, no permit sticker on the gas line. When he called the qualifier listed on the permit to ask what in the world was going on, he called me. And I had never seen that house, never seen that permit, and never signed my name on it.

So I asked him to email me a copy of the permit. And the signature on it looked like my name, written by a man who had only ever seen it once.

When I pulled into the gravel lot on West Street that afternoon, Kenny was standing next to his city truck with a clipboard, and Ray Carrillo was standing in the open bay door of the shop with his arms crossed. My framed license was still hanging in the office window behind him, where it had hung for sixteen years.

"Willis," Ray said. "You've got some nerve."

"I came to get my license off your wall. And Kenny asked me to come."

"Rosa says you threw a lamp at her."

"Rosa says a lot of things. Ask her what time."

"She says one in the morning."

"Then she's going to have a long day on December 17th."

Kenny cleared his throat.

"Gentlemen. I've got nine permits here," he said. "Pulled between June and November under Mr. Duarte's license number. Nine jobs. All of them signed as qualifier 'W. Duarte.'" He held up the clipboard. "Mr. Duarte, did you sign any of these?"

I looked at them one by one, slowly, so Ray could see me look.

"No," I said. "Not one."

Ray's arms came down.

"What do you mean, not one? Willis, you've been signing my permits since 2009."

"I've been signing them since 2009. I stopped getting them in June. I figured you'd slowed down. I didn't think anything of it." I pointed at the clipboard. "Somebody's been pulling them without me. And signing my name."

Ray took the clipboard from Kenny, and I watched him look at the signatures. Then I watched him look up across the lot, at the black Silverado parked at the side of the shop, and at the man getting out of it.

Malcolm walked over chewing ice from a QuikTrip cup.

"What's going on?"

"Kenny says somebody's been signing Willis's name on permits," Ray said. "Since June."

"Huh," Malcolm said. "That's weird."

"June's when you took over the permit desk, Malcolm," Ray said quietly. "You said it'd save Rosa time."

"Lots of people go in and out of that office."

"The W's got a little loop on it. Like the way you sign the timecards."

"Ray, come on. You believe him over me? He's the one trying to kill your business."

Ray handed the clipboard back to Kenny without looking at it again.

"Kenny," he said. "What happens now?"

Kenny looked uncomfortable.

"Well, the nine jobs get red-tagged until a licensed man inspects them. The forgery part isn't my department. That goes in my report, and somebody downtown decides what to do with it. And until there's a new qualifier, the company can't pull permits."

"How long does it take to get a new qualifier?"

"Somebody with a master's license has to agree to stand behind you. Most guys won't stand behind a company that's got nine red tags and a forged signature in the file."

Ray turned around and looked at his partner. Malcolm stopped chewing.

"You told me you passed the written," Ray said. "You told me you'd get your own license by Christmas, and we wouldn't need Willis anymore. You told me—" He stopped. "Rosa told me this was all Willis's fault. That Willis was holding us back."

"Ray, buddy—"

"You were signing his name. You were sleeping with my sister and signing her husband's name on gas lines."

Malcolm threw the QuikTrip cup on the gravel. Ice went everywhere.

"You want to do this here? Fine. Your sister came to me. Not the other way around. She said Willis was a pushover, that he'd sign anything Rosa put in front of him, that he'd never notice. She said, 'Just do what he does, nobody ever checks.'"

Ray swung at him.

It wasn't much of a punch, and Malcolm was a bigger man, but it caught him on the ear and he went sideways into the bay door, and then they were both on the gravel, rolling, and Kenny was yelling, "Hey! Hey!" and I grabbed Ray by the back of his work coat and hauled him off before Malcolm could get on top of him.

"Get off my property," Ray said, breathing hard, blood on his knuckles. "Get your truck and your eighty thousand dollars and get off my property."

"That money's in this company!"

"Then sue me," Ray said. "Get in line."

Malcolm got up, wiped his ear, looked at me like he'd like to kill me, and walked to his Silverado. He spun the tires on the way out and sprayed gravel all over Kenny's city truck.

Kenny wrote that down too.

Ray sat on the bumper of his service van and held his hand. Neither of us said anything for a while.

"She called me Thanksgiving night," he finally said. "Late. Real upset. She said she had to get you out of the house before Monday, before you pulled the license. She said she had a way."

"What time did she call?"

He looked at his phone.

"12:48."

"Ray," I said. "I'm going to need you to remember that."

He looked up at me. Then he looked down at his phone again, and I watched him do the math, and I watched him understand what his sister had done.

"Oh, Rosie," he said, very quietly. "Oh, no."

I went into his office, took my license down off the wall, tucked it under my arm, and walked out. On my way to the truck, my phone buzzed.

It was a text from my son.

"Dad can we talk. Not at home. Please."

Mateo met me the next afternoon in the cafeteria at St. Francis, at the table by the window where the night shift eats breakfast. He had taken the city bus from Northwest High, which took an hour and ten minutes, because he didn't want his mom to see his car in the hospital lot.

That was the first time I laughed in two days, right there in the cafeteria, and a couple of nurses at the next table looked over and smiled without knowing why.

The hearing was on Thursday, December 17th, in a small courtroom on the fifth floor of the Sedgwick County Courthouse, the kind with drop ceilings and wood paneling from 1978. Judge Annette Kirkland ran it. She was about sixty, with reading glasses on a beaded chain and the face of a woman who had heard every kind of story there is.

Rosa sat at the petitioner's table with her lawyer, a man named Lester Dunbar in a brown suit. She wore a cardigan and no makeup, which was not how Rosa ever left the house. Her mother sat behind her. Ray sat in the back row on my side of the room, by himself, with his hand still bandaged. Malcolm Vinson sat in the back row on her side, chewing nothing, because they don't let you bring ice into court.

Mateo sat next to Deanna Royce's paralegal, behind me.

Rosa testified first.

She told it the way she wrote it. Thanksgiving. The argument at her mother's house. Me leaving angry. Coming home drunk, late. Yelling. The lamp.

"Mrs. Duarte," Lester Dunbar said gently. "What time did this happen?"

"About one in the morning."

"How do you know?"

"I looked at the clock on the cable box. It said one. I'll never forget it."

"Thank you. Nothing further."

"Yes."

"You're sure."

"I'm positive. I looked right at the clock."

"Yes."

"Yes."

Roof hatch, eleven times between midnight and 5:40 in the morning. One-oh-two in the morning. Roof hatch, east tower. One-nineteen. Roof hatch. Your husband was on the roof of a children's hospital in an ice storm at the very minute you swore he was throwing a lamp at you in your living room."

"Those can be faked," Rosa said. "He works there. He knows the system."

Judge Kirkland took her glasses off and let them hang on the chain.

"Mr. Dunbar," she said. "Do you need a moment with your client?"

Lester Dunbar stood up very slowly.

"Your Honor, my client may have been confused about the time. It was a traumatic night. Perhaps it was earlier. Perhaps—"

"Perhaps it was at ten-thirty," Deanna said, "when Mr. Duarte was in the kitchen ironing his work shirts in front of their son. Your Honor, we have one more witness. Mateo Duarte. He's 17."

Rosa turned around in her chair and looked at our son for the first time since the hearing started. He was already standing up.

"Matty," she said. "Matty, no. Sit down. You don't have to—"

"Mrs. Duarte," the judge said sharply. "You will not speak to the witness."

Mateo walked up and sat in the witness chair, too tall for it, his knees almost hitting the rail. He swore to tell the truth. Deanna asked him to tell the judge what he saw on Thanksgiving night after his father left for work.

He told it the way he'd told me. Malcolm's truck. The yelling. The lamp. Then Deanna handed the judge a printout of his photo, and the judge looked at it for a long time, at the man with his arm coming down and the green numbers on the cable box.

"Twelve forty-one," Judge Kirkland read out loud.

In the back row, Malcolm Vinson got up and walked out of the courtroom. Nobody stopped him. Ray watched him go and didn't move.

"Mateo," the judge said. "What did your mother tell you the next morning?"

He looked at his mother. She was crying now, with both hands over her mouth.

"She told me Dad came home drunk and threw the lamp. She told me I was asleep. She said it three times."

"Thank you, son. You can step down."

He came back and sat behind me. I didn't turn around. I put my hand back over my shoulder, and he took it, and he held on to it the way he used to at the county fair when he was little.

Judge Kirkland put her glasses back on and picked up Rosa's petition.

"Mrs. Duarte. The day after Thanksgiving, you came into this building and you wrote these words, in your own handwriting, and you swore to them. 'He threw a lamp at my head. It missed me and broke against the wall. I am afraid for my safety and my son's safety.'" She set the paper down. "Today you looked me in the eye and told me you looked right at the clock. The clock, as it happens, is in your son's photograph. At that time, your husband was on a hospital roof, and another man was in your living room throwing your lamp."

Rosa didn't say anything. Her mother had her hand on Rosa's shoulder, and she was staring at the floor.

"The petition is denied. The temporary order is dissolved, effective immediately. Mr. Duarte may return to his residence. I'm sending a copy of today's transcript and the petition to the District Attorney's office for review of possible false swearing, and I'm noting for the record that the court has serious concerns about the safety of the minor child in a home where that man is welcome." She looked over her glasses at Lester Dunbar. "Counselor, I'd advise your client to make other arrangements for Mr. Vinson's visits."

"Yes, Your Honor."

"Mr. Duarte."

I stood up.

"Thank you for keeping the air on," Judge Kirkland said. "My granddaughter was in that NICU in October. We're adjourned."

I walked out of that courtroom with my son on one side and my lawyer on the other. Rosa didn't look at me as I passed. Consuelo did. She opened her mouth, and then she closed it again, which in twenty-six years I had never once seen her do.

Out in the hallway, Ray was standing by the elevators with his bandaged hand in his pocket.

"Willis," he said. "I'm sorry. For the vote. For all of it. I raised my glass. I cheered."

"I heard you whistle."

"I know." He shook his head. "I talked to a guy in Derby with a master's license. He'll stand behind me if you inspect the nine red-tagged jobs first and sign off on the fixes. I'll pay you. Full rate. And I'll fix every one of them right."

I looked at him for a long moment.

"Not for money," I said. "For the nine families with bad flue pipes. Saturday. Seven in the morning. Bring coffee and bring the permits, and this time I sign them myself."

"Saturday," he said.

Mateo pushed the elevator button and the doors opened.

"Dad," he said. "Can we go home now?"

I stepped in beside him, and when the doors were closing, I could hear Rosa crying in the courtroom behind us, and her lawyer asking her, very quietly, why she hadn't told him about the boy.

The elevator went down five floors, and we walked out of the Sedgwick County Courthouse into the cold, and my son held the door for me.

"After you, old man," he said.

"Thank you, son," I said. "Now let's go buy your grandma a new lamp."
