"Your daddy promised me half, Mitch. Ask anybody who saw me over at that lake house every single Sunday."

My ex-wife said it in the fellowship hall of First Methodist in Shreveport, Louisiana, at my father's funeral luncheon, with about sixty people holding paper plates of fried chicken and potato salad, and the pastor's wife frozen in the middle of pouring sweet tea. She said it loud enough that the ladies at the dessert table heard it, and the dessert table was forty feet away.

We'd been divorced four months. My father had been in the ground for about forty-five minutes.

Half. Daddy's estate came to about a million one, between the house on Cross Lake, his savings, and the gas royalty checks from the Haynesville wells under the back forty in DeSoto Parish. Half of that went to me, and half to my sister. And now Crystal was standing in a church basement in a black dress she'd bought for the occasion, telling sixty people she was owed half of my half.

I set down my iced tea on the folding table, very carefully, so it wouldn't spill on the tablecloth the church ladies had ironed.

"Then file it, Crystal," I said.

She blinked. She'd come ready for a fight. She didn't know what to do with a man who just handed her the next move.

Four months later, in a courtroom in the Caddo Parish courthouse, a district judge took off her reading glasses and stared down at my ex-wife like Crystal was something the judge had tracked in on her shoe.

What Crystal didn't know was what was in the shoebox behind the seat of my truck, out in the church parking lot. Sixteen little cassette tapes, and one of them had my father's handwriting on the label. Tape fourteen.

My name is Mitch Barr. I'm 54, and I own Barr Diesel on Greenwood Road, where we fix big trucks. Eighteen-wheelers, dump trucks, the oilfield rigs that come up out of the Haynesville with mud to the axles. Thirty-three years of diesel engines have taught me one thing above all the others. An engine will tell you exactly what's wrong with it, if you shut up long enough to listen. Remember that.

"File it?" Crystal said. "That's all you've got? At your own daddy's funeral?"

"You brought it up at my own daddy's funeral."

"Because you won't take my calls!"

"We're divorced, Crystal. That's generally how it works."

A few people laughed, and then remembered where they were and stopped. My sister Glenda came up beside me from the kitchen with a dish towel over her shoulder. She's four years older than me, a retired schoolteacher from Bossier City, and she has never once in thirty years called my ex-wife anything but "that woman."

"Crystal," Glenda said, "there's a parking lot outside. Go stand in it."

"I have every right to be here. Walter was my father-in-law for twenty-six years."

"Four months ago he stopped being your anything," Glenda said.

"He was like a father to me. Everybody knows it." Crystal raised her voice for the people at the folding tables. "I drove out to that lake every Sunday after his diagnosis. Every Sunday! I held his hand. I gave him emotional support when his own son was too busy under a truck. And Walter told me, he told me himself, he said, Crystal, I'm going to make sure you're taken care of."

"When did he say that?" I asked.

"In April. On the porch."

"Daddy couldn't get out to the porch in April. He was on oxygen in the bedroom."

"Well, it was in the bedroom, then! It doesn't matter where!" Her face was getting pink. "My lawyer says an oral promise counts in Louisiana. My lawyer says I've got witnesses. And my lawyer says when a woman gives a dying man emotional support for eight months, she's earned something for it."

"Your lawyer says a lot."

"You'll see what he says. In court."

"I said file it, Crystal. I meant it."

She stood there a second longer. Then she turned around and walked out of the fellowship hall with her heels clicking on the linoleum, and when the door swung open I saw a white Cadillac Escalade idling at the curb with a man at the wheel in a pressed blue shirt and a gold Upton Insurance pin on his pocket.

Dale Upton. The insurance man. The reason we'd been divorced four months.

He tapped the horn twice, the way you'd call a dog in from the yard. Crystal got in. They pulled out of the church lot and turned toward Youree Drive, and the pastor's wife finally finished pouring her sweet tea.

Glenda put her hand on my arm.

"Mitch. What are you going to do?"

"Eat some chicken," I said. "Daddy paid for it."

Three days later, Glenda and I drove out to Daddy's house on Cross Lake to start going through his things.

It's a little brick ranch house on a quarter acre, with a screened porch on the back that looks out at the water and a dock with a johnboat tied up that he hadn't taken out in two years. He bought it in 1979 when he retired from the Army after twenty-six years. Master sergeant. Korea at the very end, Vietnam twice, and Germany, where he met my mother at a dance. She's been gone eleven years. He kept her reading glasses on the kitchen windowsill the whole time.

Glenda took the kitchen. I took the bedroom closet.

That's where I found the shoebox. Top shelf, behind his good Stetson. A Florsheim box from about 1985, with masking tape on the lid and his blocky Army handwriting in black marker.

STORIES FOR THE GRANDKIDS. DO NOT THROW AWAY.

Inside were sixteen cassette tapes, numbered on the labels, one through sixteen. And one of those little handheld tape recorders, the kind reporters used to carry, a Panasonic with a dead battery.

I sat down on the edge of his bed with the box in my lap.

"Glenda. Come look at this."

She came in drying her hands, and sat down next to me, and picked up tape number one. On the label he'd written, "How I met your grandmother. Frankfurt 1961."

"He was doing this for Cody and the girls," she said. Her voice wobbled. "He told me about it at Christmas. He said he wanted them to hear it in his voice, not read it off some computer."

"He never told me."

"He told me he was going to." She picked up another. "Pork Chop Hill, the parts I can say." Another. "Your grandpa and the alligator, 1983." She laughed and wiped her eyes. "Oh, Lord. The alligator."

I picked up tape fourteen.

It was different from the others. The others had titles. This one just had a date, a Sunday in March, and under the date, in the same black marker, three words.

FOR MITCHIE. LATER.

Glenda saw it too. Neither of us said anything for a minute.

Then I remembered something. In May, about a month before he died, I'd been sitting by his bed on a Thursday night watching the Astros lose, and he'd reached over and grabbed my wrist with that big old hand, thin now, all knuckle.

"I put something on the tapes for you, Mitchie," he'd said. "You'll know when."

I'd thought it was the morphine. I'd patted his hand and said, "Okay, Daddy."

I put tape fourteen in my shirt pocket and buttoned the pocket.

"Aren't you going to play it?" Glenda asked.

"Not yet."

"Why not?"

"Because I'm not ready to hear his voice yet," I said. "And because he said I'd know when."

The doorbell rang.

Glenda went to get it. I heard a man's voice at the front door, polite and flat, and then Glenda's, much less polite. Then she came back to the bedroom doorway holding a thick white envelope like it was something the cat dragged in.

"Mitch. There's a process server on Daddy's porch. He says this is for you."

Inside was a petition filed in the First Judicial District Court for the Parish of Caddo. Crystal Upton, formerly Crystal Barr, Plaintiff, versus Mitchell Barr. A claim for two hundred and seventy-five thousand dollars, being one half of the defendant's inheritance from the late Walter Barr, on the grounds of an oral promise of the deceased, and in compensation for, and I'm reading it word for word, "eight months of caregiving services and emotional support provided by Plaintiff to the deceased during his final illness."

I read it twice. Then I laughed, sitting on my dead father's bed, so loud Glenda jumped.

"What's funny?"

"Emotional support," I said. "She's billing us for it."

Maggie Doucet's office is on Texas Street downtown, on the second floor over a bail bonds place, with a window unit air conditioner that rattles like a Peterbilt with a bad bearing. She's in her sixties, a Cajun from Opelousas with a voice like gravel in a coffee can, and she represented my father in 1994 when a neighbor tried to claim half his dock. My father said she was the only lawyer he ever met who could make a judge laugh and a liar cry in the same afternoon.

She read Crystal's petition with a pencil in her teeth.

"Emotional support," she said around the pencil. "That's a new one. I've been doing this thirty-eight years, sugar. I've seen people sue for the china, the dog, the bass boat and once a dead man's dentures. Never seen anybody sue for holding a hand."

"Can she win?"

Maggie took the pencil out. "On the law? No. In Louisiana, what you inherit from your daddy is yours, separate property, and it stays yours. A man can't promise away his estate out loud on his porch. If Walter wanted to leave Crystal something, he'd have needed to put it in his will, in writing, signed in front of a notary and two witnesses. He didn't."

"So it's done."

"I didn't say that. I said on the law." She leaned back, and her chair squeaked. "Her lawyer's a fella named Brett Ledoux. Ambulance chaser with good teeth. He's not going to argue the law. He's going to argue the story. Poor divorced woman. Drove out to the lake every Sunday for eight months. Held the old man's hand. Gave up her Sundays while her ex-husband was under a truck. And then that cold ex-husband gets half a million dollars and she gets nothing." She tapped the petition. "He's going to bring in her friends to swear Walter said things. And he's going to try to get a judge to feel sorry enough to give her something. Quantum meruit, we call it. Payment for services."

"Did she go out there every Sunday?"

"Did she?"

"For about an hour. During the divorce. Most Sundays." I looked at the window unit. "She'd bring a casserole she bought at Brookshire's and put it in her own dish. She'd sit by his bed and hold his hand where the neighbor lady could see her through the window. And then she'd leave."

"And who drove him to chemo?"

"Me. Tuesdays and Fridays. At the Feist-Weiller Cancer Center. Eight months."

"Who sat with him at night?"

"Me and Glenda. Taking turns. And a hospice nurse at the end."

Maggie wrote that down.

"Mitch, I'm going to ask you a hard question, and I want you to answer it straight. Did your daddy ever say anything about Crystal and money? Anything? Even joking?"

I thought about the tape in my shirt pocket. I'd been carrying it around for six days. I'd moved it from shirt to shirt every morning.

"He left me something," I said. "I haven't listened to it yet."

There's an old radio-cassette player on the shelf in my office at the shop, a big silver boombox from 1989 that the guys use to play the Saints games on Sundays when we're behind on a rig. That night, after everybody had gone home, I sat down at my desk with a cold Barq's and put tape fourteen in it and pushed play.

There was a click, and a hiss, and then my father's voice, so close and so clear that I had to put the root beer down.

"This is Grandpa Walt, tape fourteen. Sunday. I'm out here on the porch with my coffee. Crystal's inside with her lady friends doing lunch, so I'm hiding." He chuckled. "Today I'm going to tell you young'uns about the time your daddy, Mitchie, took apart my lawnmower when he was nine years old to see how it worked, and put it back together with three parts left over."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It was true. I still have the three parts.

He told the story. It was a good story. About five minutes in, he stopped in the middle of a sentence.

There were other voices now. Women's voices. Faint, coming through a window or a screen door. A kitchen. Plates. I recognized one of them right away, the way you'd recognize a song you hate.

"...I'm just saying, Sherri, the man's eighty-three with stage four. How long can it be? Summer?"

"Crystal!" A different voice, laughing. "He's right out there on the porch!"

"He's deaf as a post. He's got the TV going." Crystal's voice. Clear as a bell. "Gayle, pour me some more of that. Listen. I don't come out here every Sunday for my health. Mitch gets half of everything when Walter goes. The house, the gas money, all of it. And Brett says if I can show I was here, taking care of him, I can go after Mitch's half. Brett says a judge will eat it up."

"That's cold, Crys."

"It's not cold, it's smart. I sit with him an hour on Sundays and hold his hand where the neighbor lady can see. That's all it takes." A pause. Ice in a glass. "I can't wait for the old man to die so we can access the money. Dale's already looking at boats."

The women laughed. All three of them.

The tape kept running. I could hear my own heartbeat in my ears.

Then there was a creak, close to the microphone. A chair on a porch. And my father's voice came back, low and slow, the way he used to talk when I was a kid and I'd done something I couldn't take back.

"Mitchie. I heard that. Every word. I'm not as deaf as she thinks." A long breath. "I'm not going to say anything to her. Not my place. You were married to her a long time and you never once said an ugly word about her in front of me, and I'm proud of you for that. But I'm leaving this on here, son. I'm putting it in the box. You'll know when."

Another creak. The sound of a coffee cup set down on a wooden rail.

"Now. Where was I. The lawnmower."

And he finished the story, like nothing had happened.

I turned the boombox off. I sat in my office in the dark for a long time with my hand on top of it, like it was warm.

Then I picked up my phone and called Maggie Doucet at home, at ten at night, which I'd never in my life done to anybody.

"Maggie. It's Mitch Barr. I listened to it."

"And?"

"I think Daddy just did your job for you."

Maggie listened to tape fourteen in her office the next morning with the window unit turned off so she could hear it. When it was over, she sat there with her hands folded on her desk, and her eyes were wet, and she didn't bother to hide it.

"Your daddy was a smart old soldier," she said.

"Can we use it? He recorded them without them knowing."

"Louisiana's a one-party state, sugar. If one person in a conversation agrees to the recording, it's legal. But here's the beauty. Walter wasn't even in their conversation. He was on his own porch, recording himself telling a story, on his own property. They talked loud enough through his kitchen window for his machine to hear it." She shook her head. "There's not a judge in Caddo Parish who's going to throw that out. Lord knows I wouldn't."

"So we tell them we have it?"

"We do not." She tapped the desk with one red fingernail. "We disclose it the way the rules say, the week before trial, in a list of exhibits, labeled 'audio recording, Walter Barr, March.' Brett Ledoux's going to think it's an old man telling war stories. And I promise you, he won't listen to the whole thing. Lawyers like him never do."

"What about Sherri and Gayle?"

"Oh, they're going to testify. Brett's already sent me their names. Sherri Landry and Gayle Prejean. Both of them are going to swear they heard your daddy promise Crystal she'd be taken care of." Maggie smiled, and it wasn't a nice smile. "And then they're going to hear themselves on that tape. Under oath. In front of a judge."

"That's perjury."

"That's their problem."

Maggie pulled one more piece of paper across her desk toward me.

"One more thing, Mitch. Your sister brought me your daddy's bank statements yesterday, for the estate. I want you to look at something."

They were copies of checks. Walter's checks, from his account at the Barksdale Federal Credit Union. Made out to cash. Eleven of them, from October to January. All signed by my father, in a shaky hand. All endorsed on the back by Crystal Barr.

They added up to thirty-one thousand four hundred dollars.

"Groceries," Maggie said. "That's what she wrote in the memo line on every one of them. Groceries for Walter. Glenda says your daddy's whole grocery bill for a year wasn't four thousand dollars."

"He'd sign anything she put in front of him at the end. He trusted her."

"I know. And as of this morning, the Estate of Walter Barr, you and your sister, are filing what we call a reconventional demand. That's a countersuit. We're asking the court to make Crystal pay back every dime." She sat back. "She came to court wanting half your inheritance. She's going to leave owing your daddy's estate thirty-one thousand dollars."

"Maggie, she's going to come after me hard when she gets served with that."

"Good," Maggie said. "People make mistakes when they come after you hard."

Crystal didn't come after me herself. She sent Dale.

It was a Thursday in August, about a month before trial, hot enough to fry an egg on the shop floor. I was underneath a Kenworth with a cracked oil pan when I heard a car door shut out front and a man's voice say, "I'm looking for Mitch."

I rolled out on the creeper. Dale Upton was standing in my shop in pressed khakis and a polo shirt with his company logo on it, holding a manila envelope, looking around at the grease and the tools like he might catch something.

He's 55, a big soft man with silver hair and a tan from the golf course at Southern Trace. He's been selling insurance in Shreveport for thirty years and his face is on the bus benches on Youree Drive. Upton Insurance. Your Neighbor You Can Trust.

"Mitch." He held out his hand. I held up mine, black to the wrist with oil. He put his back in his pocket.

"Dale."

"Can we talk somewhere? Man to man?"

"We're in my shop. This is where I talk."

My lead mechanic, a big quiet fella named Terrell Gaines who's been with me nineteen years, stopped wrenching on the next bay over and leaned on the fender of a dump truck to listen. He didn't try to hide it.

"Look." Dale lowered his voice. "Crystal doesn't know I'm here. This is just between us. Nobody wants a trial. A trial's ugly. It's public. Your daddy's name in the newspaper, people saying things." He held up the envelope. "So here's an offer. One hundred thousand dollars. You pay her a hundred thousand, she drops the lawsuit, and you all drop this crazy countersuit about the grocery money. Everybody goes home."

"A hundred thousand dollars."

"That's less than half of what she's asking. That's a bargain, Mitch."

"For holding his hand an hour on Sundays."

Dale's smile slipped. "She was there for him. When you were busy."

"I drove him to chemo twice a week for eight months, Dale. Where were you?"

"I was supporting Crystal."

"With what? You never once set foot in that lake house." I wiped my hands on a rag. "Funny, though. Daddy remembered you. He mentioned you on his tapes."

"His what?"

"He recorded stories for the grandkids. Sixteen tapes. You come up."

Dale looked at me. A good insurance man knows when something's not in the policy. I watched him wonder what the fine print said.

"What did he say?"

"You'll hear it in court."

"Mitch, I'm trying to help you here. Crystal's got two witnesses who'll swear Walter promised her. Two. You've got nothing but your word and a bunch of grocery receipts. Take the hundred."

"Dale," I said, "let me ask you something. You ever buy that boat?"

His face went absolutely still.

"What boat?"

"The one you were looking at in March," I said. "Back when you two were waiting on the old man to die."

From the next bay, Terrell Gaines let out a long, low whistle, and went back to his wrench.

Dale stood there in my shop with his envelope. Then he turned around and walked out without another word, and I heard his Escalade start up and pull out onto Greenwood Road a lot faster than a Neighbor You Can Trust ought to drive.

That night, my son Cody called me from his apartment near LSU Shreveport. He's 22, studying to be a physical therapy assistant, and he looks just like his grandfather did in the Army pictures.

"Dad. Mom called me. She wants me to testify."

"Testify to what?"

"That she went out to see Grandpa every day. Every day, Dad. She wants me to say I saw her out there all the time." His voice cracked. "I saw her out there maybe four times. I was out there every Wednesday. I never saw her on a Wednesday once."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her I'd tell the truth." He took a breath. "She hung up on me."

"I'm sorry, son."

"Dad. Is there something on Grandpa's tapes? Aunt Glenda said there's something on the tapes."

"There is."

"Can I hear it?"

"After the trial," I said. "He made it for later."

The trial was on a Tuesday in October, four months after the funeral, in a courtroom on the fourth floor of the Caddo Parish Courthouse, the big white one downtown with the columns. No jury. Just Judge Dorothy Guidry, who's been on the bench since the first Bush administration and has a reputation for falling asleep during boring testimony and waking up for the good parts.

There were maybe thirty people in the gallery. Glenda. Cody, in a tie. Terrell from the shop. A couple of Daddy's old Army buddies from the VFW post in Bossier, in their caps. Half a dozen of Crystal's friends from her church in Southern Hills. And Dale Upton in the front row behind her, in a navy blazer, with his arm across the back of the bench.

Crystal testified first. She'd had her hair done and she wore a pale blue dress with a little white collar, like a Sunday school teacher. Brett Ledoux walked her through it like a dance he'd practiced.

"Mrs. Upton, how often did you visit Walter Barr during his illness?"

"Every Sunday. Without fail. From October to June."

"What did you do on these visits?"

"I'd bring him a home-cooked meal. I'd sit with him. Hold his hand. Read him the Bible. He'd tell me stories about the Army." She dabbed her eye with a tissue. "He was so lonely. His own son was always at that shop."

"Did Mr. Barr ever say anything to you about his estate?"

"Yes. Many times. He'd say, Crystal, you're more of a daughter to me than anybody. He'd say, don't you worry, I'm going to make sure you're taken care of. He said it right there on his back porch."

"And did you believe him?"

"With my whole heart."

"No further questions."

Maggie stood up. She didn't hurry. She never hurried.

"Mrs. Upton. You said home-cooked meals."

"Yes."

"Where did you cook them?"

"At my house."

"In your kitchen."

"Yes."

"Then I'm curious why, on four of your Sundays, Mr. Barr's neighbor, Mrs. Hattie Fields, saw you take a Brookshire's deli bag out of your trunk and transfer a chicken casserole from a plastic tray into your own Pyrex dish in Walter's driveway."

"That's... I don't... I might have run short on time once or twice."

"Four times."

"Objection," Ledoux said. "Relevance. Who cares where the casserole came from?"

"I'll allow it," Judge Guidry said. "I'm interested in the casserole."

A couple of the VFW men chuckled.

Maggie moved on. "Mrs. Upton, you testified Mr. Barr told you on his back porch that you'd be taken care of. When?"

"April. Late April."

"Mr. Barr's hospice nurse will testify that from the eighth of April he was on oxygen and unable to leave his bed."

"Then it was in his bedroom. I misspoke."

"You misspoke about where a dying man made you a half-million-dollar promise?"

"I was upset!"

"Thank you, Mrs. Upton. That's all for now."

Then came Sherri Landry. Then Gayle Prejean. Both in their fifties, both from Crystal's Sunday school class, both in nice dresses. Both swore on the Bible. And both told almost exactly the same story, almost in the same words, about one Sunday in March when they'd come out to the lake house with Crystal for lunch.

"Walter came in from the porch," Sherri said, "and he put his hand on Crystal's shoulder, and he said, Crystal, you're the daughter I never had. You're going to be taken care of. I'll never forget it."

"Which Sunday in March?" Maggie asked her, on cross.

"I believe it was the second Sunday."

"March the ninth?"

"That sounds right."

"Mrs. Landry, during that lunch on March ninth, did you ever hear Mrs. Upton say anything about Mr. Barr's health? Or his money?"

Sherri glanced at Crystal. Crystal was looking at her lap.

"No, ma'am. Nothing like that. She was very loving."

"Thank you. I'd like you to stay right there."

Maggie walked back to our table. She picked up the little silver Panasonic recorder with a fresh battery in it, and a cassette that said 14 on it, and set them both on the evidence table in front of the bench, very gently, like she was setting down an egg.

"Your Honor, Defendant's Exhibit 9. An audio recording made by the late Walter Barr on his own back porch, on his own recorder, on Sunday, March ninth. The same Sunday Mrs. Landry just described. It was disclosed to plaintiff's counsel nine days ago on our exhibit list."

Brett Ledoux stood up fast. "Your Honor, it's an old man telling war stories to his grandkids. We listened to the first few minutes. It's irrelevant."

"The first few minutes," Maggie said, "are about a lawnmower. It gets more relevant."

Judge Guidry looked at Brett Ledoux over her glasses for a long moment. "Counselor, did you listen to the whole tape?"

Ledoux opened his mouth. Closed it.

"I'll take that as a no. Play it, Ms. Doucet."

Maggie pushed the button.

My father's voice filled up that courtroom. "This is Grandpa Walt, tape fourteen." Somebody behind me in the VFW row made a sound, low in his throat. Daddy told his lawnmower story. People smiled. A few of them laughed when he got to the three parts left over.

Then the women's voices came, faint through the kitchen window.

"...I'm just saying, Sherri, the man's eighty-three with stage four. How long can it be? Summer?"

Sherri Landry, sitting in the witness chair, went the color of a boiled shrimp.

"He's deaf as a post. He's got the TV going. I don't come out here every Sunday for my health. Mitch gets half of everything when Walter goes. And Brett says if I can show I was here, taking care of him, I can go after Mitch's half. Brett says a judge will eat it up."

Brett Ledoux sat down very slowly in his chair.

"I sit with him an hour on Sundays and hold his hand where the neighbor lady can see. That's all it takes."

Crystal had both hands over her mouth.

"I can't wait for the old man to die so we can access the money. Dale's already looking at boats."

The laughter on the tape. Three women. Behind Crystal, in the front row, Dale Upton took his arm off the back of the bench.

Then the porch chair creaked, and my father's voice came back. Slow. Low.

"Mitchie. I heard that. Every word. I'm not as deaf as she thinks."

I didn't look at the judge. I didn't look at Crystal. I looked at the little silver recorder on the evidence table and listened to my father tell me he was proud of me, in front of thirty people, in a courtroom he never got to see.

"I'm leaving this on here, son. I'm putting it in the box. You'll know when."

Maggie pushed stop.

Nobody in that courtroom moved. One of the old Army men behind me blew his nose, loud, like a trumpet, and nobody even looked at him.

Judge Dorothy Guidry took off her reading glasses. She folded them and set them on the bench in front of her. Then she looked down at Crystal Upton, in her pale blue Sunday school dress, for what felt like a full minute.

"Mrs. Upton," she said, "I have been on this bench for thirty-one years. I have heard people lie to me about money, about children, about dogs, about who hit whose car. I have never once in my career heard a woman sue for payment for the emotional support she gave a dying man, and then heard that same woman, in her own voice, in his own kitchen, saying she couldn't wait for him to die."

Crystal lowered her hands. Her face was gray under the makeup.

"Your Honor, I was joking. We were just talking. Women talk."

"I heard you talk, ma'am. So did Mr. Barr. He's the one who wasn't laughing." The judge picked her glasses back up. "Plaintiff's claim is dismissed with prejudice. That means you can't bring it again, ever, in any court in this state. Plaintiff will pay all court costs and the defendant's reasonable attorney's fees, which I'll set after Ms. Doucet files her affidavit. On the estate's reconventional demand, judgment for the Estate of Walter Barr against Crystal Upton in the amount of thirty-one thousand four hundred dollars, plus legal interest. The groceries, Mrs. Upton."

"Your Honor..."

"And Mrs. Landry. Mrs. Prejean." The judge turned to the two women. Sherri was still in the witness chair. Gayle was in the second row, hunched over. "You both sat in that chair and swore on a Bible. I'm sending this transcript to the District Attorney. I'd suggest you each find a lawyer who isn't Mr. Ledoux."

"Court's adjourned," Judge Guidry said, and banged her gavel once, like she was killing a fly.

In the hallway afterward, Glenda hugged me so hard my back popped. Cody stood a little way off with his hands in his pockets and his eyes red, and when I went over, he just said, "Can I have the tape when it's done being evidence, Dad?" and I said yes.

Crystal came out of the courtroom behind Brett Ledoux. Dale wasn't with her. Dale had gone out the side door before the judge even finished, and I watched through the window at the end of the hall as his white Escalade pulled out of the parking garage onto Texas Street without her.

It was two weeks later, on a Thursday afternoon, that Maggie Doucet pulled into the lot at Barr Diesel in her old Buick and walked into my shop holding her cell phone up in the air like a winning lottery ticket.

"Mitch Barr," she said. "Look at this."

I rolled out from under a Mack. She held the screen down to my face.

Missed calls. Crystal Upton. And a number in red next to her name.

Fifty-seven.

"Fifty-seven," Maggie said. "Since the judgment. Fifty-seven times she's called my office, my cell, and my home. Twice at two in the morning. She wants to settle."

"Settle what? We won."

"The attorney's fees. Judge Guidry signed the order Monday. Forty-six thousand two hundred dollars, payable to me, plus the thirty-one four to the estate, plus court costs. Seventy-nine thousand and change." Maggie smiled. "Due in thirty days, or I start garnishing her paycheck from the furniture store and putting a lien on anything she's got with her name on it."

"Doesn't Dale..."

"Dale," Maggie said, "filed for divorce last Friday. Married seven months. Brett Ledoux told me himself. Off the record. Dale says he was misled about her finances. He's keeping the house. It was in his name. And he bought the boat in August. Twenty-two-foot bass boat. Also in his name."

From the next bay over, Terrell Gaines laughed so hard he dropped a socket wrench.

"So what does she want?" I asked.

"She wants to come talk to you. In person. She says she'll only talk to you."

I looked out the big open bay door. Across Greenwood Road, in the parking lot of the Dollar General, a silver Hyundai Elantra was sitting with the engine running. I knew it wasn't Crystal's car. Crystal drove a Lexus. The Lexus had been in Dale's name too.

"She's here, isn't she?" I said.

"She followed me from my office," Maggie said. "I didn't stop her. You don't have to talk to her, Mitch."

"I know." I wiped my hands on a rag. "Let her come."

Crystal walked across Greenwood Road in a pair of jeans and a sweatshirt from the furniture store where she'd gone back to work, and her hair pulled back in a plain ponytail. No Sunday school dress. No tissues. She came in through the bay door and stood on the concrete in the smell of diesel and hot oil, the place she used to say made her want to throw up.

"Mitch."

"Crystal."

"I need to talk to you." She looked at Maggie. "Alone."

"Maggie's my lawyer. And Terrell's my mechanic. They're staying."

Terrell picked his socket wrench up off the floor and didn't go anywhere.

Crystal took a breath.

"I can't pay it. Seventy-nine thousand dollars. I make eleven dollars an hour plus commission. Dale took everything. The house, the car, the boat, the joint account. He moved out the day after court. He changed the locks." She laughed, high and thin. "He said I embarrassed him. In front of the whole town. He said they're laughing at him at the Rotary."

"I'm sorry to hear that."

"Are you?"

"Not really," I said. "But I was raised to say it."

She flinched like I'd thrown something.

"You knew," she said. "At the funeral. When you said file it. You already had that tape."

"It was in my truck. I hadn't listened to it yet."

"Then why did you tell me to file?"

"Because Daddy taught me you don't argue with somebody who's already made up their mind. You hand them the wrench and step back. They'll find out what's broke on their own."

"Mitch, please. I'm begging you. Have her forgive the fees. Let the estate drop the thirty-one thousand. You don't need it. You got half a million dollars. You'll never miss it. I'm living in my sister's spare room in Bossier with a mattress on the floor." Her eyes filled up. "Twenty-six years. I was your wife for twenty-six years. Doesn't that count for anything?"

"It counted for twenty-six years," I said.

"Please. Just tell her to settle. Tell her half. I'll pay half. I'll pay it a hundred dollars a month for the rest of my life. Just don't let them garnish me. Please. Your daddy would have wanted you to be kind to me."

It was quiet in the shop. Just the big fans in the rafters turning and the tick of the Mack's engine cooling down beside me.

I thought about a shoebox behind a Stetson. I thought about a creaking porch chair and a coffee cup set down on a wooden rail, and a man who couldn't get out of bed anymore deciding to leave his son one last thing.

"Crystal," I said, "Daddy already told me what he wanted. He put it on tape fourteen."
