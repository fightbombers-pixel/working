"Don't worry, girls, I'll still sneak away with Vito before the honeymoon. One last time for old times' sake."

My fiancée wrote that a little before midnight on the night before our wedding, in a group chat with eight of her bridesmaids and friends, and thanks to one tired woman with a wrong thumb, nine people read it the second it landed. The ninth one was me.

I was standing at the ironing board in my basement in Scranton, pressing the shirt I was going to get married in. The iron was hissing. My phone buzzed on the dryer, and a banner slid down across the screen.

"Bethany Albano added you to Joyce's Girls."

Then the messages started stacking up underneath it, one after another, before anybody noticed who'd just joined.

"OMG Joyce you are TERRIBLE."

"Does Vito know you're getting married tomorrow lol."

"Vito knows everything," Joyce wrote. "Vito's coming to the church. Third pew. Arnold invited him himself, he thinks it's mature."

"And Arnold?"

"Arnold won't notice. Arnold's pension is the honeymoon. Vito's the vacation."

Four laughing faces. A heart. Somebody wrote "queen."

I had paid $31,000 for that wedding. The church, the hall at Genetti's, the band, the flowers, the open bar her sister insisted on. I'd also paid $8,600 for ten days in Aruba, booked in my name, leaving Sunday at six in the morning.

I read every message twice. Then I reached over and unplugged the iron.

I hung the shirt back on its hanger, zipped it into the garment bag with the tux, and hung the bag on the pipe over the washer. Then I picked up my phone and texted my best man, Augie Pellegrino, who is seventy years old and goes to bed at nine.

"Don't pick up the cake in the morning. I'll explain."

Fourteen hours later, Joyce was standing at the front of St. Peter's in an ivory dress, staring at the side door the groom was supposed to come through, and a hundred and forty people were staring at her.

What she didn't know was what was going to be waiting for her in the bride's room at five minutes to eleven, in a plain white envelope, with my ring inside it.

My name is Arnold Quinlan. I'm 61 years old, and I was a tool-and-die machinist in Dunmore for thirty-eight years, until a buyout and two bad knees retired me last spring. My first wife, Carol, died nine years ago. I met Joyce at a fish fry at St. Peter's three years after that. A machinist learns one thing before anything else: you measure twice, you cut once, and you don't argue with the part, you check it against the drawing. Hold on to that.

So I stood there in my basement and checked the part against the drawing.

The chat kept moving. Nobody had noticed yet. Somebody named Gail sent a GIF of a woman sneaking out of a window. Somebody else asked what Joyce was going to wear on the plane.

Then it stopped. For about forty seconds, nothing.

Then Bethany Albano typed, and I could almost hear her voice cracking through the screen.

"Wait. WAIT. I meant to add Arlene Quinn. Her cousin Arlene. Oh my God. Arnold? Arnold are you there??"

The little words under the messages said "Seen by Arnold Quinlan." Nine names in a row, and mine was the last.

"Arnold I'm so sorry," Bethany wrote. "Please call me."

Then Joyce. "Bethany REMOVE HIM."

Then Joyce again, in a separate message to me, private. "Babe it's a JOKE. Girls joke. You know that. Call me."

I didn't call. I didn't type. I just read.

Then I heard knocking upstairs on the front door, the kind that doesn't stop.

It was my daughter, Tess. She's 29, a nurse at Moses Taylor, and she was still in her scrubs from a double. She came in with her car keys in her fist and her phone in the other hand.

"Bethany called me," she said. "She's crying so hard I couldn't understand her for a full minute. Dad. Is it true? Did you see it?"

"I saw it."

"All of it?"

"From 'don't worry, girls' to 'queen.'"

She sat down on the stairs, right where she used to sit when she was little and wanted to hear me and Carol talk in the kitchen.

"What are you going to do?"

"Go to bed," I said. "I have to be up early."

"For what? Dad, you can't still go."

"I'm not going to the church," I said. "I'm going to the church office. Different door."

I went to the hall closet and came back with a plain white envelope, the kind I keep for paying the paperboy at Christmas. I sat at the kitchen table and printed the chat on the old inkjet in the corner, every page, with the times on it and the nine names at the bottom. Then I took the ring box out of my jacket pocket. Fourteen hundred dollars at a jeweler on Wyoming Avenue, white gold, plain, because Joyce said she didn't like "flashy on a man."

I put her ring in the envelope with the pages. Then I took a pen and wrote one line on the outside, in block letters, the way I used to label parts.

Tess read it over my shoulder and put her hand over her mouth.

"Tess," I said, "I need you to do one thing for me in the morning."

"Anything."

"You're on her side of the church. You're in the bride's room at ten forty-five for the photos."

"Yeah."

"At five minutes to eleven, you hand her this."

My phone lit up on the table. It was the eleventh call from Joyce since midnight.

Tess looked at it, then at me.

"And where are you going to be?"

"On the phone with you," I said. "On speaker."

At nine the next morning I was sitting in my truck in the parking lot of the Glider Diner with a coffee I'd let go cold. I had called the band at seven and told them they'd still get paid. I'd called Genetti's at eight, and the manager, a calm woman named Rosemary Tate, had said, "Mr. Quinlan, I've been doing weddings for twenty-six years. You'd be surprised how often I get this call. Don't worry about the food."

Augie got in the passenger side a little after nine, in his windbreaker.

"I picked up the cake anyway," he said.

"I told you not to."

"It was paid for. It's in my trunk. It's three tiers, Arnie. My wife's going to kill me."

"We'll bring it to the firehouse."

He looked at me for a long time. Augie has known me since I was nineteen and he was the foreman who taught me how to read a micrometer.

"You okay?"

"I'm fine."

"You're not fine. You're too calm. Carol used to say when you got calm, somebody was about to get a refund."

I almost laughed. "She did say that."

A few minutes before eleven, my phone rang. It was Tess.

"I'm in the bride's room," she said quietly. "She's here. Paulette's here. Bethany's here, but she won't look at anybody. Mom, I mean Joyce, she's been asking where you are for an hour. She keeps saying you're running late."

"Is Vito there?"

"Third pew," Tess said. "Gray suit. He's on his phone."

"Put me on speaker and put the phone in your pocket. Then give her the envelope."

There was rustling. Then I heard the room. Women's voices, a hair dryer, somebody laughing too loud. Then Tess said, clear as a bell, "Joyce? Dad asked me to give you this."

"Oh, finally," Joyce said. "Is this his vows? He's so old-fashioned."

The paper tore.

There's a sound a room makes when nine women stop talking at once. I'd only heard it once before, at Carol's funeral, when the priest said her name. This one was different. This one had a hair dryer still running in it.

"What is this?" Joyce said. Her voice had gone thin. "Tess, what is this?"

"It's what you wrote last night," Tess said.

"This is private. This was a private chat."

"Bethany added him by accident," Tess said. "He read it. All of it."

"It was a JOKE."

Then Paulette's voice, Joyce's older sister, who'd never liked me much but had never lied to me either. "Joyce. What does it say on the front?"

Joyce didn't answer. So Paulette read it.

"Go sneak away. Take all the time you need."

The hair dryer finally shut off.

"Where is he?" Joyce said. "Where is he? Tess, where is your father?"

"He's not coming."

"He has to come. There are a hundred and forty people out there."

"He paid for a hundred and forty people," Tess said. "He's not coming."

I heard heels on a wooden floor, fast. Then a door. Then the church, bigger, echoey, an organ playing something soft that stopped in the middle of a bar.

Joyce had walked straight out of the bride's room and up the side aisle in her ivory dress, and Tess followed her with the phone still in her scrub pocket. I heard Joyce say, loud enough to carry, "Everybody, everybody, I'm so sorry, Arnold isn't feeling well. He's sick. We're going to postpone."

A murmur. Then a man's voice I knew from the third pew.

"Joycie, what's going on?"

Vito Russo. He called her Joycie. I'd heard him do it at a Christmas party and thought it was ex-husband nostalgia.

And then Bethany Albano, who had been silent all morning, said something I'll never forget, in front of the whole church.

"He's not sick, Joyce. He read it. Tell them what you wrote."

"Bethany, shut up."

"Tell them about the honeymoon."

I heard footsteps on the phone, somebody coming close. Then Joyce's voice, right at the speaker, hot and fast.

"Tess, give me that phone. Is he on there? Is he listening? Arnold! Arnold, pick up, you coward!"

I looked at Augie. Augie looked at me.

I pressed the red button.

By noon, I had thirty-one missed calls.

Tess pulled into the diner lot at a quarter after twelve, still in her bridesmaid dress with her scrubs jacket over it. She got into the back seat of my truck behind Augie and just sat there for a second, breathing.

"Well?" Augie said.

"Well," Tess said. "Joyce told everybody you had a stomach bug. Then Bethany said, out loud, from the side aisle, 'He doesn't have a stomach bug, Joyce, he can read.' Then Paulette took the pages out of Joyce's hand and gave them to Joyce's mother, Rita. Rita read them standing up in the front pew with her glasses on the end of her nose."

"Oh, no," I said.

"Oh, yes. And then she looked around the church, and she said, very loud, 'Who's Vito?'"

Augie made a sound like a tire losing air.

"And everybody turned around," Tess said, "and looked at the third pew. And the third pew was empty. He'd gone out the side door during the stomach bug part. His seat was still warm, Dad. Mrs. DeMarco put her hand on it to check."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came up out of me like something I'd been holding since midnight, and once it started it didn't want to stop. Augie started laughing too, and then Tess, and for about a minute the three of us sat in my truck in the diner parking lot laughing like idiots while a waitress on her smoke break stared at us.

When it finally ran out, Tess wiped her eyes.

"Dad," she said. "She's going to come after you. You know that, right? She's already telling people you're not well."

"Let her tell them."

"You're really not going to say anything?"

"Not unless somebody asks me straight," I said. "Then I'll answer."

Augie and I drove the cake to the firehouse on Pittston Avenue, and the guys on B shift ate the top tier standing up and asked no questions, which is one of the best things about firemen. Then I went home.

Bethany Albano was sitting on my front steps.

She was still in her bridesmaid dress, a dusty pink thing with a bow on the shoulder, and she had a cardigan over it and mascara down to her chin. She stood up when my truck pulled in.

"I'm not here for her," she said, before I even got out. "I swear to God, Arnold. I'm not here for her."

"Okay."

"Can I come in? I don't want the neighbors to see me like this."

"The neighbors saw me carry a three-tier cake out of Augie's trunk an hour ago. They've had a big day."

She laughed, and then she cried, and then I let her in and made coffee, because that's what you do.

We sat at the kitchen table where I'd stuffed the envelope the night before. She held the mug with both hands.

"I've known Joyce since high school," she said. "Thirty-eight years. I've covered for her my whole life. I covered for her when she was married to Vito and seeing the guy from the gym. I covered for her when she was divorced and seeing Vito again. I covered for her last night." She shook her head. "I was so tired. I meant to add Arlene. Arlene Quinn, her cousin. You're right underneath her in my phone."

"Arnold Quinlan. Arlene Quinn."

"I'm so sorry."

"Don't be," I said. "You did me the biggest favor anybody's done me in nine years."

She looked at me. "You mean that?"

"I'd have married her this morning. I'd have flown to Aruba with her tomorrow. Then I'd have been the man paying for the vacation."

Bethany put the mug down.

"Arnold, there's something you need to know, and you're not going to like it."

"Go ahead."

"It wasn't a joke. And it wasn't going to be 'one last time.' It's been since March. Every Thursday. She tells you she's at book club."

"She's been in a book club since March."

"There's no book club. There's Vito's office above the restaurant. Thursdays, the restaurant's closed for lunch." She swallowed. "And it's worse than that. Vito's married."

"I know. Connie. She was invited."

"Connie didn't come. Vito told her he had a cold, then he came anyway and told everybody Connie had the cold. Connie doesn't know anything. Nobody's told her." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "And Arnold, Joyce said things. In the chat, before last night. About your pension. About your house. That she'd 'get her name on the deed by Christmas.' That once she was your wife, Vito could stop worrying about the restaurant's loan because she'd 'have access.'"

"Have access."

"Her words."

I sat there and let that settle. I wasn't angry, exactly. It felt like finding out a gauge you'd trusted for years had been reading off by a few thousandths the whole time. Every part you'd measured with it was suspect.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because this morning," Bethany said, "in the church, she looked right at me in front of everybody and told me it was my fault. That I ruined her wedding. Not her. Me. For pushing the wrong name." She stood up. "Thirty-eight years. I'm done covering."

At the front door she stopped.

"She's going to come here. Today. She's going to tell you it was girl talk and you're overreacting and you're too old to start over."

"I know."

"What are you going to say?"

Before I could answer, a car door slammed in the driveway. Through the window in the front door, I saw Joyce's white Camry parked behind my truck, and Joyce getting out of it, still in the ivory dress, the train bunched up in her fist.

Bethany went out the back.

Joyce didn't knock. She had a key.

She came in already talking, the train dragging across my hardwood, mascara perfect, because Joyce had fixed her makeup before she came. That told me more than anything she said.

"Do you have any idea what you did to me today?"

"I have some idea."

"A hundred and forty people, Arnold. My mother. My boss. Father Ray. I had to stand up there and lie to everybody."

"You didn't have to lie. Bethany offered you the truth."

"Bethany is a jealous, bitter, divorced—"

"Bethany's the one who added me."

"By accident!"

"The accident was the adding," I said. "The writing wasn't."

She threw her purse on the couch. "It was a joke. Women talk like that. It's girl talk. It's the night before the wedding, everybody's had wine, you say crazy things. You've never been in a bridal party, you don't know."

"Vito's the vacation."

"That's a joke!"

"Arnold's pension is the honeymoon."

"Arnold, stop."

"Get my name on the deed by Christmas."

She stopped. Her mouth opened and closed.

"Who told you that? Bethany? That wasn't last night. Bethany showed you old messages? Those are private."

"Nobody showed me anything," I said. "I asked her a question and she answered it."

"She's lying."

"Then where were you Thursdays?"

"Book club."

"What book?"

She actually hesitated. Three years, I'd never once seen Joyce hesitate.

"The one about the, the woman in Paris."

"What's her name?"

"Arnold, I don't remember the woman's name, it's a book."

"You've been reading it since March."

"Oh my God." She put both hands on her head. "You're interrogating me? On my wedding day? You stood me up at the altar, and you're interrogating me?"

"It's not your wedding day anymore, Joyce. It's a Saturday."

She sat down on the arm of the couch. Then she started to cry, and I'll be honest, a part of me wanted to go to her. Three years is three years. But I'd watched her fix her makeup in the rearview before she came in, and I stayed where I was.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. I made a mistake. One. Vito was a mistake, it's over, I was going to end it. That's what 'one last time' means. I was saying goodbye."

"Before the honeymoon."

"Yes."

"With my money."

"You're so cheap. That's what this is about. The money."

"No," I said. "It's about the third pew. I invited him. I shook his hand at the Christmas party and told him there were no hard feelings. I thought I was being the bigger man. You both laughed about that."

She didn't have an answer for that one.

"Here's what's going to happen," I said. "You moved your things in here in August. You gave up your apartment. That's on me, I asked you to. So you've got thirty days to find a place, and I'll help you move. The ring's yours to keep or sell. The Aruba trip is cancelled. The deposit at Genetti's is in my name."

"Thirty days? Where am I supposed to go in thirty days?"

"Vito's got an office upstairs."

She stood up so fast the train caught on the coffee table.

"You're going to regret this. You're a sixty-one-year-old widower with bad knees who irons his jeans. You think somebody else is going to want you?"

"I don't think about it at all."

"You'll be alone."

"I'll be alone in my own house," I said. "That's not the worst thing."

She grabbed her purse. At the door, she turned around one last time.

"Don't you dare tell anybody why. You hear me? You don't tell one person."

"I won't run around telling people, Joyce."

"Good."

"But if somebody asks me straight," I said, "I'm not going to lie for you. I'm too boring for that."

She slammed the door hard enough that a picture of Carol and me at Lake Wallenpaupack tipped over on the bookshelf. I went and stood it back up.

Then my phone rang, and it wasn't Joyce. It was a number I didn't know.

"Mr. Quinlan? This is Connie Russo. Vito's wife." Her voice was very steady, the way mine gets. "I got your number off the RSVP card you sent us. I've had six people call me today, and not one of them will tell me why you didn't show up at your own wedding. You're going to tell me."

Monday morning, I was at the credit union when it opened. Joyce's name was on nothing of mine, which I thanked God for, but she had the PIN to my debit card for wedding errands, so I had it cancelled and reissued. The locksmith came at ten and changed the front and back doors. I left a note on the kitchen table for Joyce that the new key was with Tess, for when she came to pack.

Then I met Connie Russo at the Glider Diner.

She was about fifty, dark hair pulled back, no makeup, a green raincoat she kept on in the booth. She did the books for the restaurant, she told me, and had since she married Vito eleven years ago. Her father had loaned Vito the money to open it. The building was in her name.

"I'm not going to make you say anything you don't want to," she said. "I'm just going to ask you one question, and I want a yes or no."

"Okay."

"Was it Vito?"

I'd told Joyce I wouldn't run around telling people, and I meant it. But this wasn't me running anywhere. This was a woman sitting across from me with her hands flat on the table, asking.

"Yes," I said.

She nodded slowly, like she'd known before she walked in.

"Thursdays," she said.

"Thursdays."

"He told me he was at the wine distributor's tasting in Wilkes-Barre. Every Thursday. Since spring." She laughed once, without any fun in it. "I bought him a new suit for those tastings. The gray one."

"He wore it Saturday. Third pew."

She closed her eyes for a second. Then she opened them, and I saw something in them I recognized from my own mirror that morning. It wasn't tears. It was arithmetic.

"Thank you for not lying to me, Mr. Quinlan."

"Arnold."

"Arnold." She slid out of the booth and put a ten on the table for her coffee, even though I told her not to. "You should know something. My name is on the building. My name is on the liquor license. My father's name is on the loan." She buttoned her raincoat. "Vito's name is on the menu."

She walked out. I watched through the window as she sat in her car in the lot for a long time without starting it.

That afternoon I called the travel agent and cancelled Aruba. I got back $6,200 of the $8,600. On the way home, I stopped at a used machinery dealer in Old Forge and stood for twenty minutes in front of a South Bend lathe, a 1960s model, in good shape, the kind I'd wanted in my garage since I was thirty. The tag said $3,800.

"You want it?" the owner asked.

"I've wanted it since Reagan," I said. "I just kept putting it off."

"Why now?"

"I've got some honeymoon money."

He gave me a look and didn't ask. He delivered it Thursday.

Thursday evening, I was in the garage leveling the lathe on its stand when Vito Russo's black Escalade pulled into my driveway and stopped about six inches from my bumper.

He got out without turning the engine off. He was still in the gray suit, or another one just like it, and he had sunglasses on even though the sun was going down behind the Hendersons' maple.

"You," he said. "You son of a witch."

I set down the level and wiped my hands on a shop rag.

"Evening, Vito."

"Don't 'evening' me. Connie threw me out. Tuesday. My clothes were on the sidewalk on Lackawanna Avenue in trash bags. In front of my restaurant. My customers walked past them."

"I'm sorry to hear it."

"You told her."

"She asked me."

"You could've said no!"

"I could have lied," I said. "That's a different thing."

He came up the driveway until he was standing at the edge of the garage, where the concrete changes color. He was ten years younger than me, heavier, and he was used to being the loudest man in any room he walked into.

"You know what you did? You know what you cost me? She changed the locks on my own restaurant. My restaurant. My name's on the sign."

"Your name's on the menu," I said. "That's what I heard."

His face went red from the collar up.

"Joycie told me you were a pushover. Three years, she said, 'Arnold doesn't notice anything, Arnold's a nice old man who irons his jeans.' I said fine, he's a good guy, why not. Nobody gets hurt."

"Nobody gets hurt."

"Nobody was supposed to get hurt!"

"You were going to sneak away with my wife before the honeymoon," I said. "In the suit your wife bought you for wine tastings that don't exist. Who exactly wasn't getting hurt, Vito?"

He opened his mouth and closed it.

"Here's what you're going to do," he said finally, and he pointed a finger at me. "You're going to call Connie. You're going to tell her it was a misunderstanding. Tell her you were confused. You're old, you misread a text, you got cold feet and blamed me. She'll believe you. She likes you, she said so."

"No."

"I'll pay you."

"No."

"Five thousand. Cash. Tonight."

"Vito," I said. "I just bought a lathe I wanted for thirty years with money from a honeymoon you were going to crash. I'm in a pretty good mood. Don't ruin it."

He stared at me. Then he looked past me at the lathe, sitting there gray and heavy and perfect on its stand.

"You're crazy," he said. "Both of you. You and Connie. Crazy and cold."

"Go home, Vito."

"I don't have a home!"

"Then go to Joyce's," I said. "Oh, wait. She lives here. For twenty-three more days."

He turned around and walked back to the Escalade, and he backed out so fast he clipped my garbage can and sent the lid rolling into the street. I went and got it. Mrs. Henderson was on her porch across the street with a glass of iced tea, watching like it was the evening news.

"Everything all right, Arnold?"

"Just a man who lost his way," I said. "He found the street."

She laughed so hard she spilled her tea.

Saturday morning, before I'd even had my coffee, Father Ray Costa knocked on my front door.

Father Ray is seventy-three and has been the pastor at St. Peter's since before Tess was born. He buried Carol. He was supposed to marry me and Joyce. He stood on my porch in a windbreaker over his collar, holding a paper bag from the bakery on Prospect Avenue.

"I brought crumb cake," he said. "I figured you've had enough cake this month that wasn't yours."

I let him in. We sat at the kitchen table. He looked at the lathe through the window over the sink and raised his eyebrows.

"That's new."

"Honeymoon money."

"Ah." He nodded like that was a perfectly normal thing to say to a priest. Then he folded his hands on the table. "Arnold, I'll be straight with you, because you've always been straight with me. Joyce came to the rectory on Wednesday. She sat in my office for an hour. She told me you'd been having what she called 'episodes.' Confusion. Forgetting things. She said the doctor was worried. She said you misread some messages from her friends and panicked, and that the kindest thing the parish could do was pray for you and not listen to gossip."

"She said I was senile."

"She didn't use that word."

"What word did she use?"

"'Fragile,'" Father Ray said. "And she asked me to tell the parish council you weren't well, so people would stop asking her questions at Mass."

I poured him a coffee and one for me. My hands were steady. That was the funny part. A month ago, a sentence like that would've had me pacing the kitchen.

"Did you?"

"No," he said. "Because on Thursday, Paulette came to see me. And on Friday, Bethany came to see me. Separately. Neither knew the other had come." He took a sip. "And they both told me the same thing, word for word, about what was written in that chat, and they both cried in the same chair."

"I'm sorry they put you in the middle."

"I've been in the middle of this parish for thirty-one years. It's where they keep the pews." He set down his cup. "I'm going to ask you one question, Arnold, and I want you to answer it like it's confession, even though it isn't."

"Okay."

"Did you see a doctor? Is there anything wrong with your memory?"

I got up, went to the drawer by the phone, and took out the folder with my retirement physical from the plant, dated in April. I set it on the table.

"I passed every test they've got, Father. Including the one where you draw the clock."

He didn't open it. He just put his hand flat on top of it.

"I didn't need to see it," he said. "I needed to see if you'd get angry. You didn't." He stood up and buttoned his windbreaker. "Most men your age, a woman stands them up in front of the whole town and calls them fragile, they come into my office ready to burn the place down. You bought a lathe."

"It's a good lathe."

"I'm sure it is." At the door he turned around. "For what it's worth, I'm going to tell the parish council one thing on Tuesday. That Arnold Quinlan is the sanest man in this parish, and anybody who wants to say otherwise can say it to me."

"You don't have to do that, Father."

"I know I don't." He smiled. "That's what makes it fun."

He walked down the steps. At the bottom, he looked back up at me.

"She'll come back around, you know. When the money runs short. They always do."

"I know."

"And?"

"And I'll be in the garage," I said.

Joyce's campaign started the next day, and I'll tell you how it went, because it went by the numbers.

Friday she called me forty-one times. I know because the phone counts them for you now, in a little red circle, and I'd glance at it when I came in from the garage. Forty-one. Then forty-seven. Then sixty-two, by the time I went to bed.

She'd been staying at her sister's since the wedding. She came by the house Saturday afternoon to pack, while Tess was there with the key, and she didn't pack. She sat at the kitchen table and cried and asked Tess to ask me to come in from the garage.

Tess came out instead.

"She wants to talk."

"Did she pack anything?"

"One box. It's her hair dryer."

"Tell her the door's open and so is the garage, and I'm in the garage."

Tess went back in. Joyce didn't come out.

Sunday, her sister Paulette stood on my porch. Paulette is sixty, a retired school secretary with a voice that could stop a hallway fight at fifty yards.

"I'm not here to defend her," Paulette said. "I want to be clear about that. I read the page out loud in that room. I know what she wrote."

"Okay."

"Our mother wants to know if there's any chance."

"Tell your mother I'm sorry for her trouble."

"That's a no."

"That's a no."

Paulette nodded like she'd expected exactly that. She went down two steps and turned around.

"You know what she said to me last night? She said she should've married you first and saved Vito for later. Like you were a starter car." Paulette shook her head. "I've been her sister for fifty-six years. I'm done driving her around too."

Monday, Joyce came with a lasagna. Her mother's recipe, the one I used to brag about at the firehouse. She rang the bell for twenty minutes. I was at the window upstairs, and I watched her stand there in the cold holding a foil pan, ringing and ringing, and when she finally gave up she set it down on the mat and walked away. I brought it in, because good lasagna is good lasagna, and I drove it to the firehouse.

Wednesday at two in the afternoon, my phone rang, and it was Joyce's voice, broken and gasping.

"Arnold. Arnold, I think I'm having a heart attack. My chest. I'm at Paulette's. Please. Please come."

I've lost a wife. I don't play around with that.

But I also didn't get in the truck. I hung up and called Paulette's landline, which I still had from three years of Easter dinners.

Paulette answered on the second ring.

"Is Joyce there?"

"She's on the back porch on her phone."

"Is she okay?"

"She's painting her nails," Paulette said. "Why?"

"She just told me she was having a heart attack."

There was a long pause.

"Arnold," Paulette said, "I'm going to go out there now, and I'm going to take that phone away from her like she's fifteen."

I didn't call Joyce back.

The counter on my phone said a hundred and nine missed calls by the end of the week. Then the calls stopped, and the texts started, one an hour, like a leaky faucet.

"Remember Cape May."

"Remember the fish fry where we met. You spilled the coleslaw."

"I gave up my apartment for you."

"The landlord on the new place wants a co-signer. My credit's not good since the divorce from Vito. It's just a signature, Arnold. You owe me that much."

That one I read twice. Then I put the phone face down on the workbench.

"I'm not a bad person. I made a mistake."

"Vito is nothing to me."

And then, the last one, on a Friday night, almost midnight.

"Bethany is telling everybody at work. Everyone knows. I can't even go to Mass. You did this."

I typed back to her once. It was the only time in two weeks.

"You wrote it, Joyce. I just read."

She didn't answer that one.

Her thirty days ran out on a Saturday morning. Tess and Augie and I loaded her boxes into a rented van, and her nephew drove it to an apartment over a pizza place in Dickson City she'd found through a friend of Paulette's. She didn't come. She sent the nephew with a list. I made sure everything on the list went into the van, and one thing that wasn't on it: the picture of her and me at Knoebels, the day she rode the Phoenix twice and screamed the whole way. I figured she might want it someday, and I knew I didn't.

That afternoon, I drove downtown to Genetti's.

Rosemary Tate, the event manager, met me in the main ballroom, the one where we were supposed to have our reception. It was set up for a different party that night, a sixtieth birthday, with blue and silver balloons tied to every chair, and a crew was laying tablecloths.

"Mr. Quinlan," she said, and she shook my hand like a woman who meant it. "I've got the paperwork ready. You said you wanted to transfer the deposit?"

"If that's allowed."

"It's your money. We hold it for one year for any event. What's the occasion?"

"My daughter got engaged last week," I said. "His name is Gabe, he's a paramedic, he asked me first. Over coffee. He said 'sir' four times."

Rosemary smiled. "Sounds like a keeper."

"He measured twice."

She laid a form on a cocktail table by the dance floor and handed me a pen. Two of the crew guys were hanging a banner that said Happy 60th Ray at the back of the room. One of them dropped a stepladder, and everybody laughed.

I'd filled in Tess's name and the date when I heard the heels.

Joyce came across the dance floor from the lobby door. She wasn't in an ivory dress this time. She was in jeans and a black coat, and she looked tired, more tired than I'd ever seen her. The crew stopped what they were doing. Rosemary took one look at her and took one step back, like a woman who'd seen this part before too.

"Paulette told me you'd be here," Joyce said.

"Paulette's honest."

"Everybody's honest now. Everybody in this whole town is so honest all of a sudden." She stopped across the cocktail table from me. "What are you signing?"

"The deposit. I'm moving it to Tess."

"That's our reception money."

"It was my money. Now it's Tess's engagement party."

She looked around the ballroom, at the balloons and the tablecloths and the banner, and at the two crew guys who were very carefully not looking at her.

"This was supposed to be our room," she said.

"I know."

"Arnold. Please." Her voice dropped, and it shook, and for once I don't think she'd practiced it. "Vito's gone. He went to his brother's in Jersey. Connie's selling the restaurant. Bethany won't talk to me. Paulette won't talk to me. My mother looks at me like I'm somebody else's daughter. I'm living over a pizza place. I made one mistake."

"You made a plan," I said. "A mistake is when you push the wrong name in your phone. Bethany made a mistake."

"I'll do anything. Counseling. Father Ray. Whatever you want. I'll never see him again. I'll never even say his name. Just give me one more chance. In front of all these people, I'm asking you. I'm begging you."

One of the crew guys put down his roll of tape very quietly.

"Joyce," I said. "Do you remember what you wrote? Exactly?"

She closed her eyes. "Don't."

"'Don't worry, girls. I'll still sneak away with Vito before the honeymoon.'"

"Arnold."

"There isn't going to be a honeymoon," I said. "So you don't have to sneak anywhere. You're free to go."

I looked down at the form, signed my name on the line at the bottom, Arnold L. Quinlan, in the same block letters I'd used on the envelope, and slid the pen and the paper across to Rosemary.

"Put it in my daughter's name," I said.
