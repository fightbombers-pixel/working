Raquel paused it on the frame where my hands were open.

"Your Honor," she said, "the 'family friend' who was 'checking on' Mrs. Solis at seven in the morning came out of the building barefoot, assaulted a licensed process server, and swung first at my client. My client used no more force than it took to stop him, and let him go. Mrs. Solis didn't see any of this from a car. She saw it from the window of the room she was sharing with him."

Marisol's lawyer stood up. "Objection. Speculation about the room."

"I'll withdraw that," Raquel said pleasantly. "I'll give the court a document instead." She handed a paper to the clerk. "This is Mr. Price's own formal complaint to his employer, the school district. In his words, he was, quote, 'in a hotel parking lot at approximately 7:20 a.m.' on Tuesday the fourteenth." She handed up a second paper. "And this is the district's attendance record, showing that at six forty-five that same morning, Mr. Price called in sick."

In the first row of the gallery, Lyndon Price stopped turning his watch.

"Next," Raquel said, "I'd like to call Consuelo Reyes."

Consuelo walked up to the witness stand like she was walking up to communion. She swore to tell the truth with her hand held up high, the way they do in the movies. She told the judge about the bazaar, and the message, and how she never erases messages. Then the bailiff played the certified copy of the recording, and Marisol's voice filled the courtroom.

"If Hector calls you in a couple of weeks, like on a weekend, I'm staying with you, okay? Just say I'm sleeping or I'm in the shower or something. Please, Mom."

Marisol put her face in her hands.

"Mrs. Reyes," the judge said, "when was this message left?"

"Two weeks before she went to that hotel, Your Honor. Two weeks before. She wasn't running from anybody. She was planning."

"Thank you, ma'am."

Raquel handed up the last of it. The five-hundred-dollar Visalia lawyer, twelve days before the banquet. The eighteen thousand dollars moved out of our daughter's college account two days before. The screenshot Sofia took, with Room 167 on the back of the bathroom door and the gold watch with the cracked face on the counter.

The judge looked at that screenshot for a long time. Then she looked up, over her half-glasses, at the first row of the gallery, at the gold watch on Lyndon Price's wrist. Everybody in the room watched her do it.

"Mrs. Solis," she said, "I've heard enough. The temporary restraining order is dissolved. The request for a permanent order is denied. Mr. Solis may return to his residence today. I'm making a finding that the petition was not credible, and I'm referring the question of your sworn statements to the family court judge who will be hearing your divorce. And Mrs. Solis, I'd strongly suggest you return the eighteen thousand dollars to the account it came from before that judge asks you about it."

Marisol didn't look up.

"We're adjourned," the judge said.

Out in the hallway, people stood around in little groups the way they do after court, everybody talking low. Nacho was shaking Raquel's hand. Consuelo had Sofia in a headlock that was supposed to be a hug.

Lyndon Price came out of the courtroom fast, buttoning his suit coat, heading for the elevators. He didn't look at me. He got about ten steps.

"Mr. Price."

Dolores Pratt, from human resources, was standing by the elevator with her leather portfolio under her arm. She didn't raise her voice. She didn't have to.

"Ms. Pratt," Lyndon said, and the radio voice came back, smooth as oil. "What a surprise. Look, this whole thing is a misunderstanding, a family situation, I was just trying to help..."

"You called in sick on the fourteenth at six forty-five," she said. "And at seven twenty you were in a hotel parking lot assaulting a process server. You put that in writing to my office yourself." She opened the portfolio and took out an envelope. "You're on administrative leave, effective immediately, pending an investigation into your attendance, your conduct, and your relationship with the parent of a student at your school. Your keys and your district badge, please."

"You can't do this in a hallway."

"I'd have done it at the school," Dolores Pratt said, "but you weren't there."

He stood there for a long moment. Then he unclipped the badge from his belt and laid it in her hand, and he pulled a ring of keys out of his pocket, and he laid those on top. His hand was shaking so badly the keys jingled.

The elevator dinged. The doors opened. Lyndon Price got in by himself and stood facing the back wall, and the doors closed.

Marisol came out of the courtroom just in time to see them close. She stood in the hallway in her gray cardigan with her lawyer next to her, and she looked at the elevator, and then at me, and then at our daughter.

"Sofia," she said. "Mija. Come here. Please."

Sofia didn't move. She stood next to me, and she reached up and touched the pocket of my shirt, right where I'd pinned her ribbon corsage that night at the banquet.

"You told Dad you were at Grandma's," she said. "And then you made me go to Grandma's. You know what, Mom? Grandma's the only one in this whole thing who wouldn't lie for you."

Marisol's mouth opened. Nothing came out.

Sofia turned and looked up at me.

"Dad," she said. "Can we go home now? Like, our home. The real one."

I put my arm around my daughter's shoulders.

"Sure," I said.
