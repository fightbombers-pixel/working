"Can't wait to see you tonight."

That's what the little gray banner said when it slid down across the photo of my fiancée and me on the Ferris wheel at the county fair. The iPad was propped on the gift table at my own engagement party, playing a slideshow for seventy-four guests in the back room of Casa Linda, and the message wasn't from me.

It was from a contact saved as "K." With a little cactus emoji next to it.

I was standing right there holding a plate of carne adovada and a glass of champagne that cost the restaurant about three dollars and cost me nine. I set the plate down. I didn't set the glass down.

Andrea had set that iPad up for me two years ago so the shop could play music, and she'd logged it into her own account because, as she put it, "you'd lose the password in a week, Stan." Hold on to that.

Across the room she was laughing at something her sister said, one hand on her hip, the ring I'd paid $14,000 for catching the light from the string bulbs. She hadn't looked at her phone in twenty minutes. She didn't need to. Her phone was in her purse, and her messages were on my iPad, and the iPad was on a table in front of seventy-four people who thought this was the happiest night of my life.

I tapped the banner. The whole thread opened. I scrolled up once, then twice, then I stopped scrolling, because I'd read enough to know the shape of it.

Then I picked up a fork, tapped it on my glass, and smiled.

Four hours later, Andrea Burris was standing in my driveway at two in the morning, reading her own handwriting on the side of a moving box.

What she didn't know, while I tapped that fork, was who my daughter had seated at table six.

My name is Stan Medrano. I'm fifty-four years old, and I own Medrano RV and Trailer Repair out on Cerrillos Road in Santa Fe, New Mexico. Thirty years of fixing campers teaches you one thing above everything else: the leak you can see is never where the water's getting in. You find where it's getting in, or you're just mopping. Remember that.

"Speech!" somebody yelled. It was Rudy Baca, my shop foreman, who'd had four margaritas and would have yelled speech at a funeral.

"Speech!" the room picked it up.

Andrea turned around, saw me with my glass up, and her face lit up like a kid on Christmas morning. She came across the room, slid under my arm and squeezed.

"You hate speeches," she said.

"I'll make an exception."

"Keep it short, mister. People want the sopapillas."

"Short," I said. "I promise."

The room got quiet. Seventy-four faces. Her sister Marcie by the bar. My daughter Nina at table six with a woman I didn't know. Two of Andrea's friends from the gallery on Canyon Road. Half my customers, a couple of guys from my Thursday poker game, and Rudy, swaying a little.

I raised the glass.

"Six years ago I buried my wife," I said. "Some of you were there. Some of you brought food for a month, and I never said thank you right, so thank you."

People nodded. A couple of the older ladies dabbed their eyes.

"And for a long time I figured that was it for me. I figured I was done with surprises." I looked down at Andrea. "Andrea surprised me. She's been surprising me for two years. And I have a feeling she's got a few more surprises left in her."

Laughter. Andrea pinched my side.

"So here's to Andrea," I said. "And to whatever she's waiting to see tonight."

"To Andrea!"

Glasses went up all over the room. Andrea went up on her toes and kissed my cheek, and her lips were warm and she smelled like the perfume I'd bought her for her birthday, and I drank the whole glass of champagne in one go, which is not something I do.

"That was sweet," she whispered. "Weird. But sweet."

"I'm a weird guy."

"You are." She patted my chest. "I've got to say hi to the Romeros before they leave."

She slipped away. I went to the bar and asked for a beer, a real one, and Rudy came over and leaned on me like a tree leaning on a fence post.

"Boss," he said. "That was the worst speech I ever heard."

"Thanks, Rudy."

"Whatever she's waiting to see tonight? What does that even mean?"

"Ask me tomorrow."

He squinted at me. Rudy has worked for me for nineteen years. He's seen me drop a transmission on my own foot and not say a word. He knows when the quiet is the wrong kind of quiet.

"Stan," he said. "What happened?"

"Not here."

"Okay." He straightened up, suddenly a lot less drunk. "Okay. Where?"

"My house. After."

He nodded and went back to the margaritas, because Rudy also knows that if he stops drinking all at once, people notice.

That's when Nina came up with the woman from table six.

My daughter is twenty-six and a paramedic with the county, and she has her mother's eyes and her mother's habit of grabbing your elbow when she wants you to pay attention. She grabbed my elbow.

"Dad, I want you to meet somebody. This is Lorraine. She's my charge nurse at St. Vincent's when we bring patients in. She keeps us all alive."

"Barely," Lorraine said. She was about fifty, short gray hair, reading glasses pushed up on her head, the kind of handshake that has lifted a lot of people off a lot of gurneys. "Congratulations, Mr. Medrano. Your daughter talks about you like you hung the moon."

"She exaggerates."

"She does not." Lorraine smiled. "I'm sorry my husband couldn't come. He's in Albuquerque on business till tomorrow. Kirk does commercial real estate. He's always in Albuquerque on business."

"Kirk," I said.

"Kirk Dunaway. Maybe you know him? He's done some deals up here."

My beer was cold in my hand. I could feel the label coming loose under my thumb.

"I don't think so," I said. "But I've got a feeling I'm going to."

Lorraine laughed politely, the way you laugh at a groom who's had a little too much champagne, and Nina gave me a look, and I gave Nina a look back, and my daughter has been reading my face since she was three years old.

She didn't say anything. She just tightened her hand on my elbow.

Andrea left at ten.

She came over with her coat already on and her keys already in her hand, and she kissed me quick on the mouth.

"Marcie's way too drunk to drive," she said. "I'm going to take her home and stay with her a little bit. You know how she gets."

"Don't wait up, okay? I'll probably just crash at her place."

"Okay."

"I'm fine. Go take care of your sister."

Andrea squeezed my hand. The ring pressed into my knuckle.

"Love you," she said.

"Drive safe."

I watched her go. Then I picked the iPad up off the gift table, closed the cover, and put it under my arm like a man carrying his Bible to church.

"Read it to us," Nina said.

"You don't want to hear it."

"Dad. Read it."

"He's sweet but he's so boring, K. He falls asleep at nine thirty watching the weather."

Rudy snorted. "You do fall asleep watching the weather."

"Thank you, Rudy."

"I'm not marrying him for fun. You know why I'm marrying him."

Nina put her coffee down hard enough to slop it on the table.

"I know you didn't, mija."

"I feel sick."

"Don't. You did me a favor. Now I know whose wife was sitting at table six."

Andrea: "Kirk thinks he's in charge of this. He's not. Once I've got the house I won't need Kirk either."

I read that one out loud twice. Rudy let out a long, low whistle.

"She's playing both of you," he said.

"Looks like it."

"Boss, that's cold. That's ice cold."

Then I did something I should have done four months ago. I opened the bookkeeping program on the laptop. Andrea had been doing my books three afternoons a week since spring. She was good with numbers. She'd told me a hundred times. She kept score of everything, who paid for dinner, who paid for gas, who owed who a birthday card. I'd always thought it was cute.

There were four payments out of the shop account I'd never looked at. Each one labeled "consulting, A. Burris." July, August, September, October.

"How much?" Rudy asked.

I added it up on the back of a napkin, because that's how I add things up.

"Eighteen thousand four hundred dollars."

Nobody said anything for a while. The refrigerator hummed. Outside, a coyote was yipping somewhere up toward the arroyo.

Nina said, "What are you going to do?"

"Rudy," I said. "Is the enclosed trailer at the shop empty?"

"The twelve-footer? Yeah."

"Go get it. Nina, there are boxes in the garage from when she moved in. She wrote on them herself. Use those."

Nina was already standing up.

"And Rudy," I said. "Change the garage code on your way back in. Make it your birthday. I'll never guess it."

"You don't know my birthday."

"Exactly."

We worked till almost two. I'm not going to tell you it felt good, because it didn't. It felt like cleaning out a dead man's closet, and the dead man was me, or at least the version of me who'd tapped a fork on a glass and believed a woman when she said she loved him.

Nina folded the sweaters. Rudy carried the heavy stuff. I took down the photo of the two of us at White Sands and put it face down on the counter and then picked it back up and put it in a box, because it was hers too.

At ten to two, headlights came up the driveway and swung across the kitchen window.

Rudy looked at me. Nina looked at me.

"She said she'd crash at Marcie's," Nina said.

"Yeah," I said. "She says a lot of things."

She'd stopped at the garage. She was standing in front of the stack of boxes Rudy had lined up on the apron under the motion light.

"Stan, what is this?"

"Your winter clothes."

"I can read. Why are they in the driveway?"

"Because you don't live here anymore."

"It's not a joke."

"How's Marcie?"

"Who?"

"Kirk. K. With the little cactus."

"They read themselves. I just stood there."

"With my sister!"

"Your sister's on the couch in my living room right now," I said.

That was a lie. I'm not proud of it. It was the only lie I told the whole time, and it lasted about four seconds, because her face went from red to the color of skim milk and then she said, "No, she's not," way too fast, and that was all I needed.

"No," I said. "She's not. But you just told me you know where she is, and it isn't here, and it isn't with you."

"You son of a witch."

"There it is."

"Then you should've asked."

"I bet you did."

"I didn't put her in it. You did."

"No."

"It's your key that doesn't work anymore. I can't give you that back."

The banker was a young guy named Tyler with a tie that was trying too hard. He typed for a while. Then he stopped typing and turned the screen toward me a little, the way they do when they don't want to say it out loud.

Kirk backed out of the bay toward his truck. He didn't pick up the tire iron. He didn't pick up the sunglasses. He got in the Silverado, sat there for a second with both hands on the wheel, and then he picked up his phone and stabbed at it with his thumb, and I could see his mouth moving before he even pulled out of the lot.

He wasn't calling a lawyer. He was calling her.

Eleven minutes later, my own phone rang. It was Andrea. I didn't pick up. She called back. I didn't pick up. On the third call I put it on speaker and set it on the workbench so Rudy could hear.

"What did you tell him?" she was screaming. "What did you tell him, Stan? He says I'm a liar! He says I set him up! What did you show him?"

"Your own words."

"You had no right!"

"Maybe not. But you put them on my iPad."

"He's saying he's going to the police! He's saying he's going to tell them it was all my idea!"

"Was it?"

The line went dead.

Friday morning there was a text from her. Not a call. Just a text, very polite, like a woman writing to a dentist.

"I think we should meet and end this like adults. I'll bring the ring. You drop the police report and the lawsuit, and I'll take the Facebook post down. Casa Linda, tomorrow, 1 p.m. Just us."

I showed it to Joan Sedillo. She read it and handed it back.

"She's offering you your own ring in exchange for a felony," Joan said. "Don't agree to anything. But go. Bring a witness. Let her talk."

I called Detective Tafoya and told her where I'd be. She said she'd been planning to call me anyway. She'd been through the records from Lorraine, and from Rio Bravo, and from the pawn database the city keeps on every ring and gun and guitar that crosses a counter in Santa Fe.

"What's the pawn database got to do with anything?" I asked.

"You told me you had something engraved inside that ring."

"S and A, and the date I asked her. Ten twelve."

"Be there at one," she said. "I'll be around."

And I called Lorraine, because I'd promised her she'd see his face, and because I figured Kirk would be at Casa Linda anyway, whether anybody invited him or not.

"Tell him where she'll be," I said. "Tell him the truth. That she's meeting me at one to make a deal. Don't tell him anything else."

"Oh, Stan," Lorraine said. "That's all I'll have to tell him."

Casa Linda at one on a Saturday is full of tourists in new cowboy hats and state workers on their lunch hour, and the back room, where my party had been exactly one week earlier, was set up for a baby shower that wasn't going to start till three. The manager, Gilbert, let me use it because I'd fixed his brother's Airstream twice and never charged him for the second time.

Rudy sat at a table near the door with a bowl of posole, pretending he'd never met me.

Andrea came in at five after one. She'd had her hair done. She was wearing the turquoise cuff from her gallery and a white blouse, and she looked exactly like a woman who wanted the whole world to think she'd been wronged. She sat down across from me and put a small black velvet box on the table between us.

"Hi, Stan."

"Andrea."

"I want to say I'm sorry about how things went."

"Okay."

"I don't think either of us wanted it to end like this."

"One of us didn't."

She pressed her lips together. "Here's your ring. I'm giving it back. I don't want anything from you. I just want my life back, and I want you to stop this. The police, the lawyer, all of it. People are talking. Patty Gonzales asked me if you hit me, and I didn't know what to tell her."

"You could tell her the truth."

"The truth is complicated."

"The truth is a bank wire," I said. "It's about the least complicated thing in the world."

"I'll pay it back."

"With what?"

"I'll figure it out!"

"Kirk took it. You know that. Rio Bravo Land Partners. You sent it to a man who owes sixty thousand on forty acres in Edgewood that can't get water."

She flinched. "He was going to pay me back. He promised. The subdivision was going to..."

"Was," I said.

I opened the velvet box.

There was a ring in it. It was the right size, the right setting, a round stone in four prongs on a white gold band. In the low light of that back room, it looked fine. I picked it up and tilted it toward the window and looked inside the band.

Nothing. No S. No A. No ten twelve.

I set it down on the tablecloth very gently.

"Where's my ring, Andrea?"

"That's your ring."

"My ring has writing in it."

Her eyes went to the box, and then to the door, and then back to me, and I saw her figure it out in real time. She hadn't known either.

"Kirk took it to get it cleaned," she said, very slowly. "On Sunday. He said he had a guy. He brought it back Tuesday."

"He had a guy," I said.

That's when the door of the back room banged open hard enough to rattle the tissue-paper bells somebody had already hung for the baby shower.

Kirk came in with his wrist in a drugstore brace and no sunglasses. Lorraine came in right behind him, still in her scrubs, and stopped just inside the door with her arms folded, and stood there like she was watching a ball game she'd paid good money for.

"You were going to cut me out," Kirk said to Andrea. He didn't even look at me. "You were going to get the house and cut me out."

"Kirk, what are you doing here?"

"I read it. 'I won't need Kirk either.' I read it!"

"You took his money!" Andrea stood up so fast her chair went over. "You took all of it! You told me it was a loan!"

"You sent it! I never touched his account! You did all of it, I just held it!"

"Held it?" Andrea's voice cracked. "You spent it! On your stupid dirt in Edgewood!"

"It was going to be worth a fortune!"

"And the ring? Where's the ring, Kirk? You had a guy?"

Kirk opened his mouth. He looked at the velvet box on the table. He looked at me. He looked at his wife by the door, and his wife smiled at him, a small pleasant smile, the kind a nurse gives you right before the shot.

"It was a loan," he said. "I was going to get it back."

"From where?" I said.

Nobody answered. And then a voice did, from behind Lorraine, where a woman with a ponytail and a manila folder had walked in without anybody noticing.

"Cash Corner on Cerrillos," Detective Tafoya said. "Pawn ticket 41187. One white gold engagement ring, round diamond, inscription inside the band, S and A, ten twelve. Pawned Monday morning for sixty-two hundred dollars." She looked up from the folder. "Pawned by Kirk Dunaway, with his own New Mexico driver's license. You'd be surprised how many people do that."

Kirk sat down in the chair Andrea had knocked over, except it was on its side, so he sort of half sat on the edge of it and slid off onto the floor, and nobody helped him up.

Andrea looked at him on the floor. Then she looked at Lorraine.

"You told him I'd be here."

"I told him the truth," Lorraine said. "It's going around."

Tafoya stepped forward. "Mr. Dunaway, I'm going to need you to stand up and put your hands behind your back. Ms. Burris, you can sit, but don't leave. We've got a lot to talk about, and some of it's about a wire on a Friday afternoon."

Out past the open door, the front dining room had gone quiet. Patty Gonzales was standing up at a table by the window with her napkin still in her hand, staring through the doorway at Kirk on the floor and the detective with the folder, and at me sitting there with my hands flat on the tablecloth.

"I want a lawyer," Andrea said.

"You'll get one."

"Stan." She turned to me. Her mascara was starting to go. "Stan, please. Please tell her it was a misunderstanding. I'll pay you back, I swear to God, every dime. Just tell her. You were going to marry me."

I picked up the velvet box with the fake ring in it, closed it and slid it across the tablecloth to the detective.

"Evidence," I said. "And Andrea? You can keep the box."
