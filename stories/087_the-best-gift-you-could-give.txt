"The best gift you could give us is to disappear."

My wife Kristen said that at the final tasting for my stepson's wedding, at a long farm table in the Magnolia Barn in Madison, Alabama, in front of the wedding planner, the bride, the bride's parents, my stepson, and the man sitting at her right hand, his real father, Rob Bentley. Eight people heard it. The wedding planner put her fork down.

I had a bite of smoked brisket halfway to my mouth. I'd picked that brisket. I'd also paid for it. The whole wedding was going to cost $63,000, and so far I'd put down $31,500 of it in deposits, every dollar out of my own checking account.

"Kristen," Colton said. "Mom. Come on."

"No, honey, somebody has to say it." She smiled at me the way she smiled at customers at her salon who wanted bangs and shouldn't have them. "Bruce, you've been wonderful. Really. But Rob is Colton's real dad, and Rob is back now, and it would mean the world to this family if Rob could walk in that room as the father. Without any confusion. Without you standing there in the pictures. Just for one day."

"Just for one day," I said.

"And honestly? After that, too. I think we both know it's been over a long time."

Rob looked at his plate. He had the decency to do that, at least.

I set my fork down. I wiped my mouth with the cloth napkin. Then I reached across the table, past the bride's mother, past the little cards with the wine pairings written in gold ink, and picked up the white three-ring binder the wedding planner had been flipping through all afternoon. It had every vendor contract for the wedding in it. Eleven of them.

I tucked it under my arm and stood up.

"Then consider it my wedding gift," I said.

Nine days later, the wedding planner would be standing on Kristen's front porch with a clipboard, reading her a list of eleven cancellations out loud, one at a time, while Rob Bentley sat in his truck in the driveway with the engine running.

What Kristen didn't know was whose signature was on the bottom line of every one of those eleven contracts.

My name is Bruce Fleming. I'm 55, and I've been an insurance claims adjuster in Huntsville for 29 years. In my job, you learn one thing fast. People always tell you the truth about the damage the minute they find out who's paying for the repair.

Hold on to that.

I walked out of the barn into the parking lot and got into my truck and sat there with the binder on the passenger seat. Through the big glass doors I could see them all still at the table. Kristen was talking with her hands. Rob was nodding. Colton had his head down.

The bride's father, Gil Hendry, was the only one looking out the window at me. He was a big, quiet man who owned a sod farm out past Hazel Green, and in the whole year of planning I don't think he'd said fifty words to me.

He was looking at me like a man trying to figure out a math problem.

My phone buzzed. A text from Kristen's sister, Faye.

"Call me. Not from the house. Not where she can hear."

Faye Larkin met me at the Waffle House on University Drive at nine that night. She was 49, three years younger than Kristen, and she cut hair at Kristen's salon, which meant she'd spent eleven years listening to her sister's business through a wall of hair dryers.

She slid into the booth across from me and ordered coffee and nothing else.

"She said it," Faye said. "Didn't she. Out loud. In front of the Hendrys."

"You knew?"

"Bruce, I've known since April." She wrapped both hands around the coffee cup. "I'm so sorry. I should've called you in April. I kept thinking she'd come to her senses."

"Since April."

"You remember when Colton and Alyssa got engaged? And Kristen put the whole thing on Facebook? The ring, the barn, the band, the whole budget practically? She couldn't help herself. She wrote 'Sixty-thousand-dollar fairy tale for my baby boy.'"

"I remember. I asked her to take it down."

"Rob saw it. Up in Nashville. Three days later he sent Colton a friend request. A week after that he was calling Kristen." She looked at me. "He hadn't called that boy in twenty-two years, Bruce. Not one birthday. Then he sees a number with a comma in it and suddenly he's a daddy."

"What does he do now? Up in Nashville."

"He told Kristen he owns a roofing company."

"Does he?"

Faye laughed, but it wasn't a happy laugh.

"He works for one. When he works. Kristen doesn't want to hear it. You know what he owes her? In back child support? From when Colton was little?"

"She always said he paid some."

"He paid four hundred dollars. Once. In 2004. The judge said he owed forty-one thousand by the time she stopped chasing him." Faye shook her head. "And now she's got him sitting at the head of the table you paid for."

The waitress came by and topped off my coffee without asking. I watched it swirl.

The truth is, the barn wasn't the first time. It was just the first time with witnesses.

There was the Christmas card in 2019, the one with the family picture where Kristen had cropped me out because "the composition was better." There was Colton's college graduation, where she'd introduced me to his professor as "my husband," not "his stepdad," not "his dad," and when I mentioned it on the drive home she'd said I was being sensitive. There was the drawer in my dresser where I kept every Father's Day card Colton ever made me, from the macaroni one at six to the one he signed "Pops" at seventeen, and how she'd once called that drawer "a little sad."

And there was the engagement dinner in March, when Colton asked me, just me, to help him pick the venue, and Kristen said, "Well, he does have the checkbook."

I'd laughed at that one. Everybody had.

"Faye," I said. "Why are you telling me this?"

She set her cup down.

"Because I sat at that table tonight too," she said. "And I watched my nephew look at you walking out that door, and I've never seen that boy look so lost. And because my sister doesn't get to do this to the only man who ever showed up."

She put a five-dollar bill on the table for her coffee and stood up.

"She thinks you'll go home and sulk," Faye said. "She thinks you'll keep paying because you always pay."

"I always have."

"I know," she said. "That's what she's counting on."

I got home a little before eleven. Kristen's car was in the driveway. All the lights were off except the one over the stove.

My suitcase was sitting in the front hall. Packed. Zipped. My good shoes on top of it in a grocery bag.

I didn't touch it. I went to bed in the guest room, and I set my alarm for six.

At seven the next morning I was the first customer through the door at Redstone Federal Credit Union. I sat down with a young man named Andre who couldn't have been older than Colton and moved every dollar of my paycheck deposit into a new account in my name only. Kristen had her own accounts for the salon. She'd always kept them separate. She'd always said that was just good business.

At eight I sat in my truck in the credit union parking lot with the white binder open on my lap and started calling.

The venue first. The Magnolia Barn. $18,000 for the night, $9,000 already paid. Contract signed by Bruce A. Fleming.

The caterer. $14,200, deposit $5,000. Signed by me.

The band. A seven-piece outfit out of Birmingham that played Earth, Wind and Fire like they'd written it. $6,500, half down. My name.

The florist. The photographer. The videographer. The cake. The bus to take guests from the hotel to the barn. The rehearsal dinner at a steakhouse downtown. The block of hotel rooms. And the honeymoon. Eight nights in Saint Lucia, booked as a gift, in my name, on my card.

Eleven contracts. Eleven times I said the same thing.

"This is Bruce Fleming. I'm the one who signed. I need to cancel."

Some of them gave back money. Some of them didn't. The Magnolia Barn kept half the deposit, which was fair, because it was six weeks out. The band gave back everything and the bandleader said, "Man, I'm sorry, that's rough," and I said, "It's all right. You'd have been great."

The last call I made was to the wedding planner. Her name was Tess Aguirre. She'd been in the barn the night before. She'd put down her fork.

"Mr. Fleming," she said. "I wondered if I'd hear from you."

"I'm sorry to do this to you, Tess."

"Don't be. I've been doing this nineteen years." She paused. "Can I ask you something? You don't have to answer."

"Go ahead."

"Does she know? That it's all in your name?"

"She knows I paid."

"That's not the same thing," Tess said. "Is it."

"No," I said. "It's not."

My phone had been buzzing the whole time I was on the calls. When I hung up with Tess, I finally looked at the screen.

Kristen. Fourteen missed calls. Nine texts.

The last one said: "WHERE ARE YOU. The barn just called ME asking if the cancellation was 'confirmed.' What did you do?"

She was waiting for me in the kitchen when I got home from work that evening, standing at the island with her arms folded and her reading glasses pushed up in her hair. She'd been crying, or she wanted me to think she had.

"The barn," she said. "The caterer. The band. Tess called me at the salon in front of a client, Bruce. A client. Do you know what that was like?"

"I can guess."

"You canceled my son's wedding."

"I canceled my contracts."

"It's the same thing!"

"It's not, Kris. The wedding's still yours. Colton and Alyssa can get married any day they want. I just won't be paying for it."

"Six weeks before! You did that six weeks before!"

"You told me to disappear six weeks before."

"That's not what I meant and you know it."

"You said, 'The best gift you could give us is to disappear.' I was sitting right there. So were the Hendrys."

"I meant from the pictures. From the aisle."

"And after that, too. You said that part as well. 'It's been over a long time.'"

She opened her mouth and shut it again.

"So I'm disappearing," I said. "The money's part of me. It goes where I go."

"That is so petty. That is so cruel."

"I did exactly what you asked."

"You know what? Fine." She slapped the counter. "Fine. Then let's be grown-ups about it. You want to be gone? Be gone all the way. Sell the house. I want my half, and I want it now."

I looked around that kitchen. I bought this house in 2001, two years before I married her, when it was a sad little brick ranch with orange carpet. I put in the cabinets. I tiled the floor on my knees over three weekends in 2006 while Colton handed me spacers and asked a hundred questions about grout.

I'd already talked to a lawyer that afternoon, on my lunch break. A friend from church who does divorces. He said that after twenty-two years of her living in it and both of us paying on it, a judge in Alabama would almost surely give her a share no matter whose name was on the deed. He said the cleanest thing was to sell it, split what was left, and be done.

"Okay," I said.

She blinked. "Okay?"

"Okay. I'll call a realtor tomorrow. We'll split it down the middle."

"Just like that."

"Just like that."

She stood there a second like a woman who'd kicked a door and found it unlocked. Then she picked her phone up off the counter and went out to the back deck and slid the door shut. Through the glass I could see her dial and put it to her ear and start smiling.

I knew who she was calling. Faye had told me his number was saved in her phone as "C's Dad."

I went to the guest room and opened my dresser drawer, the one with the cards in it. Twenty-one Father's Day cards. I didn't read them. I just counted them, and then I shut the drawer and called a realtor.

Five days later, Colton came to my office.

I work out of a little branch office off Memorial Parkway, two desks and a coffee maker, and most of my day is out in the field looking at hail damage and flooded basements. When I came in from a roof in Meridianville a little after four, he was sitting in the plastic chair by my desk in his work clothes from the power company, still wearing his climbing belt.

He was 27, six foot one, and he still sat in a chair like he was nine and in trouble.

"Hey, Pops," he said. Then he winced, like the word had come out without permission. "Hey, Bruce."

"Hey, son."

"Mom sent me."

"I figured."

"She says you canceled the wedding out of spite. She says you're punishing me because you're mad at her." He looked at his boots. "Are you?"

"No."

"Then why'd you do it? Alyssa's been crying for five days. Her mom's calling everybody. We've got two hundred and ten people with save-the-dates on their fridges."

I sat down in my desk chair. I took off my hat and set it on the desk.

"Colton. Did your mother tell you what she said at the barn?"

"She said you got upset because Rob was there."

"Was that what you heard? You were sitting right across from her."

He was quiet a long time.

"I heard her say it," he said finally. "The disappear thing. I just figured she didn't mean it like that."

"How else could she mean it?"

"I don't know." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Rob says you're doing this to make him look bad. He says any real man would just let a father walk his own son down the aisle and step aside. He says you're too proud."

"Rob says a lot of things."

"So what do I tell Mom?"

"Tell her I said yes. She asked me to disappear and I said yes. That's all I did." I leaned forward. "And son? If you want to know anything else, don't ask your mother and don't ask Rob. Ask Mr. Hendry. He was sitting right there too, and he doesn't have a horse in this race."

Colton stood up. He looked at me like he wanted to say something more, and then he didn't. He went to the door.

"There's a card in my drawer at home," I said. "From when you were seventeen. You signed it 'Pops.'"

He stopped with his hand on the doorknob.

"I know," he said. "I remember."

And he left.

On the ninth day, a Monday I took off work, I went to the house to load my workbench and my tools into a borrowed trailer. I'd taken a month-to-month apartment off Bailey Cove Road, two rooms and a balcony that looked at a dumpster, and the workbench was the one thing I wasn't leaving.

There was a pickup in the driveway I didn't know. A red Ram with Tennessee plates and a ladder rack with no ladder on it. Rob was sitting behind the wheel with the engine running and the air conditioning on, scrolling his phone.

I backed the trailer up to the garage and started carrying out tools. Rob didn't get out.

Around ten, a white SUV pulled up to the curb, and Tess Aguirre got out with a clipboard and a canvas tote bag. She walked up the front path and knocked. I stopped with a circular saw in my hands.

Kristen opened the door in her robe.

"Tess! Oh, thank God. I've been calling you all week."

"I know, Mrs. Fleming. That's why I came in person." Tess held the clipboard up a little, like a crossing guard's sign. "I need to go over the final vendor status with you, because I understand you've been telling the guests the wedding is still on."

"It is still on. This is a misunderstanding. Bruce had a tantrum and we're getting it sorted."

"Ma'am, I'm going to read you the list. All right?"

"I don't need a list."

"Venue, Magnolia Barn. Canceled by the contract holder on September 7th. Catering. Canceled by the contract holder. Band. Canceled. Florist. Canceled. Photographer. Canceled. Videographer. Canceled. Cake. Canceled. Guest shuttle. Canceled. Rehearsal dinner. Canceled. Hotel block. Released. Honeymoon. Canceled."

She read every one of them in the same calm voice, like a flight attendant doing the safety demonstration.

"Eleven," she said. "And the contract holder on all eleven is Bruce Fleming."

"Then put it in my name! Rebook it! I'll sign!"

"The barn's been rebooked for October 18th by another couple, ma'am. Two days after Mr. Fleming released it." Tess lowered the clipboard. "If you want a different venue, I'll need a new contract and a new deposit. For a Saturday in October on six weeks' notice, you're looking at fifteen to twenty thousand just to hold a room."

Kristen looked past her, out at the driveway. At Rob in his running truck.

"Rob," she called. "Rob! Come here. Tell her."

Rob rolled the window down about halfway.

"Tell her what, babe?"

"Tell her you'll cover the deposit. Just the deposit. Like you said. For our son."

Rob looked at Tess. He looked at me, standing by the trailer with a circular saw. He looked back at Kristen.

"Let me make some calls," he said. And rolled the window back up.

Tess turned around and looked at me across the yard. She didn't say anything. She didn't have to. She just nodded once, the way you'd nod at a man you'd watched tile a floor.

Kristen slammed the front door hard enough to rattle the storm glass.

I put the saw in the trailer. Rob's truck sat there running a while longer. Then, without anybody coming back out of the house, it backed out of the driveway and drove away.

I'd been in claims long enough to know what that looked like. That's what a man looks like when he finds out who's paying for the repair.

That Wednesday, Gil Hendry called me at work.

"Bruce. Gil Hendry. Are you free for lunch?"

"Mr. Hendry. I didn't think you'd want to talk to me."

"I'm buying," he said. "Dreamland, on Bob Wallace. Noon."

He was already in a booth when I got there, with a full rack of ribs in front of him and his napkin tucked into the collar of his shirt. He waved me in.

"I ordered for you. Hope you like ribs."

"Everybody likes ribs."

"Not everybody. My wife's sister's a vegetarian. We don't invite her to things." He pushed the plate of white bread toward me. "Bruce, I'm going to say this once, and then we'll eat. I've been married thirty-four years. I've run a business for thirty. I've sat at a lot of tables. I have never seen a woman say a thing like that to her husband in front of company."

"No, sir."

"And I've never seen a man answer it like you did." He picked up a rib. "My wife thinks you overreacted. My daughter thinks you hate Colton. You know what I think?"

"What's that?"

"I think you were the only grown-up at that table."

I didn't say anything. I tore a piece of white bread in half.

"I watched that Rob fellow all night," Gil said. "You know what he asked me, while you were getting your coat? He leaned over, real friendly, and asked me if the open bar was 'still on Bruce.' Those were his words."

"I'm not surprised."

"And then Monday, he called me. At home. I don't know where he got my number. Probably from Kristen. He wanted to know if the bride's family 'traditionally' covered the wedding, being as how there'd been 'a change in the money situation.'"

Gil put the rib down.

"I told him our family was covering the dress, the flowers in the church, and our share of the rehearsal dinner, like we'd agreed with you a year ago. And that if he wanted to be the father of the groom, he could start acting like one with his own wallet."

"What'd he say?"

"He said he'd been 'temporarily inconvenienced.'" Gil shook his head. "I've got a fellow at my sod farm who's been 'temporarily inconvenienced' since 1996."

We ate for a while. The ribs were good. Then Gil wiped his hands, took the napkin out of his collar, and leaned in.

"Bruce. My daughter's marrying that boy whether or not there's a barn. She loves him. And he's a good boy. You made him a good boy. I want you to know I see that." He paused. "But there's something else I came here to tell you, and you're not going to like it."

"Go ahead."

"Alyssa told me last night. Kristen asked Colton to get you to co-sign a loan. Twelve thousand dollars, at her credit union, for a new venue deposit. 'For the wedding.'" He looked at me hard. "Colton told her no. Said he wouldn't ask you. So now she's going to ask you herself."

She didn't ask me herself. Not right away. First she tried everything else.

By the end of the second week, the counter on my phone said 38 missed calls from Kristen and 61 unread texts. I read the first line of each one as it came in, the way you'd glance at the gauges on a long drive.

"We need to talk like adults."

"Colton is devastated and it's on you."

"Remember Gulf Shores? 2009? You promised me forever on that balcony."

"I'm not asking for much."

Then, on a Thursday a little after nine at night, a text that was just one line.

"Chest pains. On my way to Crestwood ER. Please come."

I sat on the edge of my bed in the apartment with my shoes already in my hand. Twenty-two years doesn't switch off because someone said something ugly at a barn. Then I put the shoes down and called Faye.

"Is your sister in the emergency room?"

There was a pause. I heard a TV in the background.

"Bruce, she's sitting on my couch," Faye said quietly. "She's eating my ice cream. She's got her phone in her hand right now."

"Okay."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. Tell her I hope she feels better."

I heard Faye cover the phone and say something. I heard Kristen say, very clearly, "He called YOU?" Then the line went dead.

Thirty-eight missed calls became thirty-nine.

The next morning Rob Bentley walked into my office.

I don't know how long he'd been sitting in the parking lot waiting for me to come back from an inspection, but he came through the door about ten seconds after I did, like he'd timed it. He was wearing a golf shirt with a roofing company logo on the chest and a pair of sunglasses pushed up on his head.

"Bruce. Chief. Got a minute?"

"You found my office."

"Kristen had your card on the fridge." He sat down in the plastic chair without being asked. "Look. Man to man. This whole thing got out of hand, and I think I'm the one who can fix it."

"Fix what?"

"The wedding. The kids. Kristen. All of it." He spread his hands. "Here's what I'm thinking. You keep paying for the wedding, like you were going to. You come. You sit up front, second row, nobody makes a fuss. I walk Colton down, you get a nice mention in the toast. Everybody wins."

"You want me to pay for you to be the father."

"I want you to pay for your son's wedding." He smiled. "Stepson. Whatever. You know what I mean."

"And what are you paying for, Rob?"

"Me? Chief, I'm giving him something money can't buy. I'm giving him his dad back."

"You're twenty-two years late for that."

The smile slipped a little at the edges.

"I don't think you understand how this works," he said. "Kristen said you'd fold. She said you'd sulk a week and then you'd pay. She said, and I'm quoting her, 'Bruce always pays.'"

"She was right. For a long time."

"So what changed?"

"She asked me to disappear," I said. "And I listened."

He leaned forward and lowered his voice, like there was anybody else in the room.

"Look. Kristen's half of the house is going to come through. I know that. When it does, things'll be a lot easier for everybody. But that's six, eight weeks off. I just need somebody to float the deposits until then."

I looked at him for a long moment.

"Her half," I said.

"Our half. We're building something."

"You're building something with her half of my house."

He heard it then. You could see him hear it. He stood up a little too fast and pulled his sunglasses down over his eyes.

"You know what your problem is?" he said. "You think because you paid for everything, it's yours."

"No, Rob. I think because I paid for everything, I get to decide whether I keep paying."

"Colton's going to hate you for this."

"Maybe. That's his to decide. Not yours. Not his mother's."

He left without shutting the door. I sat there for a minute, and then I wrote down what he'd said, word for word, on the back of a claim form, the way I'd write down a homeowner's story about how the tree happened to fall on the garage. Then I folded it and put it in my shirt pocket.

Kristen came to my apartment on a Sunday night, three weeks after the barn.

She'd gotten the address from Colton. He'd helped me carry my mattress up the stairs the first weekend, without either of us saying much. I heard her heels on the metal steps before she knocked.

She was holding a bag from Gibson's, the barbecue place where we'd had our first date in 2002. Ribs, a half-chicken, white sauce. My order. She'd remembered.

"Can I come in?"

"We can talk out here."

She looked at the balcony with the dumpster view and decided not to argue.

"I brought dinner."

"Thank you. I ate."

She set the bag down on the little plastic table anyway.

"Bruce, I'm going to say something and I need you to just listen. All right? I made a mistake at the barn. I was emotional. Rob being back stirred up a lot of old things. I said things I didn't mean."

"You meant them."

"I didn't mean them the way they sounded."

"How did you mean them?"

She pressed her lips together.

"Colton deserves a wedding," she said. "That's all I care about right now. Not you, not me, not Rob. Colton. And I found a place. A country club in Athens. They had a cancellation. They'll do October 18th. But they need twelve thousand dollars by Friday, and the credit union won't give it to me without a co-signer because of the salon loan."

"So you want me to co-sign."

"For Colton."

"So Rob can walk him down the aisle."

"Rob is his father!"

"Then Rob can co-sign."

"Rob doesn't have—" She stopped.

"Doesn't have what, Kris?"

"His credit's being rebuilt. He had a bad couple of years. That's not his fault."

"Is it his fault he paid four hundred dollars in child support in twenty-two years?"

Her head came up.

"Who told you that?"

"Does it matter?"

"Faye. It was Faye." Her voice went shrill. "You've been talking to my sister behind my back?"

"Your sister called me. The night of the barn. Because she watched it happen."

"So now you're turning my own family against me. Is that it? First my son, now my sister."

"I haven't said one word about you to anybody who didn't ask me first."

"You are being so cruel. You know that? You're doing this to hurt me."

"I'm doing exactly what you asked me to do. You wanted me gone. I'm gone. You wanted me out of the pictures. I'm out. You wanted Rob to be the father. He's the father." I picked up the Gibson's bag and handed it back to her. "Fathers pay for things, Kris. Ask him."

She stood there holding the bag. For a second she looked like she was going to throw it. Then she went down the metal stairs fast, heels ringing, and I heard her car door slam in the lot below.

Forty missed calls. Then the phone went quiet for the first time in three weeks.

I sat down on the balcony. The dumpster smelled like somebody's old shrimp. It was the best seat I'd had in a while.

At a quarter past eleven, my phone buzzed again. Not Kristen.

Colton.

"I went and saw Mr. Hendry. Then I went and saw Aunt Faye. Is it true about the $41,000?"

I looked at that text for a long time. I'd told Colton to ask somebody who didn't have a horse in the race. He'd asked two of them.

I typed back: "Yes. But don't take it from me."

He answered in under a minute.

"I'm not. I'm going to ask him myself. When's the closing on the house?"

"Friday, October 17th. Four o'clock. Valley Title on Whitesburg."

"The day before the wedding was supposed to be."

"Yes."

"Mom'll be there?"

"She has to sign."

"And Rob?"

"I'd guess he'll be in the parking lot."

The three little dots came and went twice. Then:

"Alyssa and I will be there at 3:45. Don't tell Mom."

Valley Title had a conference room with a long walnut table and twelve leather chairs and a framed aerial photo of Huntsville from 1985 on the wall. The closing agent, a brisk woman named Marlene Gaddis, had two stacks of paper laid out in front of two empty chairs on opposite sides of the table, a pen on top of each, and a little bowl of peppermints in the middle.

I got there at 3:30. The buyers had already signed that morning, a young couple from Decatur with a baby on the way who'd walked through my kitchen and asked who did the tile. I'd said I did. The husband had run his hand over the grout like it was a horse.

At 3:45, Colton and Alyssa came in. Colton was in a clean shirt instead of his work clothes. Alyssa was holding his hand so tight her knuckles were pale. They sat down at the far end of the table, by the window.

"Hey, Pops," Colton said. This time he didn't correct himself.

"Hey, son. Hey, Alyssa."

"Mr. Fleming." Alyssa gave me a small, nervous smile. "Daddy says to tell you hey. He said he'd have come, but he figured it'd look like an ambush."

"Tell him I said thanks for the ribs."

At five till four, the door opened and Kristen came in. She'd had her hair done. She was wearing the navy dress she wore to funerals and court dates. She saw Colton at the end of the table and stopped dead.

"Baby. What are you doing here?"

"Hey, Mom."

"Did he ask you to come? Bruce, did you drag my son into this?"

"I asked to come," Colton said. "Is Rob here?"

"He's... he's waiting in the truck. He didn't want to make things awkward."

"Can you ask him to come in?"

"Honey, I don't think—"

"Mom. Please. Ask him to come in."

She looked at him for a long second. Then she pulled out her phone and sent a text, and a minute later the door opened again and Rob came in with his sunglasses on his head and a big easy smile already arranged on his face.

"Hey, hey. Big guy." He put his hand out to Colton. "Didn't know you'd be here."

Colton didn't take the hand.

"Sit down, Rob," he said.

Rob looked at the hand hanging in the air, laughed a little, and put it in his pocket. He sat down next to Kristen. Marlene Gaddis looked at all of us over her glasses, decided this was not her first strange closing, and slid Kristen's stack of papers a little closer to her.

"Mrs. Fleming, whenever you're ready. Each yellow tab is a signature."

Kristen picked up the pen. She didn't sign. She looked at me across the table.

"Bruce, before I do this. Can we talk? Just for a second? Not here. In the hall."

"We can talk here."

"Not in front of the kids."

"The kids are 27 and 26," I said. "They can hear it."

Colton cleared his throat.

"Actually, I have a question first," he said. "For Rob."

Rob spread his hands. "Anything, big guy."

"How much child support did you pay my mom? When I was little. Total."

The room got very quiet. Marlene Gaddis picked up a peppermint, unwrapped it, and very carefully did not look at anybody.

"Son, that's grown-folks business, that's between me and your mama—"

"I'm grown folks. How much?"

"There were some hard years. You don't know what it was like—"

"Four hundred dollars," Colton said. "Once. In 2004. Aunt Faye has a copy of the court paper. She showed me Tuesday. It says you owed forty-one thousand by the time I was twelve."

Kristen put the pen down.

"Colton, honey—"

"I'm not done, Mom." He didn't raise his voice. He sounded exactly like he did when he was nine and asking me how a water heater worked, all patience, all wanting to understand. "Rob, Mr. Hendry says you called his house asking if the bride's family would pay for the wedding now. Is that true?"

"I was just asking about tradition—"

"And Pops says you came to his office and asked him to keep paying, so you could walk me down the aisle."

Rob looked at me. I took the folded claim form out of my shirt pocket and laid it on the table, unopened. I didn't read it. I didn't have to. Rob knew what was on it.

"He said, 'Kristen's half of the house is going to come through, and things will be a lot easier for everybody,'" I said. "That was three weeks ago."

Kristen turned in her chair and looked at Rob.

"What does that mean? 'A lot easier'?"

"Babe, it means what it sounds like. We'd have a little cushion."

"You told me you'd cover the country club deposit. You told me you had it."

"I said we'd have it."

"You said you had it!"

"I said once your check clears, we'd have it." Rob's smile was gone now. "What's the difference? It's the same money. You said yourself it's coming to us. You said we'd put it in the joint account and get the business going and do the wedding right."

"The business," Colton said. "The roofing business."

"That's right."

"Mom said you owned a roofing company."

"I'm going to own one."

"With Mom's half of the house."

Rob opened his mouth, closed it, and looked at Kristen for help. And Kristen, for the first time that whole afternoon, didn't have any to give him.

She looked at him like she was seeing him in good light for the first time. Then she looked down at the papers in front of her, at the line where it said what her share of the house would be after the mortgage and the fees. $71,400. Then she looked back at Rob.

"You were going to take it," she said. "Weren't you. You were going to take my half."

"Babe, it's 'our'—"

"Don't 'babe' me."

"Kristen, you invited me back. You called me. You put that whole wedding on Facebook. What did you think—"

"I thought you came back for your son!"

That one hung in the air over the peppermints like smoke.

Colton stood up. He walked down the length of the table to where Rob was sitting and stood over him, not threatening, not close, just there. Six foot one in a clean shirt.

"Rob," he said, "I'm going to need you not to come Monday."

"Monday?"

"Alyssa and I are getting married at the courthouse. Monday at ten. Her folks, Aunt Faye, and Pops." He glanced at his mother. "And you, Mom, if you want to come. But not him."

Rob stood up. He looked at Kristen. She didn't look back. He looked at me, and I didn't say anything at all.

"This is a setup," he said. "You know that? This whole thing. He set you up, big guy. He turned you against your own blood."

"Nobody turned me," Colton said. "I asked."

Rob pulled his sunglasses down over his eyes, there in a room with no windows on that side, and walked out. Through the glass wall of the conference room we watched him cross the lobby and go out the front door. Nobody got up to follow him.

Kristen sat very still in the leather chair for a long time. Then she turned to me.

"Bruce."

"Kris."

"I made a terrible mistake."

"I know."

"Not just Rob. All of it. The barn. What I said. The card on the fridge, the Christmas card, the drawer. I know." Her eyes were wet, really wet this time. "Tell me there's a way back. Tell me if I don't sign this, we can talk about it. Just talk. You and me. Like it was."

The whole table was looking at me. Colton. Alyssa. Marlene Gaddis with a peppermint in her cheek.

"You asked me for the best gift I could give you," I said. "I gave it to you. I'm not taking it back."

"Bruce, please."

"No."

She looked at me a while longer. Then she picked up the pen and, one yellow tab at a time, signed everything Marlene Gaddis had laid out for her. When she was done she stood up, picked up her purse, touched Colton's arm on her way past, and left without a word.

Marlene slid my stack across the table to me.

"Mr. Fleming. Yellow tabs."

I signed my name nine times. Bruce A. Fleming. The same signature that had been on the bottom of eleven contracts in a white binder, and on the bottom of a deed in 2001, and on the bottom of a permission slip for a sixth-grade field trip to the Space and Rocket Center that Colton's mother had forgotten to sign.

When I set the pen down, Colton was standing next to my chair.

"Pops."

"Yeah, son."

"Monday. Ten o'clock. Courthouse, third floor." He put his hand on my shoulder. "The best gift you could give me is to show up."

I put my hand over his, and I picked up the pen one more time and wrote it on the back of my folded claim form, right under Rob's words, so I wouldn't lose it.

"I'll be there early," I said.
