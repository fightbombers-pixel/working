"Hi, you've reached Melissa! I'm probably out closing a deal, so leave me a message and I'll get right back to you!"

That was my wife's voice, bright as a Christmas commercial, coming out of my phone on speaker in a hospice room in Grand Rapids, Michigan, with her mother dying in the bed and a night nurse and me listening to it. I'd heard that greeting so many times by then that the nurse could say the last part along with it.

Forty-seven times. I called my wife forty-seven times that Thursday night to tell her that her mother had only hours left. Her phone never rang once. It went straight to that cheerful voice every time, which means it was turned off.

I hung up before the beep, set the phone face down on the windowsill, and pulled my chair closer to the bed. Then I took Ida's hand. It was light as a bird.

"I'm right here, Ida," I said. "I'm not going anywhere."

Two days later, at her mother's funeral, my wife came through the chapel doors twenty minutes late, in front of eighty people, and the first thing out of her mouth wasn't her mother's name.

What she didn't know was what was in the manila folder in the drawer of Ida's nightstand, eighteen inches from my knee, with a lawyer's business card paper-clipped to the front.

My name is Calvin Rhodes. I'm 56, and I'm a residential real estate appraiser. I've walked through about eleven thousand houses in Kent County. The owners always want to show me the granite countertops. I always go look at the foundation first. A house is worth what's holding it up, not what's shiny. Hold on to that.

The night nurse was a big, kind woman named Kendra Pike, who'd worked the overnight at Trillium House for nine years and had a way of moving through a dark room without making the floor creak. She checked Ida's oxygen, touched her forehead, and looked at me over the bed.

"Still nothing from your wife?"

"Nothing."

"You want me to try from the front desk? Sometimes people pick up a number they don't know."

"It's off, Kendra. It's not ringing. It's just off."

She was quiet a second. "Where'd you say she was?"

"Chicago. Sales conference. Furniture."

"They don't have phone chargers in Chicago?"

"I'd have thought so."

"Did you call the hotel?"

"Twice. They don't have a Melissa Rhodes registered."

Kendra didn't say anything to that. She didn't have to. She tucked the blanket over Ida's feet, the way you'd tuck in a kid, and went out and left the door open a crack so the hall light came in.

Ida Carlisle was 81. She'd been a second-grade teacher in Wyoming, Michigan, for thirty-four years, and she'd never once in the twenty-eight years I knew her let me leave her house without a foil package of something. Her husband, Walt, died in 2009. After that it was Ida in her little condo off Breton Road, and me driving her to the doctor on Tuesdays, because Melissa was always out closing a deal.

Pancreatic cancer. They found it in March. By September, she was here.

A little before midnight, she woke up.

Her eyes opened, and they were clear, clearer than they'd been in a week. She turned her head on the pillow and looked at me.

"Calvin."

"Hi, Ida."

"Is Missy coming?"

I've told some lies in my life. Not many. That one was the easiest.

"She's on her way. Her phone died. She'll be here soon."

Ida looked at me for a long time. Second-grade teachers can look at you like that. Thirty-four years of seven-year-olds telling them the dog ate it.

"No, she's not," Ida said.

"Ida..."

"I know where she is, Calvin." Her fingers moved in mine, very weak. "I've known since July."

I didn't know what to say. I just held on.

"The folder," she said. "In my drawer. Lillian has the other copy. Don't let Missy..." She stopped and breathed for a while, a long rattling breath. "Don't let her talk you out of it. You always let her talk you out of things."

"I won't."

"Promise me."

"I promise."

She closed her eyes. "You were a better son than she was a daughter," she said. "I'm sorry I never told you."

She didn't wake up again.

I called Melissa three more times after that. Forty-five. Forty-six. Forty-seven. I texted her the same thing every time. "Your mom's asking for you. Please call. Hours, Missy. Not days."

At ten minutes after four on Friday morning, Ida Carlisle died with my hand in hers and Kendra standing at the foot of the bed. She just stopped. That was all. The machine next to the bed didn't even beep. Kendra turned it off.

"I'm so sorry, Mr. Rhodes."

"Thank you for being here."

"That's my job." She put a big warm hand on my shoulder. "You weren't required to be. That makes it different."

I sat there with Ida's hand for a while longer. Then I opened the nightstand drawer.

The manila folder was there, on top of a Bible and a box of Kleenex. On the front, in Ida's teacher handwriting, all capitals and perfectly straight, it said FOR CALVIN. A business card was clipped to it. Lillian Gates, Attorney at Law, with an address on Monroe Avenue downtown.

Inside was a single envelope, sealed, with more of her handwriting on the front. "Not to be opened by Calvin. Give to Lillian Gates."

And under that, a copy of something on legal paper with a blue backing. I didn't read it. She'd told me Lillian had the other copy, and that envelope said not to be opened by Calvin, and I'd made her a promise a few hours earlier. I put the folder in my briefcase.

My phone buzzed on the windowsill.

I grabbed it, thinking, finally. It wasn't Melissa. It was a number I didn't know, from a 616 area code.

"Mr. Rhodes, you don't know me. My name is Lorna Holloway. My husband is Grant Holloway, your wife's boss. I'm sorry to text you at four in the morning. I can't sleep. I think your wife is with my husband right now. I don't think they're in Chicago."

I met Lorna Holloway at seven that morning at the Real Food Cafe on Plainfield, because I didn't know what else to do with myself and she said she'd be there in her car anyway. I hadn't slept. I'd driven home from Trillium House, showered, put on a clean shirt, and sat in my kitchen looking at the coffee maker for twenty minutes without turning it on.

She was already in a booth when I got there. Early fifties, short gray hair, a fleece vest from the Grand Rapids Marathon. A teacher's face, honestly. She looked like she'd been up all night too.

"Mr. Rhodes. Thank you for coming. I'm so sorry about your mother-in-law."

"How did you know about Ida?"

"Your wife told Grant in August that her mother was sick. Grant told me. He said it like it was a scheduling problem." She turned her coffee cup around on the saucer. "That's when I started paying attention."

"How did you get my number?"

"Your company picnic sign-up sheet. Lakeshore Office Systems, last June, Millennium Park. You signed up to bring potato salad and wrote your cell number next to it. Grant left the sheet on our fridge for two months." She almost smiled. "I'm a school librarian. I keep everything."

"I remember that picnic. Melissa told me Grant's wife couldn't make it."

"Grant told me the picnic was canceled."

We looked at each other across the table, and something passed between us that I can only describe as two people realizing they've been reading from different copies of the same lie.

She took out her phone and set it on the table between us.

"We have a family phone plan. Grant set it up years ago, back when we shared a location app for the kids. He forgot it's still on." She tapped the screen. "He told me he was at the furniture conference in Chicago. Thursday to Sunday."

On the map, there was a little blue dot. It wasn't in Chicago. It was on the Lake Michigan shore, about forty minutes southwest of us, sitting on top of a building with a name I recognized from billboards on I-196. The Harbor Pines Resort and Spa, Saugatuck. Couples packages. Wine and sunsets.

"It's been there since Wednesday night," Lorna said. "It hasn't moved, except to a restaurant and back."

I looked at that dot for a long time.

Then I took out my own phone and pulled up the last text I'd sent Melissa, at a quarter after three that morning. Your mom's asking for you. Please call. Hours, Missy. Not days.

Delivered. Not read.

"She turned her phone off," I said.

"He did too, about ten last night. I could see it go gray on the map. I tried to call him because our daughter's car broke down in Kalamazoo." Lorna's voice didn't shake, but her hands did. "I suppose they didn't want to be bothered."

"Ida died at ten after four."

Lorna put her hand over her mouth.

"I'm so sorry."

"She knew," I said. "That's the thing. Before she died. She told me she knew where Melissa was. She said she'd known since July."

"July." Lorna lowered her hand slowly. "Mr. Rhodes, what happened in July?"

"I don't know. Not yet."

Her phone lit up on the table between us. We both looked at it. On the map, next to Grant's name, the little dot had gone from gray back to blue. Phone on. A minute ago.

"He's awake," Lorna said.

I picked up my own phone and looked at it. If Grant's phone was on, Melissa's was on too. I waited. The screen stayed dark.

"She's not going to call you yet," Lorna said gently. "Not until she's worked out what to say."

"How do you know?"

"Because Grant never calls me until he has."

It was a little after two that afternoon, and I was at the Heritage Funeral Home on Cascade Road picking out a casket with a man named Mr. Ogden, when my phone finally rang.

I'd sent forty-seven calls and nine texts into the dark. At eight in the morning, I'd sent one more, the tenth. "Your mother passed away at 4:10 this morning. I was with her."

That was the one she read.

I stepped out into the hall.

"Calvin. Oh my God. Calvin." She was crying, or she was making the sounds of crying. I've lived with her for twenty-eight years and I couldn't have told you which. "I just saw. I just turned my phone on. I'm so sorry. My phone died, and the hotel had the wrong charger, and the conference had sessions all day, and..."

"Which hotel?"

"What?"

"Which hotel in Chicago, Missy?"

"The... the Hyatt. The one by the convention center."

"I called the Hyatt. Twice. They didn't have you."

There was a pause about one second too long.

"It's under the company's name. Corporate rate. They wouldn't have had my name."

"Okay."

"Calvin, my mother just died. Can we not do this right now?"

"Sure."

"Did she... was she in pain?"

"Not at the end."

"Did she ask for me?"

"Yes."

Another pause. I heard a sound behind her. A gull. Then a door closing. People in convention centers in Chicago don't usually hear gulls.

"What did she say?" Melissa asked. "At the end. Did she say anything?"

"She said she loved you."

That was a lie too. Ida hadn't said that. But Ida had been her mother, and somebody should have said it.

Melissa was quiet for a moment, sniffling. Then her voice changed. It got lower and more careful, the voice she uses when she's walking a client toward the contract.

"Calvin. Did Mom say anything about the cottage?"

The cottage. Ida and Walt bought a little yellow cottage in Grand Haven in 1974, two blocks from the beach, for nineteen thousand dollars. I'd appraised it myself for her insurance two years earlier. It was worth about four hundred and sixty thousand now. Melissa's whole childhood was in that cottage. So was every Fourth of July of our marriage.

"No," I said. "She didn't say anything about the cottage."

"Did she say anything about her will?"

"Your mother died eight hours ago, Missy."

"I know that. I know. I just... she always said everything was going to me. And with Dad gone, I'm the only one. I just want to make sure everything's where it's supposed to be."

"Where are you?"

"I told you. Chicago."

"I'm at the funeral home picking out her casket. Mr. Ogden wants to know if you want oak or cherry."

"Oh. Um. Whatever. Cherry. Mom liked cherry. Calvin, I'll be home tomorrow. I'm getting on the road first thing in the morning. Traffic out of Chicago's going to be terrible."

Through the phone, faint, I heard a man's voice somewhere behind her. He said, "Babe, the masseuse is here."

The line went quiet. Then she said, quickly, "That's... that's the room service. I have to go. I love you. I'll be home tomorrow."

She hung up.

I stood in the hallway of the Heritage Funeral Home with the phone against my ear for a while after that, listening to nothing.

Mr. Ogden came out of his office with a binder of casket pictures.

"Mr. Rhodes? Everything all right?"

"She wants cherry," I said.

I laughed, once, out loud, in the hallway of a funeral home. Mr. Ogden, to his credit, didn't blink. I think in that business you see everything.

Melissa came home Saturday at about one in the afternoon. Not first thing. One in the afternoon. Saugatuck's forty minutes away.

I was at the kitchen table writing Ida's obituary longhand on a legal pad. Our son Colby had flown in from Detroit that morning. He's 25, an engineer at a parts supplier in Auburn Hills, tall and quiet like his grandfather Walt, and he was at the counter making grilled cheese, because when Colby doesn't know what to do with his hands he cooks.

Melissa came through the door from the garage with her rolling suitcase and her sunglasses pushed up on her head. She had a tan. In Michigan. In September.

"Oh, baby," she said to Colby, and hugged him hard. Then she came to me and put her arms around my neck from behind. She smelled like a hotel lotion I didn't know.

"I'm so sorry I wasn't there," she said into my hair. "I'll never forgive myself."

"Okay."

She let go and pulled out a chair across from me.

"What happens next? With the funeral?"

"Tomorrow at two. Heritage on Cascade. Pastor Ogden's brother is doing the service. I'm writing the obituary."

"Tomorrow? That's fast."

"Your mother planned it herself. In August. She wanted it simple and she wanted it fast. She said she didn't want people standing around for a week looking at her."

"She planned her own funeral in August?" Melissa frowned. "Why didn't she tell me?"

Colby set a plate of grilled cheese down in front of his mother a little harder than he needed to.

"She tried to tell you, Mom. You were in Chicago a lot in August."

Melissa looked at him. Then back at me.

"Calvin, I want to go over to Mom's condo this afternoon. Before the funeral. There are some papers I need to find."

"What papers?"

"Her papers. Her will. The deed to the cottage. Her bank stuff. I'm her daughter, it's my responsibility."

"Lillian Gates has her papers."

"Who?"

"Her lawyer. Downtown on Monroe."

Melissa set her sandwich down. "Mom doesn't have a lawyer. Mom's will is in the fireproof box in her bedroom closet. Dad did it in 2005. Everything goes to me."

"She has a lawyer now."

"Since when?"

"Since August."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I drove her there. She said it was the dentist." I looked at Melissa across the table. "In August, your mother had me drive her downtown to the dentist three times. I sat in the truck in the parking garage. The third time, I noticed there's no dentist in that building."

Melissa stared at me. Her face did something I'd seen a few times in twenty-eight years. It went very still, the way a cat goes still.

"What did she change?"

"I don't know."

"You drove her to a lawyer three times and you don't know what she changed?"

"She didn't tell me, Missy. She told me not to ask. So I didn't."

"Mom was on painkillers in August. She was confused half the time."

"She was doing the crossword in pen in August."

Melissa stood up so fast her chair scraped across the floor.

"Where's the folder?"

"What folder?"

"Kendra the nurse said Mom kept a folder in her nightstand. I called the hospice this morning. Kendra said you took a manila folder home with you."

Colby turned around from the stove.

"You called the hospice this morning?" he said. "To ask about a folder? Mom, did you call them to ask how Grandma died?"

Melissa didn't answer him.

"Where's the folder, Calvin?"

"It's going to Lillian Gates on Monday morning. Your mother wrote on it that I'm not allowed to open it. So I didn't."

"Give it to me."

"No."

The word sat there on the kitchen table between us like something I'd set down and couldn't pick back up. In twenty-eight years, I don't think I'd ever said that word to her and kept it.

"No?"

"Your mother asked me not to let you talk me out of it. Those were about the last words she ever said. So no."

Melissa grabbed her purse off the counter and went out the door to the garage without her suitcase. A minute later I heard her car start, and then the squeal of her tires on the cement at the end of the driveway.

Colby turned off the burner under the pan.

"Dad," he said, "where was Mom really?"

I looked at my son. He's 25. He's not a kid anymore. He got that from Ida too.

"Saugatuck," I said. "With her boss."

He stood there a long time with the spatula in his hand.

"Grandma knew, didn't she?"

"She said she'd known since July."

"July." Colby set down the spatula. "Dad. The symphony. In July. When you took Grandma to the symphony downtown for her birthday, and she came back and wouldn't talk the whole ride home. She told me on the phone the next day that she'd seen something at the Amway Grand that she'd never be able to unsee. I thought she meant the prices."

Melissa didn't come home Saturday night until almost eleven. I heard the garage door go up, and then nothing for a long time, and when I looked out the kitchen window her car was sitting in the driveway with the engine running and the dome light on, and she was in the driver's seat with her phone pressed to her ear, talking with her free hand, the way she does on sales calls.

Colby came up beside me at the window with a glass of milk.

"Who's she talking to at eleven at night?"

"Who do you think?"

He drank his milk and didn't say anything. Then he said, "Grandma's condo key's gone off the hook by the door. I noticed when I came in."

I looked. The little brass key with the green plastic tag, the one Ida gave me in 2015, was gone.

A few minutes later she came in through the garage. She didn't go upstairs. She came straight to the kitchen and stood in the doorway with her car keys still in her hand.

"You turned her against me," she said.

"Good evening, Missy."

"Don't you good evening me. You've been over there every Tuesday for six years. Driving her around. Fixing her faucets. Eating her pound cake. You were working on her. And then the minute she gets sick, you drive her to some lawyer."

"She asked me to drive her."

"She was a sick old woman. She'd have said yes to anything you put in front of her."

"Then why didn't you put anything in front of her?" I said. "You had six years of Tuesdays too."

Colby set his glass in the sink and didn't turn around.

"I have a job, Calvin. A real job. With travel. Some of us can't just sit around appraising split-levels and stopping by for coffee."

"You weren't traveling Thursday."

"I was in Chicago."

"Your mother asked for you Thursday night. Near midnight. She asked, 'Is Missy coming?' And I told her you were on your way."

For a second, just a second, something cracked open in her face, and I saw the girl in the pictures at the cottage, nine years old with a sparkler.

Then it closed again.

"You had no right to lie to her for me."

"Somebody had to tell her something."

"I'm going to the probate court Monday morning," she said. "First thing. With Mom's real will. And then I'm hiring a lawyer to find out exactly what you did to my mother in August."

"Okay."

"Stop saying okay!"

"Okay," Colby said from the sink, very quietly.

She threw her keys on the counter and went upstairs, and I heard the guest room door close. Not our room. The guest room. That was the first night in twenty-eight years she chose it herself.

At a quarter after eight Sunday morning, my phone rang. A number from downtown.

"Mr. Rhodes? This is Lillian Gates. I'm so sorry about Ida. She was one of my favorite clients in forty years, and that's saying something, because I've had a lot of clients."

She sounded about seventy, with a dry voice like a librarian who'd seen everything.

"Thank you, Mrs. Gates. I have a folder for you. I was going to bring it Monday."

"Keep it till Monday. I already have a copy of everything that matters. That's not why I'm calling." A pause. "Mr. Rhodes, your wife called my office emergency line four times last night. She wanted me to read her mother's will to her over the phone."

"Did you?"

"I told her a will isn't read over the phone at ten o'clock at night to a woman who sounds like she's been drinking wine. I told her I'd be in touch this week, as the law requires." Another pause. "Then she told me she had her mother's real will in her hand. The one from 2005. She'd taken it out of a fireproof box at Ida's condo. "I'm sorry, Mrs. Gates."

"Don't be. I've been told to go pound sand by better people than your wife." She cleared her throat. "Mr. Rhodes, I'm coming to the funeral this afternoon. Ida asked me to. And she gave me something to bring along, sealed, with very specific instructions about when to open it."

"What instructions?"

"Ida told me that if her daughter came to her funeral and asked about money before she asked about her mother, I was to stand up and read it right there." Lillian's voice softened just a little. "She said, 'Lillian, she'll ask. I raised her. She'll ask.' I told her no daughter would do that at her own mother's funeral."

"What did Ida say?"

"She said, 'You don't know Missy.'"

The Heritage Funeral Home chapel on Cascade Road holds about a hundred people. By two o'clock on Sunday afternoon, there were eighty in there, which for an 81-year-old schoolteacher with no living siblings is a full house. Former students, some of them in their sixties now, gray-haired, telling each other "She made me stay in from recess for a week in 1968." The ladies from St. Andrew's food pantry, where Ida stocked shelves every Wednesday until March. Neighbors from the condo. Kendra Pike from the hospice, in her church dress, sitting in the very last row.

Lillian Gates sat in the third row on the aisle with a big leather handbag in her lap. She was small and white-haired, with half-glasses on a chain and a navy suit. She nodded to me once when I came in, and that was all.

Lorna Holloway sat by herself near the back. I'd told her the time and place. She said she wanted to pay her respects to a woman who'd known before she did.

Melissa wasn't there.

At two o'clock, the front row had me, Colby, an empty seat, and Ida's cousin Bev from Muskegon.

At two-ten, Pastor Ogden started the service anyway, because Ida's instructions had said, in capital letters, START ON TIME. HAVE SOME RESPECT FOR PEOPLE'S SUNDAY.

He read the Twenty-third Psalm. A woman who'd been in Ida's second-grade class in 1971 got up and told the story of the class hamster, Mr. Peebles, who escaped into the heating vent and was rescued by the principal with a yardstick and a piece of lettuce. Everybody laughed. Then it was my turn.

"Ida Carlisle never once let me leave her house without food," I said. "Twenty-eight years. I'd come over to fix a faucet and leave with a pound cake. People laughed, soft, the way you do at funerals.

"Thursday night, I sat with her at the end. She woke up once, near midnight. She knew exactly where she was and exactly who she was talking to. And she told me..."

The chapel doors banged open behind everybody.

Every head turned.

"I know you were in Saugatuck."

"Mrs. Rhodes," Lillian Gates said. "My name is Lillian Gates. I was your mother's attorney. I believe you called me at home last night and told me to go pound sand."

A couple of people laughed out loud before they could stop themselves.

"'Missy. If Lillian is reading this out loud, then you've done exactly what I was afraid you'd do, and I'm sorry I was right. I raised you. I know you. Lillian folded the letter, put it back in the envelope, and sat down.

It was so quiet in that chapel I could hear the air handler kick on in the ceiling.

Melissa turned around slowly in the aisle and looked at eighty people looking at her. Her mother's former students. The pantry ladies. Kendra the nurse in the last row, who had been there at ten after four in the morning when Melissa was not. And Lorna Holloway, near the back, getting to her feet.

Then the chapel doors opened again, softer this time, and a tall man in a gray suit with a tan and silver hair came in, quietly, the way a man comes in who's late to a funeral he thinks he's supposed to be at. He had a sympathy card in his hand.

Grant Holloway.

He stopped two steps inside the door. He looked at Melissa in the aisle. He looked at me at the podium. Then his eyes found his wife, three rows in front of him, standing up and looking right at him.

"Lorna," he said. "What are you doing here?"

"Paying my respects, Grant," Lorna said. Her voice carried just fine. "Same as you. To the woman who saw you with your hand on her daughter's knee at the Amway Grand in July."

Grant's tan didn't change. Everything under it did.

"Lorna, this isn't the place."

"It wasn't the place for Ida's daughter either. She came anyway." Lorna took a folded paper out of her vest pocket. "The Harbor Pines Resort sent the bill to the house on Friday, Grant. Couples' suite, Wednesday through Sunday, two massages and a sunset dinner cruise. You put it on the company card under 'Chicago furniture conference.' I scanned it to your CFO yesterday morning, with the picture of your phone sitting in Saugatuck the whole time. He called me last night to thank me." She held out the paper. "He said he'll see you at eight tomorrow. In HR."

Grant didn't take the paper. He stood there holding the sympathy card with a little bunch of lilies printed on it.

"Lorna. Please."

"Go home, Grant. Pack."

He looked at Melissa one more time. She was looking at him the way you look at a lifeboat that's just turned out to be a picture of a lifeboat.

Grant Holloway turned around and walked out of the chapel. He set the sympathy card on the guest book table on his way out. It was the only nice thing I ever saw him do.

Melissa stood alone in the center aisle.

"Calvin," she said. Her voice had gone small. "Calvin, please. Can we talk outside?"

"I'm in the middle of your mother's eulogy, Missy."

"Please. Just for one minute. Please."

I looked at the casket, cherry, the way she'd asked. I looked at Ida's photograph from the cottage, with the lake behind her and a foil package of something in her hand, because of course there was. Then I came down from the podium and stood in the aisle in front of my wife.

"Calvin," she said, close, so only the first two rows could hear. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I didn't know she was that bad. I swear to God I didn't know it was that night. If I'd known, I'd have come."

"I called you forty-seven times."

"My phone was off."

"I know. I counted."

"I made a mistake. One stupid mistake. And Mom's gone and I can't ever fix it now, and Grant's... Grant's just gone too, you saw him, he just walked out." Tears were running down into her collar now. Real ones, I think. "I don't have anybody, Calvin. You're all I have. Please. Talk to Lillian. Tell her Mom was confused. You're the trustee, she said so, you can fix it. You can give me the cottage. Nobody would have to know. Please don't let me lose everything because of one weekend."

I looked at her for a long moment. Twenty-eight years. I thought about the cottage in Grand Haven, and the Fourth of July, and the way she used to fall asleep on the drive home with her feet on the dashboard. I thought about a dark hospice room and a phone on a windowsill that never rang.

"Missy," I said, "your mother asked me not to let you talk me out of it. It's the last thing I promised her."

"She's dead, Calvin."

"I know," I said. "I was there."

I walked back up to the podium, unfolded my legal pad, and found my place.

"She woke up once, near midnight," I said to the room, "and she asked for her daughter."
