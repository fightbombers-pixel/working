"Thirty years I settled for a small, boring man who counts other people's money. I deserve better."

My wife said it standing up at our thirtieth anniversary dinner, in the back room of Primo's Steakhouse in Council Bluffs, in front of both our families. Twenty-two people. Her mother, my sister, our daughter, two aunts, cousins, in-laws. She said it holding a glass of champagne I'd paid for, and she said it like a toast.

And everybody laughed.

Not all of them. But enough. Her cousin Darrell laughed so hard he slapped the table. Her mother laughed and shook her head like it was a cute old joke. My sister Opal didn't laugh. My daughter Jodie didn't laugh. But twenty-two people is a lot of people, and the laughing was what I heard.

Thirty years. Twenty-six of them counting money in a basement for a living, so that the woman holding that glass could have a house on a cul-de-sac and a white Lexus and a closet full of shoes.

I smiled. Then I reached into the inside pocket of my sport coat, took out a plain white envelope, and slid it across the tablecloth until it stopped against her bread plate.

"Happy anniversary, Roxanne," I said.

Twenty minutes later, my wife would be sitting alone in her Lexus in the parking lot of Primo's with the engine off and her mascara running, and her mother would be knocking on the driver's window, and Roxanne wouldn't roll it down.

What she didn't know, reaching for that envelope with a little smirk, was whose name was printed in the corner of every page inside it. Not hers. Somebody else's.

My name is Elmer Pritchard. I'm fifty-seven years old, and for twenty-six years I've been the count room manager at the Missouri Belle Casino, down on the river in Council Bluffs, Iowa. Every night, every dollar that goes into every slot machine and every table drop box in that building comes to my room, and my team counts it, under cameras, twice, before it goes anywhere else. My wife was right about that much. I count other people's money. And the first thing you learn in a count room is that money always leaves a trail. Every chip, every marker, every dollar. Somebody signed for it. Somebody played it. Hold on to that.

Roxanne opened the envelope with two fingers, still smiling at her cousins, the way you'd open a greeting card you already know is going to be corny.

Then she took out the pages.

There were seven of them. Photocopies. Each one was a marker from the Prairie Star Casino, across the river in Omaha. A marker is what a casino calls it when they let you borrow money at the table against your bank account. You sign a slip, they hand you chips, and if you don't pay it back in thirty days, they send the slip to your bank like a check.

Each of these slips had Roxanne Pritchard's signature at the bottom. Five thousand dollars. Four thousand. Seven thousand five hundred. Six thousand. All the way down. Thirty-eight thousand, five hundred dollars, against our joint checking account. Since March.

On top of them, I'd stuck a yellow sticky note in my own handwriting.

"Who did you think counts the money?"

The smile went off her face the way a light goes off.

"Roxie?" her mother said. "What is it, honey?"

Roxanne didn't answer. She was turning the pages. Her hands had started to shake.

"Roxanne," I said quietly. "Read the corner."

On every one of those seven copies, up in the top right, a woman named Marcy Gaskin at the Prairie Star credit office had written in blue pen the number of the table where the marker was signed, and the time, and the player card that was logged at that table for the same session. The same name on all seven.

Valente, R.

Roxanne stood up so fast her chair went over backward. It hit the floor of that back room with a crack like a gunshot, and the laughing stopped.

"You went through my things," she said.

"No."

"You spied on me!"

"No, Roxanne. You wrote our joint checking account on a credit application at a casino. The casino called the bank. The bank called me. I'm on the account."

"That is private!"

"Not when it's our account," I said. "Not when it's thirty-eight thousand dollars of it."

"Thirty-eight thousand?" Her mother put her hand flat on the table. "Roxanne, what is he talking about?"

My daughter Jodie said, very quietly, "Mom. Who's R. Valente?"

Every face in that room turned to my wife.

And my wife, who'd stood up five minutes earlier and told twenty-two people she deserved better, grabbed her purse off the back of her fallen chair and walked out of the private room of Primo's Steakhouse without another word. The door swung shut behind her on its hinge. Through the window, we all watched her cross the parking lot in her good dress and get in her Lexus and sit there.

Her mother went after her. Roxanne didn't roll down the window.

The room was so quiet I could hear the cooks yelling back in the kitchen.

Then my sister Opal picked up her fork.

"Well," Opal said. "The prime rib's getting cold."

I'll be honest with you. The envelope was the small thing. Thirty-eight thousand dollars is a lot of money to a man like me, but it's just money, and money can be counted. The big thing was the name in the corner of every page. Because I knew that name. Every count room manager in Council Bluffs knew that name.

I'd been waiting two weeks to find out how much my wife knew about him.

The night it started was a Tuesday, sixteen days before the anniversary. I'd come up out of the count room at the end of the graveyard shift, a little after six in the morning, and there was a voicemail on my cell from a number in Nebraska.

"Elmer, it's Marcy Gaskin over at the Prairie Star. Call me when you get out. It's about Roxanne. I'm sorry."

Marcy and I go back twenty years. She started as a cage cashier at the Missouri Belle when I was a count team lead, and we worked the same graveyard shift for six years before she went across the river to run the credit office at the Prairie Star. She's fifty, tough as a two-dollar steak, and has a laugh you can hear through a concrete wall.

She wasn't laughing when I called her back from my truck.

"Elmer," she said. "I didn't know if I should call you."

"You already did, Marcy. Tell me."

"Your wife's had credit with us since March. Five thousand dollar line. She upped it twice. She's got seven markers out, thirty-eight five. The first one came due last week and we presented it to your bank, and it bounced. Your joint account didn't have it. The bank flagged it."

I sat in my truck in the employee lot with the sun coming up over the river and listened to the gulls.

"Roxanne doesn't gamble," I said. "She's never set foot in a casino. She says they smell like cigarettes and old people."

"Elmer." Marcy's voice was gentle. "She's at our place every Thursday and Saturday night. High-limit room. Blackjack."

"Thursday's her book club."

"Then her book club plays blackjack."

I didn't say anything for a while.

"Is she alone?" I said.

That was when Marcy went quiet.

"That's why I'm calling you," she said. "Instead of just letting it go to collections. Elmer, every time she signs a marker, there's a man at the table with her. Every single time. And the chips she signs for, she doesn't play them. He does. My floor supervisor logs player cards. It's his card on the table, not hers."

"Who?"

"Rick Valente."

I knew the name. Everybody in my business in this town knew the name.

Rick Valente showed up in Council Bluffs about eight years ago, from somewhere in Illinois, in a black Mercedes with a vanity plate that said BUILDS. He called himself a developer. He'd put up a sign on a cornfield off Highway 92 that said "Coming Soon: Valente Estates, Luxury Living," and the sign had been there ever since, faded to pink, in front of the same corn.

He also used to gamble at the Missouri Belle. High-limit room. Big, loud, generous with the cocktail waitresses, the kind of player that pit bosses love right up until they don't. In the spring of 2021, his markers at our place came back from his bank three times in a row. He owed the Missouri Belle sixty-two thousand dollars, and after six months of excuses, our general manager cut off his credit and barred him from the property. I know because I counted the last of his chips.

He got barred from the Horseshoe in Council Bluffs a year after that, Marcy told me. And from a place in Sioux City. A man who can't get credit in his own name anymore needs somebody else's name.

"Elmer," Marcy said on the phone that morning, "I'm going to send you copies of the markers. They've got her signature, and they were drawn on your joint account, so you're entitled to see what's being presented against it. I'll put the table logs in the corner. That's our own paperwork, and I'll answer for it if anybody asks." She paused. "The second one comes due on the fourteenth of next month. The rest after. If they bounce, our lawyers send them to the county attorney like bad checks. In Nebraska that's not a joke."

"Thank you, Marcy."

"Don't thank me. I've watched him do this to two other women in that room. I'm tired of it." She paused again. "Are you going to pay it?"

"No," I said.

"Good," Marcy said, and hung up.

I sat in the employee lot for a long time after that. Then I drove home, and Roxanne was in the kitchen in her robe drinking coffee, and she looked up at me and said, "You look tired," and I said, "Long night," and that was all.

For sixteen days, I said nothing. I went to work. I came home. I went to bed. On Thursday night, she went to book club. On Saturday night, she went to her friend Teri's to "watch a movie." And on the morning of our thirtieth anniversary, I put seven photocopies and a yellow sticky note in a plain white envelope and tucked it in the pocket of the sport coat I'd worn to our wedding.

I didn't know, until she stood up with that glass of champagne, whether I was going to give it to her.

She came home a little after midnight. I was sitting at the kitchen table with a cup of decaf and the overhead light on. She stood in the doorway in her coat with her car keys still in her hand.

"How could you," she said. "In front of everybody. In front of my mother."

"You stood up first."

"I was joking!"

"Everybody laughed," I said. "So I guess it was funny."

"You humiliated me."

"You told twenty-two people I was small and boring and you deserved better. I just showed them what better looks like."

She threw her keys on the counter. They slid off the edge and hit the floor.

"It's not what you think. Rick is a business partner. He's helping me. I've been working with him on Valente Estates. I'm going to get my real estate license, Elmer. I'm going to do something with my life for once besides sit in this house waiting for you to come up out of that basement."

"At a blackjack table."

"That's how deals get done! That's where people with money go!"

"Roxanne, I've worked in a casino for twenty-six years. Deals don't get done there. Money gets lost there. That's the whole business."

"You don't know anything about Rick."

"I know he owes the Missouri Belle sixty-two thousand dollars. I know he's barred from my casino and two others. I know he can't get a marker in his own name anywhere in western Iowa. And I know every time you signed for five thousand dollars, he was the one playing the chips."

Her face changed. Just for a second. Then she shook it off.

"That's a lie. He told me he left the Belle because they treated him badly. Because the pit boss was jealous of him."

"I counted his chips, Roxanne."

"You count everybody's chips! That's all you do! Count, count, count!" She was crying now, angry crying. "Thirty years of counting! You never once took me anywhere! Rick took me to Kansas City. Rick took me to Chicago. Rick knows what wine to order!"

"With whose money?"

She stopped.

"Who paid for Chicago?" I said.

She didn't answer.

"The markers are due," I said. "The second one on the fourteenth. If it bounces, Prairie Star sends it to the county attorney in Nebraska. That's a crime over there, writing a bad check to a casino. It's got your name on it, not Rick's."

"Then pay it!" she said. "You're the money man! It's your job! Pay it and we'll talk about everything else later!"

I picked up my decaf and drank the last of it.

"No," I said.

"What do you mean, no?"

"I mean no, Roxanne. I'm not paying it. I'm not paying any of it."

She stared at me like I'd spoken a foreign language. In thirty years, I'd never once told her no about money. Not about the Lexus, not about the kitchen remodel, not about the cruise to Alaska she went on with her sister. I'd just counted it and paid it.

"You'll pay it," she said. "You always pay. You won't let me go to jail. You don't have it in you."

"Good night, Roxanne," I said, and went up to bed.

The next morning, a little after ten, the doorbell rang. It was Roxanne's mother.

Edna Hayward is seventy-nine. She's five feet tall, she's been a widow for twelve years, and she has a way of standing in a doorway that makes you feel like you didn't wipe your feet. She'd brought Roxanne with her. And behind them, coming up the sidewalk from her own car with her scrubs still on from a night shift, was our daughter Jodie.

"Elmer," Edna said. "We need to talk about last night."

"Come in, Edna. There's coffee."

We sat at the kitchen table. Edna, Roxanne, Jodie and me. Roxanne had on sunglasses indoors.

"I've talked to Roxanne," Edna said. "She's told me everything. She made a mistake with some investments. It happens. Lots of people make mistakes with investments. Your father lost money on that bowling alley, remember?"

"I remember."

"So what's thirty-eight thousand dollars between family? You've got savings. You've got your 401(k). You pay off these silly casino things, she cuts ties with this Rick person, and everybody forgets about it. That's what families do."

"Edna," I said, "did Roxanne tell you when she signed those markers?"

"What does it matter when?"

I got up and got the envelope out of my sport coat, which was still on the back of my chair from last night. I took out the seven copies and laid them in a row across the kitchen table, the way I lay out a count sheet. Every one of them had the date and the time written in Marcy's blue pen in the corner.

"March twenty-third," I said. "Seven-forty in the evening. Five thousand."

Edna looked at it.

"April sixth. Nine-fifteen. Four thousand."

"So?"

"Edna, March twenty-third was the night you had your hip replaced at Jennie Edmundson. Roxanne told us she was staying overnight at the hospital with you. April sixth was the night you came home from rehab. She told us she was sleeping on your couch."

Edna Hayward looked at the dates for a long time. Then she took off her reading glasses and looked at her daughter.

"Roxanne," she said. "You told me you couldn't stay that night because Elmer needed you at home."

Roxanne didn't say anything.

"Mom," Jodie said. "Answer Grandma."

"I don't have to answer anybody," Roxanne said. "I'm fifty-five years old."

"You're my daughter," Edna said. "And I was alone in a hospital bed with a new hip and a catheter while you were in a casino. Signing for money. With a man named Rick." She put her glasses back on and stood up, holding on to the edge of the table. "Elmer, I'm sorry I laughed last night. I'm sorry I came here this morning."

"It's all right, Edna."

"It isn't," she said. "But thank you." She looked at Roxanne. "I'll be in the car."

She walked out. Roxanne sat there in her sunglasses.

Jodie stayed. She's twenty-seven, an ER tech at Jennie Edmundson Hospital, and she's seen every kind of bad night a person can have.

"Mom," she said. "Dad's not going to pay it."

"He'll pay it."

"No," Jodie said. "He won't. And neither will I. And neither will Grandma, now." She stood up. "I love you, Mom. I really do. But you stood up at your own anniversary and called Dad boring, while you were spending his money on a man who can't pay for his own blackjack."

Roxanne took off her sunglasses. Her eyes were red and puffy.

"You don't understand," she said. "Rick's going to pay it all back. The Estates are about to close. He's got investors. He told me. By the fourteenth. He's going to be at the Prairie Star on the fourteenth to pay it all off himself. You'll see."

"Then you don't need me," I said.

"Fine." Roxanne stood up. "Then you can be there on the fourteenth too. And you can watch him do it. And then you can apologize to me in front of everybody."

"Okay," I said.

On Monday morning I sat across from a lawyer named Lenora Banks in a second-floor office on Pearl Street, above an insurance agency, with a view of the old courthouse.

Lenora is sixty-four. She's handled divorces in Pottawattamie County since before the casinos came to town, and she's got a sign on her desk that says "Facts, Not Feelings." She read all seven markers through a pair of red reading glasses.

"Mr. Pritchard," she said, "Iowa splits marital property in what we call an equitable way. That usually means about half. But when one spouse wastes marital money on something that has nothing to do with the marriage, gambling, an affair, a boyfriend's bills, a judge can charge it against that spouse's half. We call that dissipation. It's a big word that means she spent it, so she owns it."

"Thirty-eight thousand five hundred."

"If it gets paid out of the marital accounts, yes. If it doesn't get paid…" She tapped the copies. "Then it's her debt with her name on it. And Nebraska has its own opinion about bad markers."

"I'm not paying them."

"I wasn't going to tell you to." She took off the red glasses. "Here's what we do this week. Your paycheck goes to an account in your own name starting Friday. The joint account, you leave alone. You don't move a dime out of it, so nobody can say you emptied it. I'll file the petition Wednesday. And you don't go anywhere near that Prairie Star casino unless I know about it."

"She wants me there on the fourteenth. To watch him pay."

Lenora looked at me for a while.

"Do you think he's going to pay?"

"I've counted that man's chips, Ms. Banks. He's never paid anything in his life."

"Then go," she said. "Take a witness. And don't sign anything."

On Wednesday afternoon, while Lenora was filing the petition, I got called up to the general manager's office at the Missouri Belle.

Tom Brannon has been GM for nine years. He's fifty, a former Army finance officer, and he runs that building like a ship. When I walked in, he had a single sheet of paper on his desk and his reading glasses on.

"Sit down, Elmer."

I sat.

"I got an email this morning," Tom said. "From an attorney representing a Mr. Richard Valente. It says that you, as an employee of this casino, improperly obtained confidential customer records from the Prairie Star Casino, including player tracking data, and used them in a personal dispute. It says Mr. Valente intends to report it to the Iowa Racing and Gaming Commission." He took his glasses off. "Elmer, you've worked here twenty-six years. You've never been written up once. Tell me this isn't true."

I told him the truth. All of it. Marcy's call. The markers drawn on my joint account. The copies, which Marcy had sent me because I'm a holder on that account. The notes in the corner, which were the Prairie Star's own paperwork, which Marcy had said she'd answer for. The anniversary dinner.

Tom listened without interrupting. When I was done, he picked up the phone on his desk and dialed a number from memory.

"Marcy. Tom Brannon. Yeah. I've got Elmer in my office. I've got a letter here from a lawyer for Rick Valente." He listened. "Uh-huh. Uh-huh. Your compliance officer signed off on that? In writing? Send it to me." He listened some more, and then he did something I'd never seen him do in nine years. He laughed. "No, I know. I know exactly who he is. Thanks, Marcy."

He hung up.

"Prairie Star's compliance department reviewed it last week," Tom said. "Before you ever got the copies. A marker drawn on a joint account can be shown to either account holder. The table notes are Prairie Star's own records, and they decided to share them with the account holder because the account holder's money was at risk. They put it in writing. Marcy's emailing it over." He picked up the lawyer's letter again. "Elmer, did you look up anything about Mr. Valente in our system? Anything at all?"

"No, sir. Haven't touched it."

"I know you haven't. I already checked the access log." He set the letter down. "But I did."

He turned his computer monitor around so I could see it.

It was the Missouri Belle's own player record. Valente, Richard A. Status: barred. Unpaid markers: sixty-two thousand, four hundred dollars, since May 2021. Referred to collections. Note from our old credit manager: "Player stated he'd pay 'when Estates close.' Estates have not closed."

"Elmer," Tom said, "this man is barred from my property for owing us sixty-two thousand dollars, and his lawyer just sent me a letter threatening to report one of my employees to the Gaming Commission." He picked up his desk phone again. "I'm going to have our attorney write him back. And I'm going to put a copy of this in your file, saying you did nothing wrong. So that if anybody ever asks, there's paper."

"Thank you, Tom."

"Don't thank me. Thank Marcy for writing everything down." He put the phone to his ear and then lowered it again. "One more thing. Our attorney's going to ask Mr. Valente's lawyer, very politely, when his client intends to pay us the sixty-two thousand. Since he's apparently in a position to retain counsel."

On my way out of the building that afternoon, my phone buzzed. A text from Roxanne. The first one since Saturday.

"Rick says you're trying to get him in trouble with the gaming people. He says you're jealous. Stop it, Elmer. I mean it."

And under it, a second text, a minute later.

"Are you really filing for divorce?? Lenora Banks??? My mother said you saw a lawyer."

Then a third.

"Answer me."

I didn't answer her.

By Friday night, she'd sent twenty-six texts. Jodie kept count from her phone, because I'd blocked nothing and read everything and answered nothing. Some were angry. Some were sweet. One was a picture of us at our wedding in 1994, with no words at all.

Saturday morning, she called the house phone.

"Elmer," she said, and her voice was small. "Rick's not answering me."

"Okay."

"He hasn't answered since Thursday. I went to his condo on Valley View and he wasn't there. His car wasn't there. Elmer, the second marker's due on the fourteenth. That's Tuesday. He promised me he'd be there."

"Then he'll be there."

"What if he's not?"

"Then it'll be you," I said.

She was quiet for a long time.

"Will you come?" she said. "On Tuesday? Please? Just in case?"

"I said I'd be there," I said. "I'll be there."

On Sunday afternoon, Roxanne's cousin Darrell called me. The one who'd laughed so hard at Primo's he slapped the table.

"Elmer," he said. "I owe you an apology. I'm calling to give it."

"Okay, Darrell."

"I laughed. I'm sorry. It was the champagne and it was… I don't know what it was. I'm sorry." He cleared his throat. "But that's not the only reason I'm calling. Your Rick Valente. He called me in September."

"He's not my Rick Valente."

"He called me in September," Darrell said, "and he said Roxanne gave him my number. He said she said I'd done well with my plumbing business and might want in on the ground floor of Valente Estates. Twenty-five thousand for a lot. Ten down. He said the lots would double by spring."

I put down my coffee.

"Did you give him anything?"

"Thank God, no. My wife said if I gave a man with a pink sign ten thousand dollars she'd put me in the yard." He paused. "But Elmer, he called my brother too. And Roxanne's Aunt Bev in Glenwood. Aunt Bev gave him four thousand. She told me at church this morning. She was crying about it."

"Bev's eighty-three."

"I know how old she is."

I sat at the kitchen table for a while after we hung up. Then I called Lenora at home on a Sunday, which I'd never have done a week earlier.

"Aunt Bev should call the Iowa Attorney General's consumer office tomorrow morning," Lenora said, after I'd told her. "Selling lots in a cornfield you don't own is a different kind of problem than a gambling debt. And Elmer? Tell your wife's family not to give that man one more dollar. Not one."

"I'll tell them."

"Tell them in writing," Lenora said. "You know how this goes."

I did. I wrote it in a text to Darrell, and Darrell sent it to every Hayward in the phone book.

The Prairie Star Casino sits just across the river in Omaha, on the old racetrack grounds off Interstate 80, one of the new Nebraska casinos that opened after the voters said yes in 2020. It's a newer building than the Belle. Brighter carpet, louder slot machines. The credit office is off the main cage, behind a frosted glass door, with three desks and a long counter.

I got there at two o'clock on Tuesday, the fourteenth. Jodie came with me. Lenora had told me to bring a witness, and Jodie had taken the day off and said, "Dad, I'm not missing this."

Marcy Gaskin met us at the cage. She hugged me, which she'd never done in twenty years.

"She's already here," Marcy said quietly. "She's been sitting in the credit office since one-thirty. Alone."

"Rick?"

Marcy shook her head.

Roxanne was sitting in a plastic chair against the wall of the credit office, in a camel coat I'd bought her for Christmas, with her purse in her lap and her phone in her hand. When I came in, she stood up so fast she dropped the phone.

"He's coming," she said. "He's just running late. He texted me this morning. He said two o'clock."

"Can I see the text?"

She picked up the phone off the carpet and held it out. It said, "Can't talk. Handling it. Trust me baby." It was from last Wednesday.

"That's not this morning," I said.

"It's the same thing!"

Marcy sat down behind her desk and folded her hands.

"Mrs. Pritchard," she said, "I want to make sure you understand where things are. Your second marker, seven thousand five hundred, is due today. Your first, five thousand, was returned unpaid by your bank last month. If neither is paid by close of business today, our legal department forwards both to the Douglas County Attorney. The remaining five come due over the next six weeks."

"I know," Roxanne said. "Rick's going to pay it."

"Mrs. Pritchard, Mr. Valente doesn't have credit with us. He's never had credit with us. That's why he's never signed one of these."

Roxanne opened her mouth. Then she closed it.

"That's why he needed you to," I said.

"That's not true. That's not… he was helping me learn the game. He was showing me. We were partners."

"Mrs. Pritchard," Marcy said, gently, "you signed seven markers. You never played one hand. Our floor supervisor logged every session. You sat behind him and held his drink."

At a quarter to three, the frosted glass door opened, and Rick Valente walked in.

He looked like a man who'd been sleeping in his car. Same black Mercedes jacket, same silver hair, but the hair hadn't seen a comb, and there was a coffee stain on his shirt. He had a folded check in his hand. He saw Roxanne, and then he saw me, and then he saw Jodie, and he stopped.

"Roxie," he said. "Babe. Sorry I'm late. Traffic on the bridge."

"Rick!" Roxanne got up and went to him. "Rick, tell them. Tell them you're paying."

"Absolutely. That's why I'm here." He walked up to the counter and put the folded check down in front of Marcy, smoothing it flat with his palm. "Twelve thousand five hundred. That covers both. The rest by the end of the month, when my closing goes through."

Marcy picked up the check and looked at it.

"Valente Estates LLC," she read. "Account at Western Iowa Savings."

"That's right."

"Mr. Valente, do you mind if I call your bank to confirm funds? It's our policy for business checks over five thousand."

The smile stayed on his face a half-second too long.

"Sure. Go ahead."

Marcy picked up her phone. Rick Valente turned around and looked at me. He came over, close, and lowered his voice like we were two men at a urinal.

"Elmer, right? Look, buddy. I know you're upset. Nobody likes this. But I know you've got savings. Roxie told me. Your 401(k), your little money market. If that check's short, and I'm not saying it is, maybe you cover the gap today, and I square it with you next month. Man to man. For Roxie's sake. You don't want her name going to the county attorney."

"You want me to sign for your markers," I said.

"They're her markers."

"They're your chips."

"Come on. What's the difference? It's all family money."

"Rick," I said, "I've worked in a count room for twenty-six years. I know exactly what the difference is. The difference is whose name is at the bottom."

Behind the counter, Marcy hung up her phone.

"Mr. Valente," she said, loud enough for all of us, "Western Iowa Savings tells me the account for Valente Estates LLC was closed in August. For insufficient activity."

Nobody said anything.

Rick turned around slowly.

"That's a mistake," he said. "Banks make mistakes."

"Not that one," Marcy said. She slid the check back across the counter to him. "I'm not going to take this. And I'm going to make a note that you tried to pay a debt with a check on a closed account, in front of three witnesses." She picked up a second sheet of paper off her desk. "And while you're here, Mr. Valente, I've been asked by our director of security to give you this."

She held it out. He didn't take it. So she read it.

"'Notice of trespass. Richard A. Valente is hereby barred from the property of the Prairie Star Casino, effective immediately, for conduct involving the use of another patron's credit.'" She set it on the counter on top of the bad check. "Security's waiting outside the door to walk you to your car."

Through the frosted glass, I could see the shape of a big man in a dark blazer standing very still.

Rick looked at the notice. Then he looked at Roxanne. And something in his face just let go, the way a smile lets go when there's nobody left to sell to.

"Well, that's it, Roxie," he said. "That's that. I'm done here."

"What do you mean, done?" Roxanne grabbed his sleeve. "Rick. What about the markers? What about the Estates? You said we were partners. You said you'd pay it all back by the fourteenth."

"Babe, I said a lot of things." He pulled his sleeve free. "You signed them. Not me. I never told you to sign anything."

"You told me every time! You said, 'Sign for five, Roxie, I'm on a heater!' You said that! In this building!"

"I never said that."

"You said it in front of the dealer!"

"Then the dealer can say it," Rick said. "I'm not saying anything else without a lawyer."

"You don't have a lawyer," I said. "You've got a lawyer who emailed my boss on Monday. The Missouri Belle's attorney wrote him back on Wednesday asking when you're paying them their sixty-two thousand. I'd guess he's not your lawyer anymore."

"And Rick," I said, "Roxanne's Aunt Bev in Glenwood is eighty-three. You sold her a lot in that cornfield on Highway 92 for four thousand dollars down. She's calling the Attorney General's office in Des Moines in the morning. So are two of her nephews. You might want to put that on your calendar too."

Roxanne turned to him.

"You took money from Aunt Bev?"

"She wanted in," Rick said. "Everybody wants in."

"She's eighty-three! She lives on Social Security! I gave you her number because you said you'd take her to lunch!"

"I did take her to lunch," Rick said.

Rick looked at me for a long moment. I don't think anybody had ever counted him out loud before.

"You're the boring guy," he said. "The count room guy. That's what she called you. Boring."

"I know," I said. "She said it into a microphone."

He turned and pushed out through the frosted glass door, and the big man in the dark blazer fell into step beside him without a word, and they walked together across the bright carpet, past the slot machines, out toward the parking lot. He never looked back at her. Not once.

Roxanne stood in the middle of the credit office with her purse hanging off one shoulder.

Marcy cleared her throat.

"Mrs. Pritchard. The markers."

"I don't have it," Roxanne said. Her voice was very far away. "I don't have twelve thousand dollars. I've got two hundred and eleven dollars in my purse and a Lexus with a payment."

"Then I'm required to send them to the county attorney at five o'clock," Marcy said. "I'm sorry. I truly am."

Roxanne turned and looked at me. Then at Jodie. Then at me again. The mascara was going already. She came across the office to where I was standing, and she took hold of the lapels of my coat with both hands, the way she used to when we were dancing at weddings in the nineties.

"Elmer," she said. "Please. I'm begging you. I know what I said at Primo's. I know. I was wrong. He told me to say it. He said, 'Tell him in front of everybody, so he can't back out.' I was so stupid. I was so stupid, Elmer. Please. Pay it. Just these two. Just today. You can take it out of my half. You can take everything. I'll sign anything Lenora wants. Please don't let them send my name to the county attorney. Please. Thirty years. You've always taken care of me. You're the only one who ever counted anything for me."

I looked down at my wife's hands on my coat. Her ring was still on. Mine was in a dish on my dresser at home, where I'd put it the night of the anniversary.

Jodie stood by the door with her arms folded, saying nothing. Letting me decide. That's the kind of daughter she is.

I took Roxanne's hands off my lapels, gently, one at a time, and I put them back at her sides.

"Roxanne," I said, "you told twenty-two people you settled for a small, boring man who counts other people's money."

"I know. I know I did."

"Well, I'm not going to count yours," I said. "It's your turn."
