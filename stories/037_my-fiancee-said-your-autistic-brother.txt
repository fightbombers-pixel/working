"Your autistic brother can't come to the wedding, Ben. He's ruining the aesthetic."

My fiancée said that standing in the middle of a restored red barn outside Fort Collins, eighteen days before our wedding, holding the seating chart on a foam board, in front of our wedding planner, our photographer, her mother, and two of her bridesmaids. Six people heard it. Five of them looked at the floor. Her mother nodded.

I was looking at the board. Table one, right up front by the dance floor, had a little gold place card with my brother's name on it, Theo Hollis, in the calligraphy Erin paid a woman in Loveland $4 a card to do. She'd already put a sticky note over it. The sticky note said "?? photos."

"He rocks," Erin said. "He does that thing with his hands. He's going to be in the background of every single shot, and this wedding is going on my page, Ben. Forty-one thousand people are going to see these pictures. I'm sorry, but it's true."

I had paid $38,000 for that wedding. The barn, the band, the open bar, the woman with the calligraphy. I had also paid $20,400 for the honeymoon, ten nights on a cruise ship down the Mexican Riviera, a suite with a balcony, because Erin said a regular cabin "wouldn't photograph."

I looked up from the board.

"I agree," I said. "He shouldn't be there."

Erin smiled. Her mother, Sherry, actually patted my arm. The photographer, a young guy with a beard, let out a breath like he'd been holding it.

I reached over, peeled off the sticky note, and slid my brother's gold place card out of its slot on table one. I put it in my shirt pocket, behind my pen, and buttoned the pocket.

"Thank you, Annette," I said to the planner. "You've been great."

Then I walked out of the barn to my truck.

Twenty-three days later, my phone showed sixty-three voicemails from Erin, and my brother was reading the count out loud from a deck chair off the coast of Cabo San Lucas, with a pineapple drink in his hand and his feet up.

What Erin didn't know was what was sitting on the passenger seat of my truck in that barn parking lot. A two-page form the cruise line had emailed me that morning, which I'd printed at the shop because I print everything. The heading said "Guest Name Change Request."

My name is Ben Hollis. I'm 54, and I run a plumbing company in Fort Collins, Colorado. Six trucks, nine guys, thirty years. I'd never been married. When our mother died, Theo was thirty-four and I was thirty-eight, and he's lived with me ever since. A plumber learns one thing early: water always finds the low spot, and whatever's wrong in a house, you'll find it where things settle. Hold on to that.

I sat in the truck with the engine running. Through the big open barn door, I could see Erin talking with her hands to the bridesmaids, laughing, flipping her hair. She thought we'd just solved a problem.

The form was on the seat next to me. I'd printed it to change Erin's middle name on the booking, because she'd decided to start using her maiden name again as a middle name, for her brand. One line on it had a blank for the new name.

My phone buzzed. I figured it was Erin asking where I'd gone.

It wasn't. It was a text from a number I had saved but almost never used. Rachael Vitale. Erin's older sister.

"Ben. I was standing by the bar. I heard her. Can we talk? Not about the wedding. About Theo. There's something you need to know before you do anything."

I read it twice. Then I texted back.

"Silver Mine Subs on College Ave. Tomorrow, seven in the morning."

"I'll be there."

When I got home, Theo was at the kitchen table with his library badge still clipped to his shirt, eating a peanut butter sandwich cut in four squares, which is the only correct way, and reading a book about the Queen Mary that he's read at least nine times.

"You were gone two hours and forty minutes," he said without looking up. "The walkthrough was supposed to be ninety minutes."

"It ran long."

"Did Erin like the barn?"

"She liked the barn."

"Did she like where I'm sitting? Table one. Right by the dance floor. Annette said table one is family."

I took the gold card out of my shirt pocket and set it on the table in front of him. Theo Hollis, in gold calligraphy.

He picked it up and looked at it closely, the way he looks at everything, turning it to the light.

"This is very nice handwriting," he said. "Why do you have it?"

I sat down across from him.

My brother is fifty years old. He has worked at the Poudre River Public Library for twenty-two years sorting returns, and he has never once put a book on the wrong cart. He knows the gross tonnage of every ship Cunard ever built. He doesn't like loud rooms, or shirts with tags, or people who say they'll be somewhere at three and show up at ten after. He rocks a little when he's happy. He'd been rocking for two months, every time anybody said the word wedding.

"Theo," I said. "How would you like to go on a cruise?"

He put the card down very carefully, square with the edge of the table.

"Which ship?"

"The Coral Majesty. Out of Long Beach. Ten nights. Cabo, Mazatlán, Puerto Vallarta."

He didn't answer for a long time. Then he said, very quietly, "She's 91,000 gross tons. She was built in Italy in 2009. She has a promenade deck that goes all the way around."

"That's the one."

"That's the honeymoon ship."

"It was."

He looked at me. My brother doesn't do a lot of eye contact, so when he does, you notice.

"Ben. Is it because of me?"

I'd known he'd ask. I'd been figuring out what to say for the whole drive home, and none of it was any good, so I just told him the plain thing.

"It's because of her," I said. "Not you. Never you."

He looked at the card a little longer. Then he picked up his sandwich.

"I'll need sunscreen," he said. "SPF fifty. And a lanyard for the room key, because I lose cards in my pocket."

"We'll get you a lanyard."

"A blue one."

"A blue one."

He started to rock in his chair, just a little.

That night I called Annette, the planner, and told her the wedding was off. She was quiet for a second, and then she said something I didn't expect.

"Mr. Hollis, I've been doing this for nineteen years. I want you to know I was standing right there. If anybody asks me what happened in that barn, I'm going to tell them exactly what she said."

"Nobody's going to ask, Annette."

"Oh, honey," she said. "Somebody always asks."

At seven the next morning, Rachael Vitale was already in a booth at the sub shop on College Avenue with two coffees, one of them for me. She was forty-nine, three years older than Erin, a middle-school vice principal with gray coming in at the temples that she'd stopped coloring. She looked like somebody who hadn't slept.

"I'm going to say this fast," she said, "because if I stop, I'll chicken out."

"Okay."

"That wasn't new. What she said yesterday. It was the first time she said it to your face." She turned her coffee cup in a circle. "She's been talking about Theo for months. To me. To Mom. To her girls. That the wedding is 'content.' That she's got a deal with a bridal boutique in Denver and a home décor brand, and they're paying her to post it. Sponsored, she calls it. And Theo's 'off-brand.'"

"Off-brand."

"That's the word she uses." Rachael's jaw got tight. "But that's not why I texted you, Ben. I texted you because of what she said at Mom's last Sunday. After dinner. She said once you two were married and she moved in, Theo was going into a group home. She'd already looked at three of them. She said, and I'm quoting, 'Ben will cave. He always caves. He thinks agreeing with me is the same as loving me.'"

I set my coffee down.

Water finds the low spot. You just have to know where to look.

"She's toured group homes," I said.

"Two. In Greeley. She told the lady at one of them she was Theo's 'future guardian.'" Rachael's eyes were wet, and she was angry about it. "I'm her sister. I love her. But I've watched her do this my whole life. She decides who's useful and who's in the way, and then she smiles until you agree with her. And I sat there at that barn yesterday and heard you say 'I agree,' and I thought, oh God, he's caving."

"I didn't cave."

"I know that now. Annette called me last night. Erin doesn't know yet, does she? That it's off?"

"I texted her at nine," I said. "Two words. 'Wedding's cancelled.'"

"And?"

"And she hasn't answered. She thinks I'm being dramatic. She went to bed."

Rachael started to laugh, and then she put her hand over her mouth.

"She's going to wake up," she said.

"She's awake," I said. My phone was face up on the table between us. It had been buzzing since six forty-five. The screen said eleven missed calls, and while we both looked at it, it went to twelve.

Then a text from Erin came through, and I turned the phone so Rachael could read it.

"I'm coming over with Mom. We're going to fix this. Be home by six."

Erin and Sherry came at six on the dot, which tells you everything about Erin. She'd never been on time for anything in two years, except things she'd decided to win.

Theo was upstairs in his room with his headphones on, watching a video about the Panama Canal locks. I'd asked him to stay up there, and he'd said, "Because it's going to be loud," and I'd said yes, and he'd said, "Okay, then I'll be loud too," and turned up the volume.

Erin walked in without knocking and set her purse on my kitchen counter like she was setting down a gavel. She was in a white sweater and her hair was done. Sherry came in behind her in a quilted vest and stood by the refrigerator with her arms crossed.

"Okay," Erin said. "Let's talk about this like adults."

"Sure."

"You sent me a two-word text at nine o'clock at night canceling a wedding I've been planning for fourteen months."

"I did."

"Because of one comment."

"Because of one comment."

"Ben." She laughed, short. "I said what everybody was thinking. Annette was thinking it. The photographer was thinking it. I was just the only one honest enough to say it."

"Annette called me last night," I said. "She wasn't thinking it."

Erin's smile flickered. "Annette works for me."

"Annette works for whoever pays her. That's me."

"Benjamin," Sherry said from the refrigerator, "you're being very sensitive. Erin is under a lot of pressure. This wedding is her business. You know that."

"I know that now, Sherry. Your other daughter told me this morning."

That landed. Erin's head snapped around.

"Rachael? You talked to Rachael?"

"She texted me. She was by the bar in the barn. She heard you."

"Rachael is jealous of me and she always has been. She's a vice principal at a middle school, Ben. She drives a Corolla."

"She also told me about the group homes in Greeley."

The kitchen went quiet. Upstairs, faint through the ceiling, I could hear a man's voice on Theo's video explaining how a lock fills with water.

"I was looking into options," Erin said, very carefully. "For his future. For when you're older. That's responsible. That's what a wife does."

"You told the lady at one of them you were his future guardian."

"Because I was going to be!"

"You were never going to be his guardian, Erin. I'm his guardian. I've been his guardian for sixteen years. You never even asked him what he likes for dinner."

"He likes peanut butter cut in squares. Everybody knows that. It's like living with a six-year-old."

Sherry made a little sound, and even she looked at her daughter.

I didn't raise my voice. I find I get quieter when it's bad, and this was bad.

"You stood in that barn," I said, "in front of six people, and told me my brother was ruining your aesthetic. And I agreed with you. He shouldn't be there. He shouldn't be anywhere he's a problem to be fixed in Photoshop. And neither should I."

"So you're just going to throw away two years. Over one sentence."

"Over the truest sentence you ever said to me."

She stared at me. Then she switched, the way she does. I'd seen it a hundred times with contractors and caterers. The voice went soft, and her eyes went wide, and her hand came across the counter toward mine.

"Ben. Baby. I'm sorry. Okay? I'm sorry. Theo can come. He can sit at table one. I'll take the sticky note off myself. We'll just ask the photographer to, you know, frame things."

"Frame things."

"Get the good angles."

"Erin," I said. "Listen to what you just said."

She pulled her hand back.

"Fine. You want to be like this? Fine." She grabbed her purse. "But I'm keeping the cruise. That's my honeymoon. I planned it, I picked the suite, I picked the ship, I've already told my followers. The Coral Majesty, October fourteenth. If you don't want to go, I'll take Mom."

Sherry brightened up a little at that.

"The cruise is booked in my name," I said. "On my card."

"It's a honeymoon. It's half mine."

"We're not married. There's no half."

"Then I'll sue you for the deposits I put down."

"You didn't put down any deposits."

"I put down my time! Fourteen months of my time!"

She walked to the front door, and Sherry followed her, and at the door Erin turned around.

"You're going to regret this, Ben. You're fifty-four years old, you smell like PVC glue, and you live with your brother. You think anybody else is ever going to want that?"

"I don't need anybody to want it," I said. "I already have it."

She slammed the door. The window in it rattled.

I stood in the kitchen for a minute. Then I went out to my truck, got the cruise line's form off the passenger seat, brought it inside, and sat down at the table with a pen.

Upstairs, the video had stopped. Theo was sitting at the top of the stairs with his headphones around his neck. He'd heard the whole thing. Of course he had. He hears everything.

"I'm not six," he said.

"I know you're not."

"I'm fifty. I'm four years younger than you."

"I know, buddy."

He came down the stairs, one at a time, and sat across from me, and watched me fill in the blank on the form.

New guest name: Theodore James Hollis.

"That's my whole name," he said.

"That's your whole name."

He put his hand flat on the form, the way he does when he wants a thing to stay where it is.

"Can I have the side of the bed by the balcony?"

"It's two beds now, Theo. I'll call them."

"Okay," he said. "I'd still like the side by the balcony."

The next two weeks went the way Annette said they would. Somebody always asks.

The morning after Erin and Sherry came by, Erin posted a photo on her page. It was a picture of an empty white chair in a field, with a long caption about how "sometimes the person you love can't handle a strong, independent woman," and how she'd been "blindsided weeks before my dream day by a man who chose his comfort over our future." She didn't say what had happened in the barn. She said I'd "made the choice for both of us without a conversation."

By lunchtime, it had four thousand likes and nine hundred comments, mostly hearts and women telling her she'd dodged a bullet.

My foreman, Lalo Reyes, showed it to me on his phone in the shop, standing by the pipe rack.

"Boss," he said, "you want me to say something? My wife wants to say something. My wife's ready to go to war."

"Tell your wife thank you, and no."

"Nobody even knows what happened."

"The people who were there know," I said. "That's enough for me."

Lalo shook his head and put his phone away. "You're a better man than me."

"I'm a more tired man than you."

But I didn't have to say anything, as it turned out. Because on the third day, one of the people who'd been in the barn said it for me.

It wasn't Annette. Annette was a professional, and she kept her mouth shut on the internet. It was one of Erin's own bridesmaids, a woman named Kim Duarte, who'd been standing two feet away when Erin said it. She didn't write a long post. She just left one comment under Erin's photo of the empty chair, and it sat there right below Erin's caption, where four thousand people could read it.

"I was there. She told him his autistic brother couldn't come because he was 'ruining the aesthetic.' He said 'I agree, he shouldn't be there,' and he cancelled the wedding. I'm not a bridesmaid anymore."

Lalo's wife sent it to Lalo, and Lalo sent it to me, and by the time I looked, it had more likes than Erin's whole post.

By the next morning, the empty-chair photo was gone. So was the comment. Erin had turned off comments on everything. The Denver bridal boutique had taken down the reel they'd made with her in their dresses, the one with "Coming Soon: Erin's Dream Barn Wedding" across the bottom. The home décor brand put out a short statement about "values" without using her name.

That afternoon the phone started for real.

I don't answer when I'm under a sink, and I'm under a lot of sinks, so I'd look at it when I came up for air. Seventeen missed calls. Then twenty-four. Then thirty-one by supper. Theo started keeping count for me at the table, because Theo likes to keep count of things.

"Thirty-four," he said on Thursday. "She called during the news."

"Thanks, buddy."

"Are you going to call her back?"

"Once," I said. "When it's time."

Friday, Sherry showed up at a job site, a kitchen remodel on Mulberry Street, and stood on the homeowner's driveway in her quilted vest until I came out.

"She's not eating," Sherry said. "She's lost her two sponsors. She can't go to the grocery store. People are looking at her. You need to call her and tell people it was a misunderstanding."

"It wasn't a misunderstanding, Sherry. You were there. You patted my arm."

"I was being supportive!"

"Of what?"

She didn't have an answer for that, and the homeowner, a retired teacher named Mrs. Gallo, came out on her porch with a cup of coffee and asked if everything was okay.

"This is my fiancée's mother," I said. "My ex-fiancée's mother. She was just leaving."

"Are you the one with the brother?" Mrs. Gallo said to me. "From the comment?"

I didn't say anything. I didn't have to. Mrs. Gallo looked at Sherry over her coffee for a long, slow moment, the way a teacher looks at a kid who just got caught.

Sherry got in her car.

On Monday, Erin called me from the shoulder of I-25, crying. Her car had died near the Harmony Road exit, she said, and it was getting dark, and she was scared, and could I please, please come, she didn't have anybody else.

I didn't get in the truck. I called the roadside service on the account I still had her car on, because I'd added her to my plan in the spring, and asked them to send a tow to her location.

The dispatcher called me back eleven minutes later.

"Sir, the driver's at the Harmony exit. There's no vehicle there."

"Is there a white Lexus at the Starbucks off Harmony?"

There was a pause. "Driver says yes, sir. She's inside. With a friend."

I thanked him, and cancelled the call, and took her car off my roadside plan while I was at it.

Then I sent Erin the only text I sent her that whole two weeks.

"Glad your car's okay."

That Saturday was supposed to be our wedding day. I spent it at the outlet mall in Loveland with Theo, buying him swim trunks, two linen shirts with no tags in them, SPF fifty sunscreen, and a blue lanyard. He wore the lanyard out of the store.

At the register, my phone buzzed. One voicemail. Erin. The first one she'd left, after two weeks of hanging up.

I listened to it in the parking lot, with the phone against my ear and Theo next to me holding the bag.

"Ben. It's Erin. I know you're taking him. I called the cruise line this morning to put Mom on the booking, and the girl said the second guest is already Theodore Hollis. You changed the name on my cruise. My cruise. To him." Her voice was shaking, and not with tears. "You're doing this to humiliate me. That's all this is. You're taking a man who can't even look people in the eye on the honeymoon I designed. You know what that's going to look like? Do you have any idea what that's going to look like?"

The message cut off there, because she'd hit the time limit.

Theo was looking at me.

"Was that Erin?"

"Yes."

"What's it going to look like?"

I thought about it.

"Like two brothers on a boat," I said.

He considered that for a while. Then he nodded once and put on his sunglasses.

"That's accurate," he said.

We drove to Denver on Sunday and flew to Long Beach, and Theo had the window seat and narrated the whole descent, which runways, which wind, why the pilot banked left over the water. The woman across the aisle listened to all of it and at the end said, "I learned more in twenty minutes than in thirty years of flying," and Theo said, "That's probably true," and she laughed so hard she snorted.

Monday afternoon we stood at the rail of the Coral Majesty as she pulled away from the pier, and Theo held on with both hands and rocked a little, and didn't care who saw. A band was playing on the pool deck behind us. It was loud. He'd brought his headphones, and he didn't put them on.

"91,000 gross tons," he said. "Ben. We're on her."

"We're on her."

"I've wanted to do this since I was nine."

"I know, buddy. I remember the poster."

He'd had a poster of the old Queen Mary over his bed when we were kids, in the house in Loveland, and when our dad told him to take it down because it was "a baby thing," Theo didn't speak to him for three days. Our mother bought him a frame for it. It's still on his wall.

The suite was on deck ten, with a balcony. The cabin steward, a cheerful young man from the Philippines named Rey, had folded two towels into the shape of swans and set them kissing on the bed, the way they do for honeymoons, because nobody had told him otherwise.

Theo looked at the swans for a long time.

"They think we're married," he said.

"They think somebody's married."

"Can I keep one?"

"They're towels, Theo. They'll unfold."

"I'll take a picture, then." He took a picture.

Rey came back an hour later with a second bed and a lot of apologies, and Theo told him about the ship's Italian shipyard, and Rey told him about his uncle who worked there, and the two of them talked for twenty minutes in the hallway while I unpacked.

I put my phone in the safe in the closet that first night. I took it out once a day, at breakfast, the way you'd check a pressure gauge. Theo took over the counting, because he asked to, and because I didn't want to look at it any more than I had to.

"Forty-one voicemails," he said on Tuesday morning, scrolling, with a plate of eggs in front of him on the balcony. "Twelve new ones. Do you want me to read the first sentence of each one?"

"Sure."

"'Ben, pick up.' 'Ben, this is childish.' 'Ben, I know you can hear this.' 'Ben, my mother is crying.' 'Ben, you're being cruel.'" He looked up. "That's five Bens. They all start with Ben."

"She's consistent."

"'Ben, I posted a video. Please watch it.'" Theo frowned. "Do you want to watch it?"

"No."

"I watched it."

I put my coffee down. "Theo."

"It's okay. It's two minutes and fourteen seconds. She's crying in her car. She says she made a mistake and she was under pressure and she has always loved and supported neurodivergent people." He sounded that word out carefully. "Then she says, 'I hope Theo is having a wonderful time.' Then she says her link for a new skincare sponsor is in her bio."

"She said all that in one video?"

"Yes. It has two thousand likes and three hundred comments. Most of the comments are about the skincare."

I laughed. I didn't mean to. Theo looked at me laughing, and then he started laughing too, his real laugh, the one that sounds like a car that won't quite start, and a woman on the next balcony over leaned out to see what was so funny.

That's how we met Gil and Marjorie Teague. Retired, from Zanesville, Ohio, forty-four years married. Gil had been a mailman and Marjorie had run the cafeteria at a high school. They were on their eleventh cruise, and they knew everything, including which bartender made the best piña colada and which elevator was always empty.

"You two brothers?" Marjorie said, the first night at dinner. The ship had put us at their table.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Where are your wives?"

Theo looked at me. I'd told him he could tell it however he wanted. I figured he'd skip it.

He didn't.

"Ben's fiancée said I couldn't come to the wedding because I was ruining the aesthetic," he said. "Because I'm autistic. So Ben cancelled the wedding and brought me on the honeymoon instead. This was supposed to be the honeymoon. The steward made swans."

Gil stopped chewing. Marjorie put down her fork.

"Well," Marjorie said, after a long moment. "Good for you, honey. Both of you."

"I'm not a honey," Theo said. "I'm fifty."

"Everybody's honey at my table," Marjorie said. "Gil's seventy-six and he's honey."

Gil nodded. "It's true. You get used to it."

Theo thought about that, and then he said, "Okay," and passed her the bread basket.

Wednesday was a sea day. By Wednesday, the counter was at fifty-two.

The voicemails were changing. The first ones had been angry. Then they'd gone soft, and sad. Wednesday's started to get specific, the way they do when somebody runs out of ideas.

"'Ben, I need the deposit from the barn back,'" Theo read on the balcony. "'The décor company paid me in advance for the reels, and now they want six thousand five hundred dollars back, and you're the only one who can help me. You owe me that much after what you did.'"

"What did I do?"

"She doesn't say."

"Keep going."

"'Ben, I'll take Theo to dinner when you get back. Just the two of us. I want to apologize to him myself.'" Theo stopped. He put the phone down on the little balcony table. "I don't want to go to dinner with her."

"You don't have to."

"She called me a six-year-old. I heard her. In the kitchen."

"I know, buddy."

"Six-year-olds don't sort four hundred returns a day."

"No, they don't."

He picked the phone back up and read the next one without being asked, the way you'd rip off a bandage.

"'Ben, you took my words and twisted them. I said he shouldn't be in the photos, and you made it sound like I said he shouldn't exist. You're the cruel one here. You're the one who used your brother as a weapon. Everyone's going to see that eventually.'"

He set it down again.

"Am I a weapon, Ben?"

I'll tell you the truth. That one got me. I got up and went inside, into the bathroom of that big honeymoon suite with the gold fixtures, and I ran the water in the sink so he wouldn't hear me, and I stood there for a while. A plumber knows how long a faucet can run before somebody notices. I gave it about two minutes. Then I washed my face and came back out.

Theo was still at the table. He hadn't moved.

"No," I said, and sat down across from him. "You're my brother. You're the guy who reads the voicemails so I don't have to. That's not a weapon. That's a partner."

He nodded slowly.

"Okay," he said. "I'd like to be a partner."

"You already are."

"Then as your partner," Theo said, "I think you should call her once. Like you said. When it's time."

"You think it's time?"

"She called fifty-two times. That's a lot of times."

So that afternoon, standing on the promenade deck that goes all the way around, the one Theo had been describing to me since he was nine, with the Pacific going by on the left and a gray whale spouting so far out you had to believe somebody to see it, I took my phone out of my pocket and called Erin.

She picked up on the first ring.

"Ben. Oh my God. Ben. Thank you. Thank you for calling."

"I've got three things to tell you," I said, "and then I'm going to hang up."

"Ben, please, just listen to—"

"One. I'm not paying back your sponsor. That was your deal, in your name. Two. You're not taking Theo to dinner. He doesn't want to go, and he gets to decide that. Three. I didn't twist your words, Erin. I agreed with them. You said he shouldn't be there, and he wasn't. I'm the one who isn't there now, too. That's all I did."

"You humiliated me in front of the whole internet!"

"Kim did that. I just bought sunscreen."

"Ben—"

"Goodbye, Erin."

I hung up. I put the phone back in my pocket. I walked around the promenade deck one more time, all the way, and by the time I got back to Theo at the rail, he'd spotted the whale too.

That night, at dinner, my phone buzzed in my pocket, and since it wasn't Erin's number, Theo let me look. It was Rachael.

"Thought you'd want to know. Sunday dinner at Mom's. Erin started in on how you 'stole' her honeymoon. Mom put her fork down and said, 'Erin, I was standing in that barn. I patted his arm. I'm ashamed of that. You did this to yourself.' Then she passed the potatoes. Nobody said a word for ten minutes. Tell Theo I said hi. Tell him I want to see the whale pictures."

I read it out loud at the table, because Theo asked me to.

"Sherry said that?" Theo said.

"Sounds like it."

"Sherry nodded in the barn," Theo said. "I wasn't there, but you told me. She nodded and she patted your arm."

"She did."

"Now she put her fork down." He thought about it. "That's a change of position."

"It is."

Gil raised his glass of iced tea. "To changes of position," he said, and Marjorie clinked it, and Theo clinked it too, very carefully, because he doesn't like the noise but he likes the idea.

Then Theo took my phone and typed a reply to Rachael himself, with two fingers, and showed it to me before he sent it.

"Hi Rachael. The whale was a gray whale. It was approximately one mile away. I have four pictures but you can only see it in one. Thank you for telling Ben the truth. Theo."

He sent it. A minute later she wrote back one word, with a lot of exclamation points.

"Legend!!!"

Theo read it three times. Then he put the phone in his shirt pocket and buttoned the pocket, the same way I'd buttoned his place card into mine in the barn.

Friday we were in Cabo, anchored in the bay with the arch out there at the end of the land, and Theo was stretched out on a deck chair by the pool with his feet up and his blue lanyard around his neck and a pineapple drink with no rum in it, because he doesn't like how rum makes his mouth feel. Marjorie was in the next chair under a hat the size of a manhole cover.

He took my phone out of his shirt pocket. He'd been carrying it, at my request, so I wouldn't look.

"Sixty-three," he said.

"Sixty-three what?"

"Voicemails. Total. Since the outlet mall. The last one came in this morning. Do you want to know what it says?"

"Not really."

"It's short," Theo said. "It just says, 'I'll be at the terminal when the ship comes in. Long Beach. Thursday. I'm not going to stop until you look at me.'"

Marjorie lowered her sunglasses and looked at me over them.

"Honey," she said, "do you want Gil and me to walk off with you two on Thursday?"

I looked out at the water for a while.

"I wouldn't say no," I said.

On Thursday, ten nights after we'd sailed, the Coral Majesty came back into Long Beach a little after sunrise, and she came in slow, the way big ships do, and Theo stood at the rail the whole time and told Gil the depth of the channel.

The night before, on the last formal night, the ship's photographer had taken our picture by the grand staircase. Her name was Dottie Lang, a stout woman in her sixties with a big camera and a voice like a drill sergeant. She'd been taking pictures on that ship for eight years. She'd set us up, me in my one sport coat and Theo in a navy linen shirt with no tags, and when Theo rocked a little, she didn't tell him to stop. She just waited until he settled and took it right then, and when she showed it to us on the little screen on the back of the camera, Theo was smiling with his whole face.

"That's the one," Dottie had said. "Don't touch a thing."

We bought the print. Twenty-nine dollars. It was in a cardboard folder in Theo's carry-on as we came down the gangway into the terminal with Gil and Marjorie behind us.

Erin was standing just past the customs line, where the crowd waits with signs and balloons for people coming off. She was in the white sweater again. Her hair was done. She'd been crying, or she wanted it to look like it. When she saw us, she pushed forward past a family with a "Welcome Home Grandma" banner.

"Ben."

I stopped. Theo stopped beside me. Gil and Marjorie stopped behind us, and so did a few other people from our deck, who'd heard enough at dinner all week to know who she was.

"Ben, please. Just give me five minutes. I flew out here. I've been here since six."

"Okay," I said. "You've got five minutes."

"I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I lost everything. My sponsors. My followers. My friends won't text me back. Kim won't even look at me at the gym. Mom says I did it to myself, and maybe I did, and I'm sorry. I'll do anything. I'll go to counseling. I'll go to one of those classes about autism. I'll learn everything about ships." She looked at Theo, finally, for the first time. "Theo, I'm sorry. I really am. I should never have said that. You can come to the wedding. You can sit wherever you want. Right up front. You can be in every picture."

People around us were watching now, the welcome-home crowd, the porters, a guy from customs with a clipboard. Somebody had their phone up.

Theo looked at her for a long moment, straight on, the way he does when he's decided something.

"There isn't going to be a wedding," he said. "Ben cancelled it."

"Theo, honey, I'm talking to your brother."

"You're talking to both of us," Theo said. "Ben agreed with you. You said I shouldn't be there, and I wasn't. I was here. On the Coral Majesty. 91,000 gross tons. We saw a gray whale."

Erin looked at me, desperate. "Ben. Please. Tell him."

"He just told you," I said. "Goodbye, Erin."

I put my hand on my brother's shoulder and we walked past her toward the doors and the parking lot and the rental car, and Gil and Marjorie came right along behind us.

Behind me, I heard Erin say, to nobody, to the crowd, "He's ruining everything." It came out in the same voice as the first one, in the barn. Only this time, nobody nodded.

Then Marjorie Teague stopped, turned around, and took the cardboard folder out of Theo's hand, because he'd asked her to hold it, and opened it right there in front of Erin, so she could see the picture from the grand staircase.

"Honey," Marjorie said, loud enough for half the terminal, "I've been on eleven cruises, and that is the best-looking picture anybody took on that whole ship. Aesthetic's just fine."
