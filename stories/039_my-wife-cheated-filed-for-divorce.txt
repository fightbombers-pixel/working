"I filed this morning, Doug. And I'm asking for permanent alimony, because I'm too stressed to work."

My wife said that at my mother's eighty-first birthday dinner, at our dining room table, in front of fourteen people, right after my mother blew out the candles and right before anybody had cut the cake. She said it like she was announcing the weather. Then she folded her napkin and set it next to her plate.

My mother still had smoke curling off the candles in front of her.

Thirty-one years of marriage, and the knife for the cake was lying right there by my hand. I'd paid for that cake. I'd paid for the dining room table. I'd paid for the house the table was in, and the dental hygiene license she hadn't used since 2019, and the nineteen-thousand-dollar SUV parked out front that she'd driven to the courthouse that morning to file against me.

Nobody moved. My son-in-law had a fork halfway to his mouth. My sister Joyce's hand was over her heart.

I didn't pick up the knife. I picked up my reading glasses from my shirt pocket and put them on.

"Is the petition in your purse?" I said. "Can I read it?"

She blinked. "What?"

"The petition. You've got a copy. Can I read it?"

She actually handed it to me. I think she was so surprised she didn't know what else to do. I read all nine pages at my mother's birthday table while fourteen people watched me do it, and when I got to the sworn financial statement in the back, the one where she'd listed her assets and income, I read that page twice.

Income: none. Real property: none, other than marital residence.

"Okay," I said. I folded it up and handed it back. "Mom, you want the corner piece?"

Four months later, in Courtroom 3 of the Burleigh County courthouse, my wife would be staring at a television on a rolling cart, watching herself, and her lawyer would be trying to remember how to object.

What she didn't know was that on Tuesday, five days before my mother's birthday, a deed had crossed my desk at work. A two-bedroom condo, Unit 4B, at a new building on the river in Mandan, recorded at the county by a couple who'd bought it as joint tenants. Shari Purcell and Boyd Shepard.

My name is Doug Purcell. I'm fifty-eight years old, and I own Purcell Abstract and Title in Bismarck, North Dakota. For thirty-four years I've read deeds and mortgages and liens all day so that when somebody buys a house, they know who really owns it. Here is the only thing that job has ever taught me: paper doesn't lie. People lie about paper. Hold on to that.

My mother looked at Shari. Then she looked at the corner piece of cake I'd cut for her. Then she looked at Shari again.

"Too stressed to work," my mother said. "Honey, I worked at the Woolworth's lunch counter till I was seventy. I'll tell you what's stressful. Standing up."

Somebody snorted. My son-in-law put his fork down.

"Ethel, this isn't your business," Shari said.

"It's my birthday," my mother said. "Everything's my business today."

A horn honked outside. Twice. Short and impatient.

Shari stood up and picked up her purse with the petition in it.

"That's my ride," she said. "I'll be staying with a friend. My lawyer will be in touch about the house."

"Who's the friend?" I asked.

She smiled at me. "Somebody who notices me."

She went out the front door. Through the dining room window I watched her walk down the driveway in the snow, in her good boots, and climb into a big black Ram with a snowmobile trailer hitch on the back and "Shepard Powersports, Mandan" in silver letters across the tailgate.

My mother leaned over to me with frosting on her lip. "Douglas," she said. "Is that the snowmobile man?"

"That's the snowmobile man, Mom."

"I never liked his commercials."

Nan Whitcomb has been practicing family law in Bismarck since before I bought my first suit. She's sixty-three, she wears cardigans with holes in the elbows, and she has a sign on her desk that says "I Charge Extra For Crying." She read Shari's petition on Monday morning with a cup of coffee and a pencil, making little marks in the margins like she was grading a spelling test.

"Permanent spousal support," she said. "Ambitious."

"Can she get it?"

"Maybe." She put the pencil down. "Here's how it works in North Dakota, in plain English. The judge looks at a list of things the courts have used for sixty years. How long you were married. How old you both are. Your health. What each of you can earn. What each of you has. Long marriage, wife hasn't worked in five years, wife says she's got anxiety and a doctor signs a letter saying so? That's the kind of case where a judge says you're paying her for the rest of her life."

"How much?"

"With your income? Maybe four thousand a month. Till she dies or remarries."

"She won't remarry," I said. "She's not that dumb."

"Most of them aren't." She tapped the back pages. "But this is the part I like. Her sworn financial statement. She signed it under penalty of perjury. That means if she lied on it, she committed a crime."

I took the copy of the deed out of my briefcase and slid it across the desk.

Nan read it. She read it again. She put her coffee down very carefully, like she was afraid of spilling it on something valuable.

"Recorded on the eleventh," she said. "She signed this affidavit on the sixteenth."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Swearing she owns no real property."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Joint tenants with the snowmobile man." She took off her glasses. "Doug, how much did they put down?"

"Forty thousand. The mortgage is in both names. It was on the recorded deed of trust. Her signature's on page six."

"Where did she get forty thousand dollars?"

"That's what I'd like to know."

Nan thought about it. Then she did something I'd never seen her do in twenty years. She grinned.

"Okay," she said. "Here's what we do. Nothing."

"Nothing?"

"Nothing, out loud. We don't tell them we've got this. We don't say a word about the condo. We let her lawyer build the whole case on that affidavit. Every brick. And then we pull it out from under him." She put her glasses back on. "Speaking of her lawyer, you know who she hired?"

"No."

"Ward Pemberton." She said it the way you'd say a tornado's name. "He drives a Porsche to court in January, Doug. In Bismarck. He's never lost a support case in his life."

"There's a first time for everything."

"That's what I keep telling him."

The first time I saw Ward Pemberton in person was three weeks later, at the interim hearing on temporary support. He was about forty-five, with a tan in February and a suit that fit him like it had been poured on, and he called Shari "my client" in a soft voice like she was made of glass.

"My client has been out of the workforce for five years, Your Honor," he said. "Her husband controlled every dollar of the household. She has been diagnosed with generalized anxiety disorder." He held up a letter. "Her physician states she is not currently able to maintain employment. She is living with a friend. She has no income and no assets. She is asking for temporary support so she can eat."

Shari sat beside him in a gray sweater with no makeup on. She looked smaller than I'd ever seen her. She dabbed at her eyes with a tissue twice, and both times it was right when Pemberton paused.

Nan stood up. "We don't dispute that Mrs. Purcell isn't currently employed, Your Honor."

"Thank you, counsel," the judge said. Judge Eleanor Price was in her sixties, with silver hair cut short and a pair of red glasses she kept taking off and putting back on. "Anything else?"

"Not at this time."

Pemberton glanced over at Nan with a little smile, like a man who'd just been handed a free lunch.

The judge looked at the papers for a long time. Then she ordered me to pay my wife thirty-one hundred dollars a month in temporary support, starting the first of March, while the divorce was pending.

Out in the hallway, by the drinking fountain, Pemberton stopped beside me and Nan.

"That's just the appetizer, Doug," he said. Nobody had told him he could call me Doug. "Wait till you see the entrée."

"I've got a big appetite," I said.

He laughed like I'd said something cute and walked off with his hand on Shari's back.

Shari didn't look at me. But at the end of the hallway, she glanced back once, quick, and I saw her face. She wasn't sad. She'd never been sad. She looked like somebody who'd just won the first hand of a long poker game and was trying not to grin.

"Thirty-one hundred a month," I said to Nan in the parking lot, with the wind coming off the river so hard it took my breath. "And you said nothing."

"I said exactly what I meant to say," Nan said. "Every dollar you pay her is another dollar she swore she needed. The more she wins now, the farther she falls."

"And if she doesn't fall?"

Nan pulled her hat down over her ears. "Doug, she signed a sworn statement saying she doesn't own a condo she owns. Gravity's going to take care of the rest."

My phone rang before I got back to my truck. It was my daughter Brooke. She's twenty-four, she teaches fourth grade in Lincoln, and she hadn't called me since the birthday dinner, because her mother had gotten to her first.

"Dad," she said. Her voice was shaking. "Mom says you starved her. She says you made her ask you for grocery money for thirty years. Is that true?"

"Come by the office," I said. "Tomorrow, after school. Bring your phone."

"Why my phone?"

"Because I'm not going to tell you anything. I'm going to show you, and you're going to read it yourself."

Brooke came in at four fifteen the next afternoon with her school lanyard still around her neck and her eyes already red. My office is on the second floor of an old brick building on Fourth Street with a view of the Capitol tower, and the walls are lined with plat books going back to 1889. She sat down in the client chair across from my desk like she was there to close on a house.

"Mom said you'd do this," she said.

"Do what?"

"Try to turn me. She said you'd have spreadsheets."

"I don't have spreadsheets." I slid a single sheet of paper across the desk. "I have a check register. Thirty-one years of it. Your mother had her own debit card on our joint account the whole time. No limit. Here are the grocery charges for last year. Hy-Vee, Cash Wise, Dan's. Her card. Never mine."

Brooke looked at it. She didn't pick it up.

"That's just paper," she said. "She said you'd say that too."

"You're right," I said. "So don't read mine. Read yours."

"What?"

"Your mom texts you every day. Probably ten times a day since she moved out. Go back and read them. Not the ones about me. The other ones. Where she is. What she's doing. Who she's with."

"Dad, that's..."

"I'm not asking to see them. I'm asking you to read them. Your phone. Your messages. Take your time."

She stared at me for a long minute. Then she took out her phone, turned her chair a little toward the window so I couldn't see the screen, and started scrolling.

I went back to the plat book I'd been working on. I examined the same section of the Kirkwood addition three times without understanding a word of it.

After about ten minutes, Brooke said, "Who's Boyd?"

I didn't look up. "Who does she say he is?"

"She says he's a friend. That she's staying with." She scrolled. "She sent me a picture of the sunset over the river from the balcony. She said, 'Our new view.' Our." She scrolled more. "She said the balcony has a hot tub." Her thumb stopped. "Dad. She said 'I'm never going back to scraping plaque off strangers. Boyd says I deserve to be taken care of. And your dad's going to do it whether he likes it or not.'"

She put the phone face down on my desk.

"She told me she had anxiety," Brooke said. "She told me she couldn't get out of bed."

"She might have anxiety. I don't know. I'm not her doctor."

"She sent me a picture of a hot tub, Dad."

"I know, honey."

"She told me you starved her." Brooke's voice cracked. "I yelled at you. On the phone. I told Matt I wasn't speaking to you till Easter."

"You didn't yell. You asked. That's different."

She put her face in her hands. I came around the desk and stood next to her chair and put my hand on her back, and she let me, and she cried for a while in front of a hundred years of plat books.

The door downstairs opened and closed. Footsteps came up the stairs, slow and heavy, and my receptionist Dolly poked her head in, looking embarrassed.

"Doug, I'm sorry. There's a woman here who says she has to see you. She doesn't have an appointment. She says it's not about a house." Dolly lowered her voice. "She says she's Shari's friend. Candy."

Brooke lifted her head. "Candy Belknap?"

"You know her?"

"She's Mom's best friend. Since hygiene school. She's at every Christmas." Brooke wiped her face with her sleeve. "Dad, why is Candy Belknap here?"

Candy Belknap is a short, solid woman of fifty-five with a gray bob and a Minnesota Vikings fleece that she has worn, as far as I can tell, every day since 1998. She came into my office, saw Brooke, stopped, and then hugged her so hard Brooke squeaked.

"Oh, honey," she said. "Oh, you know, don't you. I can see it on your face."

"I just found out," Brooke said.

"Okay. Okay." Candy let go of her and turned to me, and she didn't sit down. "Doug, I'm going to say this fast because if I slow down I'll chicken out. I've known Shari thirty-three years. I stood up at your wedding. And I've got something on my phone you need to see."

"Candy, if this is going to get you in trouble..."

"It'll get me in trouble with Shari. That's all." She already had the phone out. "In January, for my fifty-fifth, six of us went to Deadwood for the weekend. The Historic Franklin Hotel. Slot machines and too much wine. Saturday night, in the bar downstairs, Shari got loaded. And she started talking. And I filmed it, because I thought it was going to be funny." She pressed her lips together. "It wasn't funny."

She held out the phone. I took it. Brooke stood up and stood next to me so she could see.

It was a bar with an old pressed tin ceiling and a band setting up in the corner. My wife was in a booth with a glass of white wine in her hand, a pink feather boa from somebody's party store around her neck, and five women laughing around her. Her face was red. She was happier than I'd seen her in five years.

"Here's the plan, girls," Shari said on the video, too loud. "I file in March. I tell the judge I'm too stressed to work. My doctor already wrote it down, I just told him I can't sleep. And Doug pays me for the rest of my life."

"Shari!" one of the women on the video squealed.

"What? He can afford it! He's got that whole title company. And I'm never going to work a day in my life again, girls. Never. Not one day. Doug pays for the retirement, and Boyd pays for the fun."

The women screamed with laughter. Somebody said, "Who's Boyd?" and Shari said, "Wouldn't you like to know," and wiggled her eyebrows, and drank half the glass. Then she looked straight into Candy's phone and said, "Don't you dare post this, Candace."

"I won't," Candy's voice said behind the camera. "Cross my heart."

The video ended.

My office was very quiet. Out the window, a city plow went by on Fourth Street with its blade down, scraping.

"She told my doctor she couldn't sleep," Brooke said slowly. "So he'd write the letter."

"That's what she said," Candy said.

"Why?" I asked. "Candy, I'm grateful. But why now? She's your best friend."

Candy finally sat down, in the chair Brooke had left.

"Two reasons," she said. "First one, last fall she borrowed six thousand dollars from me. For a 'medical thing.' I took it out of my 401(k) and paid the penalty. I found out last week the 'medical thing' was her half of a down payment on a condo in Mandan. When I asked her for it back, she laughed and said I'd get it after her alimony kicked in." She looked down at her fleece. "Second reason. Same night in Deadwood, after I stopped filming, she said I'd always been the fat one in the group, and that's why I could never keep a husband. She said it in front of everybody. And everybody laughed."

"I'm sorry, Candy."

"Don't be." She pushed the phone across the desk to me. "I'll text you the file. And I'll say all of it in court, under oath, if your lawyer wants me to."

Nan Whitcomb watched the video in her office the next morning with her reading glasses on her nose and her hands folded over her stomach. She watched it twice. The second time, she laughed out loud at "Doug pays for the retirement, and Boyd pays for the fun."

"Is it legal to use it?" I asked. "She didn't know she was on camera."

"North Dakota's a one-party state. That means as long as one person in the conversation knows it's being recorded, it's legal. Candy was in the conversation. Candy was holding the phone." Nan took her glasses off. "And it's a bar. With a band. Nobody gets privacy in a bar with a band."

"So what do we do?"

"We keep doing nothing, out loud." She was writing again. "I'll get a sworn statement from Candy. I'll subpoena the down payment records from the title company in Mandan. And I want one more thing. Where's she getting money now? Ward says no income. But she's paying for wine and boas."

"Shepard Powersports," I said. "I'd bet on it."

"Don't bet. Subpoena." She wrote it down. "We'll ask for their payroll records. Every name. Every check."

On the first Saturday in May, Shari came to the house to get her things.

She came in Boyd's black Ram with the snowmobile trailer hitch, but Boyd stayed in the truck with the engine running and his sunglasses on, looking at his phone. Shari came up the walk alone with a stack of flattened boxes from the U-Haul store and a roll of packing tape on her wrist like a bracelet.

"I want the good silver," she said, when I opened the door. "And my mother's jewelry box. And the KitchenAid."

"Take what's yours."

"It's all mine. Half of everything in here is mine, Doug. You'll find that out."

I stood aside and let her in. She went through the house like a woman shopping, picking things up and putting them down, taping boxes on the dining room table where she'd announced the divorce at my mother's birthday. I sat at the kitchen counter with a cup of coffee and my phone face down next to it and the Saturday Tribune open to the obituaries, which at my age is the sports page.

"You know what's funny?" she said from the dining room. "For thirty years you never once asked me how I was doing. And now you're going to pay me every month to do nothing. I'm going to sit in a hot tub and think about you writing that check."

"Too stressed to work," I said.

"That's right." She came into the kitchen with the KitchenAid in her arms. "Too stressed to work. My doctor says so. The judge already agreed once. You heard her."

"I heard her."

"Ward says you'll be paying till you're ninety."

"Ward says a lot of things."

My phone buzzed on the counter. Face down. Once, then again, the way it does with a text.

Shari's eyes went to it. She'd lived with me thirty-one years. She knew I never got texts on Saturday mornings. She set the mixer down on the counter right next to my phone, and her hand stayed there, a couple of inches from it.

"Who's texting you at nine on a Saturday?"

"Probably Dolly. A closing got moved."

"Dolly doesn't work Saturdays." She smiled at me. "Got a girlfriend, Doug? That would be interesting for Ward to know."

She reached for it.

I picked it up first. Not fast. Just first. I turned it over in my hand without looking at it and put it in my shirt pocket.

"It's Dolly," I said. "She's doing a closing for the Larsons on Monday and she can't find the survey."

"Show me."

"No."

We looked at each other across the kitchen counter for a long few seconds. Then she laughed, short and mean, and picked the KitchenAid back up.

"You were always boring," she said. "Even your secrets are boring."

She carried her boxes out to the Ram one at a time and Boyd never got out to help her. When the truck pulled away, I took the phone out of my pocket and looked at it.

It was from Candy Belknap. "Signed my sworn statement at Nan's yesterday. Every word. She won't know till court. Stay strong, Doug."

I deleted nothing. I put the phone back in my pocket and went back to the obituaries, and for the first time since my mother's birthday I read one all the way to the end.

The last move before the hearing was Shari's. It came in late May, in a thick envelope from Pemberton's office. A settlement offer.

Nan put it on her desk between us and tapped it with her pencil.

"They're nervous," she said. "Good offer, on its face. She keeps the support. Thirty-five hundred a month, permanent. You keep the house and the business. All very generous." She flipped to the back. "And here, on page twelve, the last page, in small print, a mutual release. Both of you give up any claim to any other property either of you might own, known or unknown."

"Known or unknown," I said.

"They're trying to get you to sign away the condo without ever telling you the condo exists. If you sign this, she keeps Unit 4B free and clear, and you can never touch it."

"They don't think I read the last page."

"Most men don't read the last page." Nan pushed it toward me. "What do you want me to tell them?"

I looked at the offer. I looked at page twelve. Then I put the whole thing back in its envelope and slid it back across the desk.

"Tell them okay."

"Okay what?"

"Just okay. Tell Pemberton his client's offer has been received and reviewed. Okay. Don't say yes. Don't say no." I stood up and put on my coat. "And don't sign anything."

Nan looked at me over her glasses for a long moment. Then she wrote on her legal pad, in big block letters, OKAY, and underlined it twice.

"Ward is going to tell her you're about to cave," she said.

"I hope so," I said. "I really hope so."

The hearing was set for the second Tuesday in July. Four months, almost to the day, after my mother's birthday.

The night before, Brooke called me.

"Mom wants me to sit with her tomorrow," she said. "On her side. Behind her. She says it'll look good for the judge."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her I'd be there." Brooke was quiet for a second. "I didn't tell her which side."

The Burleigh County courthouse sits on Thayer Avenue, a big pale stone building with a dome you can see from the river, and on the second Tuesday in July it was ninety-one degrees by nine in the morning. The air conditioning on the third floor had been broken since June. Somebody had propped the window at the end of the hallway open with a phone book.

My mother came. I'd told her not to. She'd told me she was eighty-one and she'd go where she pleased, and then she'd made my sister Joyce drive her and walked from the parking lot with her cane and her good purse and a sweater over her arm in case the courtroom was cold, which it wasn't.

"Where's the snowmobile man?" she asked, the minute she got off the elevator.

"By the window, Mom."

Boyd Shepard was standing by the open window in a sport coat with no tie, looking at his phone. He was forty-nine and built like a man who used to play hockey and now mostly watched it. He had the tan you get from a boat. When he saw me, he lifted his chin a little, like we were two guys at a stoplight.

"He's shorter than on the commercials," my mother said, loud enough that he heard it.

Shari came out of the restroom in a navy dress and flat shoes and no jewelry at all. She'd pulled her hair back. She looked like a woman who'd been through something terrible and was being brave about it, and I'll give her this, if I hadn't seen the boa I'd have bought it.

She saw Brooke sitting on the bench beside me, and her face did something complicated.

"Brooke," she said. "Honey. I thought you were sitting with me."

"I'm sitting with the truth, Mom," Brooke said. "Whichever side it's on."

Ward Pemberton came off the elevator with a rolling briefcase and his tan and a junior associate carrying two boxes. He shook Nan's hand like he felt sorry for her.

"Nan," he said. "We got your 'okay.' My client's ready to sign the agreement today, right here. Save everybody the heat."

"That's thoughtful, Ward."

"So he'll sign?"

"He said okay," Nan said. "That's all I've got."

Pemberton glanced at me. I was reading the courthouse building directory on the wall like it was the most interesting thing I'd seen all year.

"Suit yourself," he said. "We'll do it the hard way."

Courtroom 3 has wood paneling, a flag in the corner, an old ceiling fan that turns too slowly to do anything, and a television on a rolling cart that the bailiff wheels in for exhibits. Nan had asked for the cart that morning. Pemberton had noticed and hadn't asked why.

Judge Eleanor Price came in at nine thirty with her red glasses in her hand and sat down and said, "Purcell versus Purcell. Final hearing on spousal support. Mr. Pemberton, it's your client's motion."

Pemberton called Shari first.

She took the oath with her hand flat on the Bible and her voice very quiet. He walked her through it gently. The thirty-one years. Raising Brooke. Working as a hygienist until 2019, when she said the stress of my "controlling behavior" made it impossible to go on. The anxiety. The trouble sleeping. The doctor's letter, which he had her read out loud.

"Mrs. Purcell," he said, very softly, "can you work right now?"

"No." Her voice broke. "I've tried. I can't even go into the grocery store some days without my heart racing."

"Do you have any income at all?"

"None."

"Do you own any property other than the marital home?"

"No."

"Where are you living?"

"With a friend. Who's been very kind to me."

Out of the corner of my eye I saw Boyd Shepard, in the second row behind her, shift on the bench and look at the floor.

"What are you asking this court for today?"

Shari turned and looked at the judge with wet eyes. "I'm asking for enough to live on. That's all. I gave him thirty-one years. I just don't want to be afraid anymore."

"Thank you, Mrs. Purcell. Nothing further."

Nan stood up slowly. She buttoned her cardigan, which she never does.

"Mrs. Purcell," she said. "Just a few questions. I'll be quick. It's hot."

"Okay."

"You signed a sworn financial statement on March sixteenth, isn't that right? This one." She held up the paper. "Under penalty of perjury."

"Yes."

"And that's still true today? Everything in it?"

"Yes."

"No income?"

"No."

"No property but the house?"

Shari glanced at Pemberton. He gave her a tiny nod.

"No," she said.

"Thank you. And would you say you're able to work, in any capacity, right now?"

"No. I told you. I'm too stressed to work."

"Thank you," Nan said, and sat down.

Pemberton actually looked disappointed. I think he'd been looking forward to a fight.

He called the doctor next, on the telephone, a family physician from Mandan who said Shari had come to him in February complaining that she couldn't sleep, that she was worried about money and her marriage, and that he'd diagnosed anxiety and written the letter at her request. Nan asked him one question.

"Doctor, did you ever see Mrs. Purcell outside your office? At a bar, say? In Deadwood?"

"Objection," Pemberton said. "Relevance."

"I'll withdraw it," Nan said. "For now."

Then Pemberton rested.

The ceiling fan turned. Somebody's phone buzzed in the gallery and got silenced fast. Judge Price took off her red glasses, looked down at her notes, and put the glasses back on.

"All right," she said. "I'll tell you where I am, counsel, so nobody wastes the afternoon. This is a thirty-one-year marriage. Mrs. Purcell is fifty-four, out of work for five years, with a documented diagnosis and no assets of her own on this record. Mr. Purcell owns a profitable business. Under the guidelines, permanent support is not unusual on these facts." She looked at Nan. "Ms. Whitcomb, unless you've got something I haven't seen, I'm inclined to grant the motion."

Shari let out a breath. I heard it from across the room. Pemberton leaned back in his chair. In the second row, Boyd Shepard smiled down at his shoes.

Nan stood up.

"Just one thing, Your Honor," she said. "One exhibit. It's short. It's a video."

"What kind of video?"

"A video of Mrs. Purcell. Taken in January by a woman who was sitting next to her, who's here in the courtroom and will swear to when and where she took it. It goes straight to the petitioner's ability and intention to work."

Pemberton was on his feet. "Objection, Your Honor, we've never seen any video. This is an ambush."

"It's in our exhibit list, Ward," Nan said mildly. "Exhibit Fourteen. 'Audio-visual recording, Deadwood, South Dakota, January twentieth.' You signed for it two weeks ago."

Pemberton's associate started flipping through a binder very fast.

"I'll allow it," Judge Price said. "Bailiff, the cart."

The bailiff wheeled the television out to the middle of the courtroom and turned it so the judge could see the screen and the gallery could see it too. Nan plugged in a little laptop. I didn't look at the television. I'd seen it. I looked at my wife.

The pressed tin ceiling came up on the screen. The band in the corner. Six women in a booth. And Shari, in a pink feather boa, red in the face, laughing.

"Here's the plan, girls," Shari said, in Courtroom 3, out of a television speaker, loud and clear. "I file in March. I tell the judge I'm too stressed to work. My doctor already wrote it down, I just told him I can't sleep. And Doug pays me for the rest of my life."

The gallery made a noise. It wasn't a word. It was the noise thirty people make when they all take a breath at once.

On the screen, Shari waved her wine glass.

"And I'm never going to work a day in my life again, girls. Never. Not one day. Doug pays for the retirement, and Boyd pays for the fun."

The real Shari was standing up at the petitioner's table. I don't think she knew she'd done it.

"Turn it off," she said. "Turn that off!"

"Mrs. Purcell, sit down," Judge Price said.

"That's... she said she wouldn't... Candy, you promised! You crossed your heart!"

Candy Belknap, in the third row in her Vikings fleece, didn't say a word. She just looked back at her.

"Mrs. Purcell," the judge said again, and this time her voice could have stripped paint. "Sit. Down."

Shari sat down.

The video ended on her face. "Don't you dare post this, Candace." The bailiff turned the screen off. The ceiling fan kept turning.

"Ms. Whitcomb," the judge said, "you said the woman who took this is here."

"She is. Candace Belknap. Third row."

"Ms. Belknap, would you stand, please?"

Candy stood up in her fleece. Her chin was shaking, but her voice wasn't.

"Did you record this video, ma'am?"

"Yes, Your Honor. On my phone. January twentieth, at the Franklin Hotel in Deadwood. It was my fifty-fifth birthday."

"Has it been changed in any way?"

"No, ma'am. That's the whole thing. I didn't cut anything." Candy glanced at Shari. "I wish I had. I wish she'd never said it."

"Mr. Pemberton, do you want to examine this witness?"

Pemberton looked at Candy. He looked at his client. He looked at the binder his associate was holding open to Exhibit Fourteen, the one he'd signed for and never watched.

"Not at this time, Your Honor," he said.

"You can sit down, Ms. Belknap. Thank you."

"Your Honor," Nan said, "two more things, and they're quick. Exhibit Fifteen is a certified copy of a deed recorded in Morton County on March eleventh, five days before Mrs. Purcell signed her sworn financial statement. Unit 4B, Riverside Lofts, Mandan. Grantees, Shari Purcell and Boyd Shepard, as joint tenants. Forty thousand dollars down." She laid it on the judge's bench. "Exhibit Sixteen is the payroll register for Shepard Powersports, produced under subpoena. Shari Purcell has been on their payroll since October at nineteen hundred dollars a month. Her title is Marketing Coordinator."

"Marketing," my mother said, very clearly, from the front row of the gallery.

"Ma'am," the bailiff said.

"Sorry," my mother said, not sorry.

Judge Price picked up the deed. She read it with her red glasses on. Then she picked up the payroll register and read that. Then she picked up Shari's sworn financial statement, the one from March, the one Nan had held up an hour before, and she read that too. It took her a long time. Nobody in the room moved. In the second row, Boyd Shepard had stopped smiling at his shoes. He was looking at the back of Shari's head the way you'd look at a slot machine that just ate your last twenty.

Then Judge Price took off her glasses, set them on top of the deed, and looked at my wife.

"Mrs. Purcell," she said. "I've been on this bench nineteen years. I've seen a lot of people who were too stressed to work. Most of them were working two jobs." She tapped the papers. "You told this court under oath, an hour ago, that you have no income and no property. Here's your paycheck. Here's your condo. And here, on a television in my courtroom, is you explaining exactly how you planned to lie to me."

Shari opened her mouth.

"I'm not asking you a question," the judge said. "I'm telling you what I see. The motion for permanent support is denied. The temporary support order is vacated as of today. Ms. Whitcomb, you'll file a motion for return of the temporary support paid, and I expect I'll grant it. And I'm sending a copy of Mrs. Purcell's sworn financial statement, Exhibits Fifteen and Sixteen, and the transcript of this hearing to the Burleigh County State's Attorney, who can decide for himself what to call it." She put her glasses back on. "Mr. Pemberton, I'd advise your client not to say anything else today."

Pemberton was already sitting down. His tan had gone kind of gray.

"We're adjourned till the property division," Judge Price said, and banged her gavel once, not very hard, like she was knocking on a door she didn't want to go through.

Boyd Shepard was the first one out. He didn't wait for Shari. He went out the double doors into the hallway while the gavel was still echoing, and by the time Shari caught up to him at the elevator I was ten feet behind her with Brooke and my mother, and we all heard it.

"Boyd. Boyd, wait."

"Nineteen hundred a month, Shari? You put the payroll on my company? You told me he never reads anything!"

"He doesn't! He never did!"

"He read the deed! He's a title guy! It's all he does!" He stabbed the elevator button three times. "The state's attorney? My accountant's going to have a heart attack. You said this was a sure thing."

"It was a sure thing!"

"It was a sure thing till you got drunk in Deadwood and told six women." The elevator dinged. He stepped in. "Take your stuff out of the condo by Friday. I'll buy out your half. Or I'll let the bank have it. I don't care."

"Boyd!"

The doors closed on him.

Shari stood there in her navy dress in the hot hallway with the phone book propping the window open behind her. She turned around. She saw Brooke. She saw my mother leaning on her cane. And she saw me, and her face went through all of it at once, the way a house settles all at once in a hard freeze.

"You read the last page," she said. "Didn't you. The agreement. You said okay. Ward said you'd sign."

"I read every page, Shari," I said. "That's the job."
