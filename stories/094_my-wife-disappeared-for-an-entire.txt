"I needed time away from you. You should be grateful I returned."

My wife said it laughing, standing in our kitchen in Lufkin with her suitcase at her feet, in front of her sister, her best friend and her best friend's husband, who had all driven her home after seven days without one phone call. Four people heard it, and three of them laughed with her.

I looked at the suitcase. It was the blue one I'd bought her for our 25th anniversary trip to Galveston, and it still had the airport tag from that trip on the handle. Seven days, no call, no text, and a $2,100 charge on our joint card I hadn't made.

"Good," I said. "Because from now on you'll have all the time in the world."

Then I took the black envelope off the top of the refrigerator and laid it on the kitchen table in front of her, right next to her car keys.

Nineteen minutes later, the woman who'd laughed was standing in my driveway in her bare feet, dialing my number with both hands while her sister watched from the porch.

What she didn't know was that the name of the place she'd spent that week was written on the back of that envelope, in her own sister's handwriting.

My name is Willie Pruett. I'm 58 years old, and I've been a shift foreman at the Angelina Timber sawmill outside Lufkin, Texas, for 22 of the 31 years I've worked there. A head saw will tell you before it breaks. It runs a little hot, it sings a little higher, and if you're listening you shut it down before the blade comes apart and kills somebody. Most men don't listen. Hold on to that.

Bernadette picked up the envelope with two fingers, like it might be wet.

"What is this, Willie? A card? You got me a card?"

"Open it."

"Is it an apology card? Because you should be apologizing, the way you've been moping around this house for a year." She looked back at her sister and grinned. "Margo, he got me a card."

Margo didn't grin back. Margo Tisdale was three years younger than Bernie and about ten times quieter, and right then she was looking at the floor like it owed her money.

Pam Ritter was the one still laughing. Pam had been Bernie's best friend since the two of them worked the drive-through at the Sonic on Timberland Drive in 1986, and she was the kind of woman who laughed at a thing before she knew if it was funny, just to be safe.

"Open it, Bernie," Pam said. "Maybe it's a cruise."

Dwayne, her husband, set the second suitcase down by the door and stayed there. Dwayne had worked green chain at the mill for six years back in the nineties. He knew me. He wasn't laughing.

Bernie slid her nail under the flap and pulled out the papers.

The first sheet was a copy of the Original Petition for Divorce, filed Friday afternoon at the Angelina County courthouse, with my lawyer's name at the bottom. The second was a letter from our bank, Lufkin Federal, showing that the joint checking account had been split straight down the middle on Saturday morning, half to an account in my name and half to an account in hers. The third was a typed list.

She read the list out loud before she understood what it was.

"Winter clothes, two boxes. Shoes, three boxes. Bathroom, one box. The sewing machine. The quilt from your grandmother." She stopped. "What is this?"

"Your things," I said. "They're in the garage. Boxed and labeled. Nothing broken."

"My things are in my closet."

"Not anymore."

That's when the color came up her neck, red and fast, and kept going until she looked like a tomato somebody had left on the windowsill too long.

"You packed my things?"

"You were gone a week. I had time."

"You don't get to do that! This is my house!"

"It's our house, and the lawyer will sort that out. Until he does, it's yours to live in. I'm the one leaving."

Pam stopped laughing. She actually put her hand over her mouth, which was the first time in 38 years I'd seen Pam Ritter cover anything up.

"Willie," Bernie said, and now her voice was going up into that high, tight place it went when she was losing an argument with the cable company. "Willie, I was gone for one week. One week! People take breaks. It's healthy. Dr. Phil says couples need space."

"Dr. Phil didn't pay the $2,100 on the Visa."

"That was a retreat!"

"Where?"

She opened her mouth and nothing came out. Behind her, Margo turned around and walked out onto the back porch.

I picked up my duffel bag from beside the pantry. I'd packed it Saturday night, right after I came home from the bank. Three shirts, my work boots, my shaving kit and the shoebox of cards from the drawer in the bedroom. I keep every card anybody's ever given me. Birthday, Christmas, get well, the one the crew signed when my dad died. Bernie used to call it my junk drawer. I wasn't leaving it with her.

"You're seriously walking out," she said. "Right now. In front of everybody."

"You came home in front of everybody."

"Where are you even going to go?"

"Ray's."

"Your brother has one bathroom and a raccoon in the attic!"

"The raccoon never laughed at me."

Dwayne made a noise in his throat that might have been a cough. I nodded to him, and he nodded back, and I walked out the front door and down the steps to my truck.

I was backing out when she came out barefoot onto the concrete, holding the papers in one hand and her phone in the other. My phone started buzzing on the dash before I hit the street.

One missed call. Two. Three.

I didn't answer. At the stop sign at the end of Pecan Street I looked down, and there was a text sitting under the missed calls. It wasn't from Bernie.

It was from Margo.

"I'm sorry. I'm the one who should have told you a year ago. Can I come by Ray's tonight? There's more."

My brother Ray lives in a little frame house on the north side of town that our mother left the two of us, and which I let him have because he needed it more and because he's the only man I know who can fry catfish and talk at the same time without burning either one.

He opened the door before I knocked, holding a spatula.

"Well, look at this," he said. "The prodigal husband."

"That's not how that story goes."

"It is now. Come in. Bathroom's on the left, and don't look up when you're in there."

"The raccoon?"

"His name's Gilbert. He pays no rent, but he's quiet."

I put my duffel in the back room, the one with the bunk bed we'd slept in as boys, and came back out to the kitchen. Ray had two plates going and a can of Lone Star sweating on the table for me.

"So she came back," he said.

"She came back."

"And?"

"And she said I should be grateful."

Ray set the spatula down. My brother is 61 and has been divorced twice, and he says that makes him the family expert, the way a man who's wrecked two trucks is an expert on ditches.

"Grateful," he said.

"That's the word she used."

"In front of who?"

"Margo. Pam. Dwayne Ritter."

"Pam laughed, didn't she?"

"Pam laughed."

"Pam would laugh at a funeral if the casket was crooked." He slid a piece of catfish onto my plate. "And you handed her the envelope."

"I handed her the envelope."

"How'd she take it?"

"She turned red."

"Like a stop sign red, or like a sunburn red?"

"Like a tomato."

Ray laughed so hard he had to hold on to the stove. And I'll be honest with you, I laughed too, standing there in our mother's old kitchen with a beer in my hand and my whole life in a duffel bag in the back room. It came out of me like air out of a tire, and it felt better than anything had felt all week.

My phone buzzed on the table. I turned it over.

"Fourteen missed calls," I said. "Six texts."

"Read me one."

"'Willie, this is childish. Come home and we'll talk like adults.'"

"Read me another one."

"'Pam says you're embarrassing me.'"

"Pam says." Ray shook his head. "Read me the last one."

"'You'll be back by Wednesday. You can't even make your own coffee.'"

"Can you make your own coffee?"

"I've made the coffee in that house every morning for 31 years. She drinks it in bed."

Ray pointed his fork at me. "Put that in the divorce."

Somebody knocked on the front door, two short taps, polite. Ray looked at me. I got up and opened it.

Margo was standing on the porch in the yellow porch light, holding her purse against her stomach with both arms, the way people hold a purse when they're afraid somebody's going to take it.

"Can I come in?" she said. "I won't stay long."

"Come in, Margo."

She sat down at Ray's kitchen table and wouldn't take a beer, so Ray gave her a glass of sweet tea, and she held that with both hands instead. She looked at me, and then at Ray, and then at me again.

"I wrote it on the envelope," she said. "Thursday, when you came by the dental office. I couldn't say it out loud, so I wrote it."

"I know."

"Toledo Bend," she said anyway. "A camp house on the Sabine side. It belongs to a man named Colt Haney."

"Who's Colt Haney?" Ray asked.

"He sells bass boats at the marina in Zavalla," Margo said. "He's 41. He wears sunglasses inside the restaurant like there's a sniper. She met him in March at the boat show at the civic center. Willie, she wasn't at any retreat. She was at his camp house the whole week."

I nodded. I'd figured that much from the back of the envelope. I hadn't figured on how much it would hurt to hear it in a kitchen out loud.

"Why are you telling me this?" I said. "She's your sister."

Margo put the tea down. Her hands were shaking a little.

"Because she borrowed $6,000 from me for that week," she said. "She told me it was for a women's wellness retreat in Austin and she'd pay me back out of her bonus. And on the drive home today, in Pam's car, she laughed about it. She said, 'Margo, you'll get your money back when Willie's done crying.'" Margo looked straight at me. "And because this isn't the first time."

The kitchen got real quiet. Even the refrigerator seemed to stop humming.

"When?" I said.

"The girls' trip to Branson in 2019. The one you paid for." She swallowed. "There weren't any girls."

Ray put his fork down very carefully, like it might go off.

I sat there and thought about Branson. I thought about the three hundred dollars I'd given her in cash for shows, and how she'd come home with a T-shirt for me that said "My Wife Went To Branson And All I Got Was This Lousy Shirt," and how I'd worn it to the mill and the crew had laughed, and I'd laughed with them.

It wasn't one bad week. It never is. There's always a saw running hot a long time before anybody hears it.

"There's one more thing," Margo said. "And this is the part I came over here to tell you, because you need to know it before you see her again."

"Go ahead."

"Pam bet her fifty dollars. In the car. Pam said you'd cry and beg her to stay, and Bernie said no, he won't even cry, he'll just come crawling home by Wednesday because he can't cook." Margo's mouth twisted. "They shook on it. Fifty dollars says you come home by Wednesday."

Ray started to laugh, then caught himself.

"And you should know this too," Margo said. "Bernie's already told her whole office that the divorce papers are a bluff. She told them you'd never go through with it. She's planning to walk into the mill on Monday and make you take her to lunch in front of your whole crew, so everybody sees who's boss."

"In front of my crew," I said.

"In front of your crew. She said, and I'm quoting her, 'Willie never says no to me where people can see.'"

Ray looked at me over his beer. "Is that true?"

I thought about it. I thought about the Christmas party in 2017 when she'd told the plant manager's wife I was "a sweetheart but not too bright," and I'd smiled and passed the bean dip. I thought about our daughter Kaylee's wedding, when Bernie told the photographer to take one without me because I "ruined the lines." I thought about the time she'd announced at Pam's fish fry that I'd been "promoted to foreman because nobody else wanted nights," and how I'd gone and gotten everybody another round of hush puppies.

"It used to be," I said.

Margo picked up her purse and stood up.

"I'm sorry, Willie," she said. "I should have told you about Branson. I just didn't want to be the one."

"You're the one now," I said. "That counts."

She left. Ray and I sat there a long time without saying anything, and then Ray got up and washed the plates, which is something I had never once seen him do.

"Monday," he said, with his back to me.

"Monday."

"What are you going to do?"

"Go to work," I said. "Like always."

Monday morning I was at the bank when it opened at seven.

The teller was a young woman named Destiny Ruiz, whose daddy used to run the planer at the mill before his back went. She looked at the screen and then at me.

"Mr. Pruett, your paycheck deposit's already set to the new account. Mrs. Pruett called first thing this morning, before we even opened, and asked us to move it back."

"Did you?"

"No, sir. It's your direct deposit. She's not on that account." Destiny lowered her voice. "She was pretty upset."

"I imagine she was."

I was at my lawyer's office on Lufkin Avenue by eight. Candice Moore was 64, wore reading glasses on a beaded chain and had handled half the divorces in Angelina County since the first Bush administration. She talked like a woman who billed by the quarter hour and didn't want to waste either one of us.

"She'll be served Tuesday," she said. "Texas is community property, Willie. The house gets split, the retirement gets split, the debt gets split. Cheating doesn't change much of that, but it can change a little, and $6,000 of her sister's money plus a $2,100 card charge spent on a boyfriend is the kind of little I like."

"I don't want to fight her."

"You don't have to fight her. You just have to not give her anything you don't owe her." She took her glasses off. "Can you do that? Because most men in your spot can't. They cave the first time she cries."

"I listened to her laugh at me in my own kitchen."

"Good. Hold on to that sound."

I was at the mill before nine, and by noon I'd counted 31 missed calls and 19 unread texts. I didn't read them. I put the phone in my locker and walked the line with the crew.

A little after noon, my lead sawyer, a big quiet man named Junior Batiste, came up beside me at the edger.

"Boss," he said. "Your wife's at the front gate."

"I know."

"She brought brisket."

"From Shorty's?"

"Looks like it. Two big sacks."

"She knows I like Shorty's."

"Yeah." Junior shifted his weight. "Boss, she ain't alone."

I walked out to the front lot with Junior two steps behind me, and by the time we got to the gate there were maybe fifteen men from my shift standing around the break shed pretending not to look. Lunch at a sawmill is thirty minutes long, and that's a lot of time for men who don't have a television.

Bernie was standing by the guard shack in her good blue dress, the one she wore to church on Easter, holding two white sacks from Shorty's Barbecue. Her hair was done. She'd put on lipstick. She smiled at me like we were in a commercial for life insurance.

Behind her, parked in the visitor spot, was a white F-250 with a bass-boat dealership sticker on the back window, and leaning against the door was a man in a tight black fishing shirt and wraparound sunglasses. It was cloudy. It was about to rain.

"Hey, baby," Bernie said, loud enough for the break shed. "I brought you lunch. Your favorite. Come on, let's eat in the truck like old times."

"Which truck?"

Her smile held. It held like a bad weld holds, right up until it doesn't.

"Don't be like that. I'm trying here, Willie. Everybody can see I'm trying."

"Everybody can see who drove you."

She glanced back at the F-250 and then back at me, and lowered her voice.

"My car's making a noise. Colt was kind enough to give me a ride."

"Your car's in my name, Bernie. I paid it off in 2020. If it's making a noise, call Wilson's Garage on Frank Avenue, and tell them to bill me."

"See?" she said, and turned halfway toward the break shed, like she was talking to the jury. "See how he is? I bring him his favorite lunch and he talks to me about a car."

Nobody in the break shed said anything. Somebody's radio was playing George Strait, and that was the only sound.

"Willie, take the food," she said through her teeth.

"I've got a ham sandwich in my locker."

"Take the food or I swear to God."

"You swear to God what?"

She didn't have an answer for that one. That's when the man by the F-250 pushed off the door and came walking over, slow, rolling his shoulders, the way men walk when they've watched too many movies and think the slowness makes them dangerous.

He stopped about four feet from me and took his sunglasses off, which I guess was supposed to mean something.

"You and me need to talk, old man," he said.

"You're Colt."

"That's right."

"You sell boats at Zavalla."

"I sell a lot of boats." He grinned. He had very white teeth, the kind you pay for. "And I'm here because this lady's been through enough. She came back to you out of the goodness of her heart, and you served her papers. That's low, man. That's real low."

"She hasn't been served yet. That's tomorrow."

"Whatever. She deserves some respect."

"From who?"

"From you!"

"She just spent seven days at your camp house on Toledo Bend," I said, not loud, just clear. "I think she got all the respect she's going to get from me on Friday afternoon at the county courthouse."

There was a sound from the break shed like fifteen men breathing in at once.

Bernie's face did the tomato thing again, worse this time, up past her ears. She hadn't known I knew where she'd been. I watched her figure out how I could know, and I watched her get it wrong, because she looked at Colt, not at her sister's car, which wasn't there.

"You told him?" she said to Colt.

"I didn't tell him nothing!"

"Then how does he know about the camp house?"

"Baby, I don't know!"

"Don't baby me in front of these people!"

Colt turned back to me, and now his grin was gone and his neck was getting thick. He stepped in close, close enough that I could smell the cologne, the expensive kind that smells like a department store had an accident.

"Here's how it's gonna go," he said. "She needed a real man for a week. She found one. And when she decides to come back to you, you're gonna say thank you, because nobody else would put up with a dried-up old sawdust jockey like you."

"Then she's got you," I said. "Good. Now you've got all the time in the world together."

He shoved me. Two hands flat on my chest, hard enough to knock me back a step against the guard shack.

I didn't shove him back. I put my hands up, open, at shoulder height, where everybody could see them, and I didn't say a word.

Junior Batiste stepped up on my right. Then Leon Coffey from the debarker stepped up on my left. Then four more men came out of the break shed and just stood there behind me in their hard hats and safety glasses, with their lunch boxes still in their hands, not saying anything, just standing. Fifteen men who'd stood on that floor with me through two plant fires and a tornado warning and the night the head rig blade cracked.

Colt looked at them. He looked at me with my hands up. He put his sunglasses back on.

"This ain't over," he said.

"It was over when you drove her home on Sunday," I said. "You just didn't know it yet."

The office door at the end of the building opened, and the plant manager came out onto the steps. Hollis Grant was 67 years old, wore a white short-sleeved dress shirt every day of his life and had been running Angelina Timber since before most of my crew was born. He didn't come down the steps. He didn't have to.

"Mr. Haney," he said. "This is private property, and you just put your hands on one of my foremen in front of a camera." He pointed up at the guard shack, where a gray camera had been bolted under the eave since 2009. "I'd get in my truck if I were you."

Colt got in his truck. He gunned it, too, and threw gravel, which was the most predictable thing I'd ever seen a man do.

That left Bernie standing by the guard shack in her Easter dress, holding two sacks of brisket, in front of fifteen men from my shift and the plant manager.

"Willie," she said. Her voice cracked. "How am I supposed to get home?"

"You can call Pam," I said. "She owes you fifty dollars. Tell her to come get you."

Bernie's mouth fell open. That was the second time in two days she'd found out I knew something she thought I didn't, and this time she knew exactly where it came from, because only three people had been in that car.

"Margo," she said. "That little...!"

"Margo lent you six thousand dollars," I said. "Be careful what you call her."

I turned around and walked back toward the mill, and my crew turned around with me like it was a drill we'd practiced. Behind me I heard her drop one of the sacks of brisket on the gravel.

I was almost to the door when Hollis called down from the office steps.

"Pruett."

"Yes, sir."

"My office. Seven tomorrow morning." He looked at me a long second over the top of his glasses. "Don't be late."

He went back inside and closed the door, and I stood there with sawdust on my boots and no idea whether I'd just kept my job or lost it.

I was in the chair outside Hollis Grant's office before seven the next morning with a cup of coffee I'd made myself in Ray's kitchen, which tasted like it had been strained through Gilbert the raccoon.

Hollis opened the door at seven on the dot.

"Sit down, Willie."

He'd never called me Willie in 31 years. I sat down.

His office hadn't changed since 1994. There was a calendar from the Texas Forestry Association on the wall, a photograph of his late wife on the desk and a framed board from the first log the mill ever cut. Hollis sat down behind the desk and folded his hands.

"Ed Thibodeaux is retiring at the end of the month," he said. "You know that."

"Yes, sir."

"That leaves me without a mill superintendent. The company wants me to bring in a young fellow from the Georgia operation with a degree and a laptop. I told them I'd rather have a man who's never missed a shift and can hear a head saw going bad from the parking lot." He paused. "I put your name in three weeks ago."

I didn't say anything. I didn't trust my voice.

"Then yesterday happened," Hollis said. "And the company's going to see that camera footage, because I'm required to send them any incident on the property. So I need to ask you straight, and I need a straight answer, because I'm going to have to defend whatever you tell me. What's going on at home?"

I looked at the picture of his wife. Her name had been Lorene, and she'd made peanut brittle for the whole mill every Christmas until the year she died.

"My wife left for a week without a word," I said. "She spent it with that man at his camp house on Toledo Bend. She came home Sunday with her sister and her friends and told me I should be grateful she came back. I filed on Friday, before she ever got home. I moved out Sunday. I haven't touched her, I haven't threatened her and I haven't said one word about her to anybody on this property until right now, because you asked."

Hollis nodded slowly.

"That's all of it?"

"That's all of it."

"You didn't hit him back."

"No, sir."

"Why not?"

"Because a man who hits back in front of a camera is a man who gets fired," I said. "And because I didn't need to. My crew was standing there."

Hollis was quiet a long time. Then he picked up his pen and wrote something on the pad in front of him.

"The board meets Thursday," he said. "Announcement's at the safety banquet Friday night at the VFW. Don't miss a shift between now and then."

"I haven't missed one in 31 years."

"I know," Hollis said. "That's why your name's on the paper."

Monday night, a little after ten, somebody knocked at Ray's door. It was Dwayne Ritter, still in his work clothes from the paper plant, holding something flat against his leg like he was embarrassed to be seen carrying it.

"She threw this in the yard Sunday," he said, and held out the black envelope. The papers were back inside it, a little bent. "Right after you drove off. Pam told me to leave it. I didn't."

"Why not?"

Dwayne scratched the back of his neck. "Because I stood in that kitchen and watched my wife laugh at a man I used to work beside," he said. "And I didn't say a word. That's on me." He turned the envelope over so I could see the back, where Margo had written "Haney camp, Sabine side, Toledo Bend" in blue ink. "Figured you'd want the whole thing. Writing and all."

"Thank you, Dwayne."

"Don't thank me. Just don't go back." He was halfway down the porch steps when he stopped. "Pam's going to owe her fifty dollars, you know. She bet you'd cry. She's mad as a wet cat."

I put the envelope in the inside pocket of my good sport coat, the one hanging on the back of the bedroom door, and I left it there.

My phone had 47 missed calls by Tuesday night. I know because Ray counted them out loud every time it buzzed on the kitchen table, like a man calling bingo.

"Forty-five," he said. "Forty-six. Ooh, a text. 'Willie, I got served at work in front of Dr. Hargrove. How could you do this to me?'"

"Read the next one."

"'Colt says you threatened him.'"

"Read the next one."

"'Colt isn't answering.'" Ray looked up. "Well, now. That's interesting."

Wednesday night it was 61 calls and our daughter.

Kaylee is 29 and lives in Houston with her husband and a job doing payroll for a hospital system, and she'd stayed out of it, which was the right thing to do. She called me a little after eight.

"Daddy," she said. "Mom says you've lost your mind."

"Your mom might be right. I'm living with your Uncle Ray."

"She says you packed her clothes in boxes and abandoned her."

"I packed her clothes in boxes. I labeled them. I left her the house, the car and half the money. Then I left."

There was a long quiet on the line. I could hear a television somewhere behind her.

"Daddy," Kaylee said, quieter. "Mom told me she was at a women's retreat in Austin last week. She sent me a picture of a yoga mat."

"Okay."

"Aunt Margo called me this afternoon."

"Okay."

"Is it true? Toledo Bend?"

I took a breath. I'd promised myself I wouldn't put my daughter in the middle of this, and I wasn't going to start now.

"Kaylee, that's between your mother and me. I'm not going to talk to you about her. But I'm not going to lie to you, either. Your Aunt Margo doesn't make things up."

Another quiet.

"Okay," Kaylee said. "I love you, Daddy. Are you eating?"

"Your Uncle Ray fries catfish every night."

"Oh, God. Go see a doctor."

Thursday, in the middle of the night, my phone rang, and it wasn't a text. It was Bernie, and she was crying, a real crying with hitches in it.

"Willie, my chest hurts. I can't breathe right. My arm feels funny."

I sat up on the bottom bunk. My heart was going. Thirty-one years is a long time, and you don't stop caring whether somebody's breathing just because they laughed at you in a kitchen.

"Hang up and call 911, Bernie."

"I want you to come."

"Hang up and call 911. Right now."

"Willie, please, I'm scared, you're the only one who..."

"I'm calling Pam," I said. "She's four minutes from you. I'm forty. Call 911."

I hung up and called Pam Ritter, and Pam answered on the second ring, wide awake, the way women answer who've been expecting the call. She said she'd go. She called me back a couple of hours later from the emergency room at Woodland Heights.

"It's not her heart," Pam said. "They ran everything. EKG, blood, the works. The doctor says it's anxiety. He gave her a pill and told her to stop drinking wine on an empty stomach."

"Is she okay?"

"She's fine. She's mad, but she's fine." Pam stopped. "Willie, she asked me in the car if you were coming. When I said no, she said, 'He'll come. He always comes.' And then you didn't."

"No."

"I've known Bernie 38 years," Pam said slowly. "And this is the first time I ever saw her wait for somebody who didn't show up."

Friday morning the last rung came, and it came in a text a little after six, while I was standing at the head rig waiting for the first log.

"Willie. I need you to co-sign for a car. The Tahoe dealer says my credit isn't enough by itself. It's just a signature. You owe me that much after 31 years."

I read it twice. Then I typed back one word, which was the first thing I'd written to her all week.

"No."

Ninety seconds later, a second text.

"Colt's gone. He found out about the house note and the credit cards and he said he wasn't signing up for my debts. He blocked me. Are you happy now?"

I looked at that one a long time, standing on the platform with the saw idling under me and the smell of fresh-cut pine all around. I wasn't happy. I want to be honest with you about that. There's no happy in it. There's just the thing you knew was coming, finally arriving.

I didn't answer.

Pam called at noon.

"Willie," she said, and there was something different in her voice, something careful. "I'm calling because I think you need to know, and because I owe you something after Sunday."

"Go ahead, Pam."

"Bernie's coming to the banquet tonight. The mill banquet at the VFW."

"How does she even know about the banquet?"

"Lorene Grant's niece works the front desk at the dental office. She told everybody yesterday that Hollis was announcing a new superintendent Friday and it was you." Pam took a breath. "Bernie's got her red dress out. She told me she's going to walk up in front of the whole mill, in front of Hollis and the company people, and ask you to take her back. She said, 'He can't say no in front of two hundred people. He never could.'"

I looked out the break room window at the yard, at the log trucks lined up waiting at the scale, at Junior Batiste eating a ham sandwich on the tailgate of his truck.

"Thanks, Pam," I said.

"Willie?"

"Yeah."

"She's wrong, isn't she? This time?"

"Come see," I said.

The VFW Post 8904 on Atkinson Drive smells like fried chicken and floor wax and about sixty years of cigarettes, and every October the mill rents it for the safety banquet. There's a buffet from Pittman's and a sheet cake from the H-E-B bakery with the mill logo on it in blue icing, and the men bring their wives and the wives bring the kids, and by eight o'clock there are better than two hundred people in that hall.

I sat at the crew table with Ray and Junior and Leon Coffey and their wives. Ray wore a tie. I didn't know Ray owned a tie. Margo sat at the end of our table with her hands in her lap. Pam and Dwayne were two tables over, and Pam kept looking at the door.

A little after eight, Hollis Grant went up to the microphone with an index card.

He talked about the safety numbers, 412 days without a lost-time accident, and everybody clapped. He talked about Ed Thibodeaux retiring after 44 years, and everybody stood up and clapped for Ed, and Ed cried a little and pretended he had something in his eye. Then Hollis put his card down.

"The company asked me to find a new mill superintendent," he said. "They sent me a fine young man from Georgia with a fine degree. I sent him home with a sack of brisket." Laughter. "Because the best sawyer I ever saw is sitting at that table right there, and he's never missed a shift in 31 years, and on Monday he stood in our parking lot with his hands up and his mouth shut while a man put hands on him, and he kept his head, and his crew kept theirs. That's what I want running my mill. Willie Pruett, come on up here."

The room came up on its feet. I'd never heard a sound like it. Junior was pounding the table so hard the iced tea glasses were jumping.

I walked up to the front, and Hollis shook my hand, and somebody from the company handed me a plaque with my name spelled right on it, which surprised me.

That's when the doors at the back of the hall opened.

Bernie came in wearing the red dress. She'd had her hair done again. She walked straight down the middle aisle between the tables, and the clapping thinned out and stopped as people saw her, the way a room goes quiet one table at a time. She walked all the way up to the front, right in front of the head table, and turned around so she was half facing me and half facing the room.

"Can I say something?" she said, loud. "I'd just like to say something."

Hollis looked at me. I nodded. He stepped back.

"Most of you know me," Bernie said, and she smiled at the room, the big smile, the life-insurance-commercial smile. "I'm Willie's wife. Thirty-one years. And I just want everybody to know how proud I am of him tonight." She turned to me, and her eyes were wet, and I believe they were really wet. "Willie, I made a mistake. I'm big enough to say it in front of everybody. I went away, and I came back, and I'm asking you, right here, to come home. I came back, didn't I? Doesn't that count for something?"

Two hundred people looked at me. Somewhere in the back a baby fussed and somebody hushed it.

"You came back," I said. "You told me I should be grateful. In our kitchen, in front of your sister and Pam and Dwayne."

"I was upset!"

"You were laughing."

"Willie..."

"I am grateful," I said, and I meant every word. "You were gone seven days, Bernie. That was the first week in 31 years nobody told me I was stupid at my own table. I slept. I made my own coffee. I listened. And I heard the thing I should have heard back in 2019, when you went to Branson."

Her face went red, all the way up, right there under the VFW lights.

"You don't get to bring that up here," she whispered.

"You came here so I'd have to answer in front of everybody," I said. "So I'm answering in front of everybody. No."

"No?"

"No."

"After everything? Where am I supposed to go, Willie? Colt's gone. Margo won't talk to me. The Tahoe man said no. Where am I supposed to go?"

I set the plaque down on the head table. I took the black envelope out of the inside pocket of my sport coat, the same one she'd thrown in the yard on Sunday and Dwayne had carried back to me, with her sister's handwriting still on the back. I held it out to her.

"Anywhere you like," I said. "You've got all the time in the world."

She looked at the envelope. She didn't take it. She looked at the room, at two hundred faces from the mill, and not one of them was looking back at her the way she wanted.

Then, two tables over, Pam Ritter stood up, opened her purse, took out a fifty-dollar bill and laid it flat on the table in front of the empty chair where Bernie had planned to sit.

"He didn't cry, Bernie," Pam said, clear enough for the whole hall. "You won."
