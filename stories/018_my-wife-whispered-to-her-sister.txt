"I'm reading fake vows. The real ones are for Marcus next month."

My wife whispered it to her sister at the altar of First Methodist in Jonesboro, Arkansas, at our thirtieth anniversary vow renewal, with two hundred people in the pews behind us and the pastor still flipping through his notes. She said it with her back half-turned to me and her lips barely moving. She didn't know I was close enough to hear it.

She also didn't know that ten inches from my left hand sat the little sound board for the chapel, the one I'd run every Sunday for eleven years before the church hired a kid to do it.

Thirty years of marriage. A ten-thousand-dollar party she'd planned down to the napkins. And she'd just told her sister the vows she was about to say to me were a rehearsal for another man.

I didn't say a word. I reached over, found the fader marked Pulpit, and slid it all the way up.

The pulpit mic was eight inches from my wife's mouth. Her sister Lorene, standing right next to her in a lavender dress with a bouquet of white roses, leaned in and whispered back.

"Does Marcus know you're doing this whole thing just to get Ernie to sign the building over?"

Except it wasn't a whisper anymore. It came out of every speaker in that chapel, big and warm, like the voice of God with a south Arkansas accent.

And my wife, who hadn't noticed a thing, answered her.

"Marcus is the one who said to do it. Get the deed signed at the reception, file in three weeks, and then it's his and mine. Thirty years of that man, Lorene. I've earned a building."

Two hundred people heard it. Every word. You could hear somebody's purse hit the floor in the fourth row.

Seven seconds later, my wife figured out why the whole chapel had gone dead quiet. I watched her turn and look at the pulpit mic, then at me, then at my hand still resting on the fader, and her mouth opened, and nothing came out.

What she didn't know was that up in the balcony, above the choir loft, a little red light was glowing on a camera that had been streaming the whole thing live to the internet, and she wasn't the one who'd asked for that camera.

My name is Ernest Cobb. I'm fifty-six. I've owned Cobb Family Pharmacy on Main Street in Jonesboro for twenty-two years, and the building it sits in, which my daddy bought in 1979 for a hundred and forty thousand dollars. A pharmacist learns one thing above all others: you never sign for something you haven't counted. Remember that.

"Ernie," Darcy said. Her voice came through the speakers too, because I'd left the fader up. "Ernie, that was a joke. Lorene and I, we have this joke."

"I heard the joke," I said.

Pastor Gene Wilkes stood there with his mouth open and his notes in his hand. In the front row my mother-in-law Arlene, eighty-one, said "Lord Jesus" loud enough that the mic picked her up too.

Darcy turned to the pews. She smiled, the big smile she uses on the ladies at her Pilates class.

"Y'all," she said into the mic. "It's a bit. It's from a movie."

Nobody laughed. A man in the third row, my fishing buddy Clay Hester, actually said "What movie?" out loud.

On the little table next to the altar, under the unity candle, was a cream-colored folder tied with a gold ribbon. Darcy had put it there herself that morning. She'd told me it was my anniversary gift to her, a surprise, something we'd sign together at the reception in front of everybody. I'd thought it was a card.

I picked up the folder, slid off the ribbon and opened it.

It was a quitclaim deed. Cobb Family Pharmacy building, 402 South Main Street. Grantor, Ernest L. Cobb. Grantee, Darcy Ann Cobb. Not Ernest and Darcy. Just Darcy.

There was a yellow sticky arrow pointing to the signature line. In her handwriting it said, Sign here, baby!

I closed the folder.

"Ernie, don't," Darcy said.

I tucked it under my arm. Then I slid the fader back down to zero, very gently, the way you're supposed to so it doesn't pop, and I leaned into the pastor's handheld mic instead.

"Folks," I said, "thank y'all for coming. There's brisket in the fellowship hall. I won't be needing any."

I walked down the center aisle. Two hundred faces turned like sunflowers following me. I didn't run. I didn't slam the door. I went out into the March sunshine, got into my truck, and set the folder on the seat beside me.

My phone buzzed before I'd even gotten the key in. It was a text from a number I didn't have saved.

"Don't sign anything. The camera was me. You'll understand soon. —A friend."

I drove straight to my sister's.

Frankie Cobb is three years older than me and has practiced divorce law in Little Rock for thirty-one years. She'd driven up that morning for the renewal, and she'd left the reception before the brisket, too. She was standing in her hotel room at the Hilton Garden Inn with her heels kicked off and her phone already in her hand when I knocked.

"Twenty-four hundred views," she said, before hello. "The church posted it on their Facebook page. Live. The whole thing. The fader, the whisper, the building, Marcus. Some woman in the comments has already clipped the good part and put music under it."

"What music?"

"Taylor Swift. I don't know which one."

I sat down on the edge of the bed and handed her the folder. She read the deed the way she reads everything, with her lips pressed together and one eyebrow higher than the other.

"Grantee, Darcy Ann Cobb," she said. "Sole. Notarized on a blank signature line. Ernie, who notarized a blank deed?"

"I don't know."

"Find out. That's a notary with a problem." She turned the page. "This would have given her the building outright. Not half. All. You'd have handed her a one-point-one-million-dollar building as an anniversary gift in front of two hundred witnesses, and then she'd have filed for divorce and argued in court that a gift to a spouse is her separate property."

"Could she win that?"

"In Arkansas? She might." Frankie set the folder down. "Okay. Here's where you are. Arkansas still has fault grounds. Adultery is one of them, and so is general indignities, which is lawyer for she made your life miserable. But fault alone doesn't hand you the house. The building is yours from before the marriage, from your daddy's estate. As long as you never put her name on it, it stays yours."

"I never put her name on it."

"Then all she's got is what you just carried out of a church." She looked up at me. "Ernie, did you sign anything else? Anything? Ever? A loan, a line of credit, a form at the bank?"

"No."

"You sure?"

"I'm a pharmacist, Frankie. I don't sign for anything I haven't counted."

She almost smiled. "Daddy used to say that."

"Daddy said it to me first."

My phone buzzed again. Same unknown number. This time it was a video. Thirty seconds, shot from somewhere low and close, near the front of the chapel, not from the balcony camera. It started before the ceremony. You could see the side of my wife's face, very close, and hear her clear as anything, talking to somebody off camera in the hallway behind the sanctuary.

"Once he signs it, Marcus can put it on the papers for the investors. The closing's on the twentieth. He just needs it in my name for the twentieth, Lorene. That's all. Then we're done with Ernie."

The video stopped. Under it was one more line.

"There's more where that came from. Don't let her near the building."

Frankie read it over my shoulder.

"Who is that?" she said.

"I don't know."

"It's somebody standing next to your wife before the ceremony."

"There were fifteen people next to my wife before the ceremony."

"Then one of those fifteen people," Frankie said, "hates her more than you do."

Darcy came home Sunday a little after noon. I was at the kitchen table with a cup of coffee and the Sunday paper, which I wasn't reading. She'd spent the night at her mother's. She came in still wearing the lavender sweater from the reception she never went to, and she put her purse on the counter very carefully, like it might wake somebody.

"Can we talk like adults?" she said.

"Sure."

She sat down across from me. She'd been crying, or she'd done a very good job of looking like it.

"It was a joke," she said. "Lorene and I were being stupid. We'd had mimosas in the bride's room. You know how she gets. She says something outrageous, I say something more outrageous. It's a thing we do."

"Who's Marcus?"

"Marcus is nobody. Marcus is Marcus Teague. He does my IRA. You've met him. At the Chamber thing."

"The financial planner."

"Yes. Ernie, he's married. He's got grown kids. It was a joke about a man nobody would ever look at twice."

"You said you were reading fake vows."

"I said a lot of dumb things after two mimosas."

"You said the real ones were for him next month."

She reached across the table and put her hand over mine. Her hand was cold.

"Thirty years," she said. "You're going to throw away thirty years over something you heard through a speaker?"

"I didn't throw anything. I turned up a fader."

She took her hand back. "You humiliated me in front of my entire family and our whole church. My mother had to be helped to the car."

"Your mother said Lord Jesus into a live microphone. She'll be fine."

"You think this is funny?"

"No, Darcy. I think it's on the internet."

Her face changed. Just a flicker. "How many?"

"Last I checked, eleven thousand."

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, the crying was gone and something sharper was sitting there instead.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. I'll fix that. Where's the folder?"

"What folder?"

"The anniversary folder, Ernie. From the table. With the ribbon. It's mine. It was my gift."

"It's a deed to my building."

"It's a deed you were going to sign. You promised me something special for thirty years." She leaned forward. "Did you sign it? Last night? Did you sign it at Frankie's?"

There it was. The only question she'd actually come home to ask. Not are we okay. Not do you still love me. Did you sign it.

I took a long sip of coffee.

"Okay," I said.

"Okay what? Okay you signed it?"

"Okay, I heard you."

She stared at me. I stared back. I've counted pills for thirty-four years. I can look at a woman for a long time without blinking.

"You're impossible," she said. She grabbed her purse off the counter. "I'm going to Mama's. Don't call me. You'll hear from a lawyer."

"I'll be here."

The door slammed hard enough that a magnet fell off the fridge. It was the one from Gulf Shores, 2009, the year we took the kids. I picked it up and put it back.

Then I went out to the garage and wrote down everything she'd said, word for word, with the date and the time, on a legal pad. My sister had told me to.

At a quarter to seven on Monday morning, before the pharmacy opened, there was a woman on my front porch.

She was about fifty, in hospital scrubs with a cardigan over them, and she was holding a white envelope with both hands like it was a casserole. I'd never seen her before in my life.

"Mr. Cobb?" she said. "I'm Denise Teague. Marcus's wife."

I stepped out onto the porch and pulled the door shut behind me.

"Ma'am."

"I'm a nurse at St. Bernards," she said. "Your address is in the old First Methodist directory. Marcus and I went there till 2015. I've got a shift at seven. I don't have long." She held out the envelope. "My sister-in-law sent me the video from your church Saturday night. I watched it nine times. Then I went into my husband's office at home and I found this in his desk."

Inside was a printout. It was a letter on Teague Wealth Partners letterhead, addressed to four names I didn't know, about an investment called Main Street Medical Properties. It said the fund had "secured" a commercial building at 402 South Main in Jonesboro, "free and clear, appraised at one point one million dollars," which would serve as collateral for a loan of eight hundred thousand. The closing date was the twentieth of April.

"That's my building," I said.

"I know." Denise's chin was trembling, but her voice wasn't. "Marcus has been losing money for two years, Mr. Cobb. Bad bets, a land deal in Branson. He's been paying old clients with new clients' money. Our house is mortgaged twice. If he doesn't close on the twentieth, those four people are going to start asking where their three hundred thousand dollars went." She swallowed. "And one of those four people is your sister-in-law."

"Lorene?"

"Lorene Pruett. Ninety-two thousand dollars. Her whole retirement from the school district. He told her it was safe as a church." Denise laughed, a little bitter. "Then he made it a church."

I stood on my porch in my reading glasses, holding a letter that said my daddy's building belonged to a man I'd met once at a Chamber of Commerce mixer.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked.

"Because I've been married to him twenty-six years," she said, "and I'd like to be the one who finishes it. Not the police. Not the investors. Me." She looked at her watch. "I filed this morning at eight. Well, I'm going to file at eight. My lawyer's waiting on me after shift."

"Mrs. Teague, was it you? The camera? The text from the unknown number?"

She shook her head. "I wish it had been. I didn't even know about the vow renewal until the video."

She went down the steps to her car, a little Honda with a St. Bernards parking sticker, and then she turned around.

"Mr. Cobb. Whatever they put in front of you before the twentieth, don't sign it. They need it more than you know."

That afternoon, I found out what Darcy had meant by fixing it.

My sister called me at the pharmacy between two prescriptions.

"Go look at Darcy's Facebook," she said.

I didn't have Facebook. My tech, Rosa Ibarra, did. Rosa is twenty-six, wears her hair in a big claw clip and has been with me five years. She pulled it up on her phone behind the counter and turned it around.

Darcy had posted a video of her own. She was sitting in her mother's living room in front of the china cabinet, in the lavender sweater, with her mascara running just a little.

"I need to say something about the video that's going around," she said into the camera. "What you heard on Saturday was a private, joking conversation between two sisters. It was taken out of context and broadcast without my consent by my husband, who has been controlling and cruel in this marriage for a very long time. I'm not going to say more. My attorney will handle it. Please pray for me."

It had four hundred comments. About half of them were praying for her.

"Controlling and cruel," Rosa said. "Mr. Cobb, you brought me chicken soup when I had the flu."

"I bring everybody chicken soup when they have the flu."

"That's my point."

The letter from Darcy's lawyer came by certified mail on Wednesday. Clint Ostrander, of Ostrander and Blythe. I'd heard of him. Everybody in northeast Arkansas who'd ever been divorced had heard of him. Clint Ostrander was the man your wife hired when she wanted to leave you in a rented duplex with a card table and a microwave.

The letter said my wife was seeking a divorce on grounds of general indignities. It said I'd engaged in "a pattern of emotional cruelty culminating in a public broadcast designed to humiliate." It demanded temporary support of five thousand dollars a month, exclusive use of the marital home, and "an equitable interest in all business assets including Cobb Family Pharmacy and its real property."

At the bottom, it proposed a meeting. "In the interest of a dignified resolution." Friday of next week. Ostrander's office.

Frankie read it on speakerphone and snorted.

"Dignified," she said. "Clint Ostrander hasn't been dignified since the Clinton administration. We'll go. Bring your legal pad. Wear the tie Mama gave you."

"Why that tie?"

"Because Clint went to high school with Mama's cousin and it'll throw him off."

Clint Ostrander's office was on the fourth floor of a bank building downtown, with a big window looking over the railroad tracks and a stuffed largemouth bass on the wall that he'd almost certainly bought. He was sixty-five, tall, silver hair combed back, and he shook my hand like he was checking it for weapons.

Darcy sat at the end of the conference table in a gray suit I'd paid for. She didn't look at me. She looked at a spot on the wall about a foot to the left of my head.

"Ernest," Clint said warmly. "Frances. Thank y'all for coming. I think we all want the same thing here, and that's to get this behind us without a lot of ugliness in a courtroom. Darcy's prepared to be very generous."

"Darcy's always been generous," Frankie said. "With other people's buildings."

Clint smiled with his teeth only. "Let's keep it friendly, Frances."

"I am being friendly, Clint. You should see me when I'm not."

He slid a stapled document across the table. Settlement Agreement, it said on the front. Twenty-two pages.

"The highlights," Clint said. "Darcy waives temporary support. She waives alimony. She waives any claim to your retirement accounts. In exchange, she keeps the house, which is only fair since she's lived in it twenty years, and she keeps her car. And the two of you split the business assets in a simple, clean transfer. Page nineteen. It's all very standard."

"She waives alimony," I said.

"She does."

"Darcy," I said. "You're waiving five thousand a month."

She finally looked at me. "I don't want your money, Ernie. I just want to be done."

Frankie was already on page nineteen. She read for a while in silence. Then she turned the agreement around and laid it in front of me and tapped one paragraph with her pen.

"Exhibit C," she said. "Read it slow."

Exhibit C was three pages long. Buried in the second page, in the same tiny type as everything else, it said that "as part of the equitable division of business assets," I would execute a quitclaim deed conveying the real property at 402 South Main Street to Darcy Ann Cobb, to be "delivered at signing and recorded no later than April 19."

April nineteenth. One day before Marcus Teague's closing.

"A simple, clean transfer," I said.

"It's half the business," Clint said smoothly. "She gets the building, you keep the pharmacy. You'd be her tenant. Fair rent, long lease. Very standard."

"I'd pay rent to my wife," I said, "on a building my daddy bought in 1979."

"To your ex-wife. Respectfully."

"What happens if I don't sign?"

Clint folded his hands. "Then we go to court, Ernest. And we talk about the video. We talk about how a man secretly turned on a microphone to humiliate his wife in a house of God. We talk about the pattern. Judges don't love that."

"And Darcy wants five thousand a month again."

"And Darcy wants a great deal more than five thousand a month again."

I looked at my wife. She was looking at the agreement, not at me, and her fingers were drumming on the table, fast, the way they do when she's running late for something.

The twentieth was in sixteen days.

"Darcy," I said. "Why April nineteenth?"

Her fingers stopped.

"It's just a date," Clint said.

"I asked her."

"It's just a date, Ernie," she said. "So we can all move on before summer."

"Before summer," I said. "Okay."

I picked up the pen Clint had laid on top of the agreement. It was a nice pen, heavy, with Ostrander and Blythe on the barrel in gold. Darcy's eyes went to it and stayed there. I clicked it once.

Then I put it down on top of the agreement, unsigned, and slid the whole stack back across the table.

"Okay," I said again. "I'll think about it."

"Ernest—" Clint started.

"You heard him," Frankie said, standing up and getting her purse. "He'll think about it. He's a pharmacist, Clint. He counts. It takes a while."

We were at the elevator when Clint caught up to us. He wasn't smiling at all now.

"Frances," he said, low, "you should know my client has filed a complaint with the Arkansas State Board of Pharmacy. It went in this morning. She has concerns about controlled substances at your brother's store. About inventory that doesn't add up. She saw things over the years. Things she's prepared to testify to."

The elevator dinged. The doors opened. I didn't get in.

"What things?" I said.

"Oxycodone," Clint said. "Hydrocodone. Missing counts. A husband who brings pills home." He shrugged. "I'm sure it's nothing. But the Board takes these things very seriously. They can suspend a license during an investigation. They can close a pharmacy." He looked at me with something almost like pity. "It's amazing how fast a building becomes less important when you can't run a business in it."

The elevator doors closed on their own. Frankie and I stood there looking at our reflections in the brass.

"She just accused you of stealing opioids," Frankie said quietly.

"Yep."

"Ernie, that can take your license."

"Only if the count's wrong."

"Is the count wrong?"

"Frankie," I said, "I've been counting since I was twenty-two."

The inspector from the Board of Pharmacy came on a Tuesday, eight days later, unannounced, which is how they always come. Her name was Marla Givens and she'd been doing it nineteen years. She showed me her badge at the counter in front of three customers and Mrs. Pinkley from the Baptist church, who was there for her blood pressure pills and was not going anywhere until she saw how this turned out.

"Mr. Cobb, I've got a complaint alleging diversion of Schedule II narcotics," she said. "I'll need your perpetual inventory, your DEA 222 forms for the last two years, your biennial inventory, and access to your vault."

"Rosa," I said, "get Mrs. Givens a chair and a cup of coffee. And the binders. All of them."

"All of them?" Rosa said.

"All twelve."

Marla Givens worked for six hours. She counted every bottle of every Schedule II drug in my vault by hand, tablet by tablet, on a counting tray, while Rosa and I filled prescriptions around her. She checked them against my perpetual log, which I keep in two places, on the computer and in a green ledger in my own handwriting, because my daddy did, and his daddy did. She pulled invoices from my wholesaler and matched them against what came in the door.

At four-thirty she put down her pen.

"Mr. Cobb," she said, "I've inspected two hundred pharmacies. I've never seen a count this clean. You're off by zero. On every line. For two years." She shook her head. "Who keeps a handwritten ledger anymore?"

"Men whose wives file complaints," I said.

She almost laughed. Then she pulled a page out of her folder and read it to herself, and her face went thoughtful.

"You understand I can't tell you who filed this," she said.

"I understand."

"But I can tell you that a complaint found to be unsubstantiated goes in the file with a note. And that a complainant who swore to specific facts on a notarized form, and whose facts don't match a perfect count, has made a false statement to a state agency." She closed the folder. "That's a misdemeanor in Arkansas. I'll be noting it."

"Who notarized it?" I asked.

She hesitated. Then she turned the folder around just far enough for me to see the stamp at the bottom of the complaint.

Notary Public, State of Arkansas. Marcus D. Teague.

The same notary, I'd find out the next day, who'd stamped a blank signature line on a quitclaim deed in a cream-colored folder with a gold ribbon.

Lorene was waiting in the parking lot behind the pharmacy when I locked up that night. She was leaning against her little blue Buick with her arms crossed, in a school district windbreaker, and she looked like she hadn't slept in a week.

"I hear you passed," she said.

"News travels."

"Mrs. Pinkley's in my Sunday school class."

I stood there with my keys in my hand. Lorene is three years younger than Darcy. She taught third grade for thirty years and has the voice for it. We'd always gotten along, in the way a man gets along with his wife's sister, which is to say from across the room at Thanksgiving.

"It was you," I said. "The camera."

She nodded.

"The livestream too?"

"I told the church Mama wanted to watch from home. Mama's never watched a livestream in her life. She thinks Facebook is a magazine." Lorene took a breath. "And I asked to stand next to Darcy at the pulpit. Not on the other side, where the bridesmaids go. Right next to the mic. I knew you ran that sound board for eleven years. I knew you'd be standing right by it. And I knew if I got her talking, she'd talk."

"You set her up."

"I gave her a chance to tell the truth in church," Lorene said. "She took it."

"Why?"

She looked at the ground. When she looked back up, her eyes were wet.

"Ninety-two thousand dollars," she said. "My whole retirement. Marcus Teague put it in his fund two years ago, because my own sister told me he was a genius. Then last fall I asked for some of it back for a new roof, and it took him four months to send me eleven thousand. And at Christmas, Darcy got drunk on eggnog and told me not to worry. She said Marcus had a plan to fix everything, and the plan was your building, and once he closed on it, I'd get my money and she'd get Marcus." She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand. "She was going to steal from you to pay back what her boyfriend stole from me. And she thought I'd be grateful."

"So you started recording."

"So I bought a little recorder at Walmart for thirty-nine dollars and hid it in my bouquet." She reached into her windbreaker and handed me a thumb drive. "That's everything. Twenty-two minutes from the bride's room before the ceremony. She explains the whole thing to me like I'm one of my third graders. The deed, the notary, the closing on the twentieth, the investors. She even says the part about Marcus's wife being too dumb to notice."

I turned the thumb drive over in my fingers.

"Denise Teague noticed," I said.

"I know. Denise called me Thursday. We had lunch." Lorene almost smiled. "We're starting a club."

"Lorene, Clint Ostrander is going to say you're a bitter sister with an axe to grind."

"I am a bitter sister with an axe to grind," she said. "That doesn't make the recording any less true." She opened her car door. "The hearing's on the sixteenth, isn't it? The temporary one?"

"How do you know that?"

"Because she called me last night and asked me to come testify that you've been cruel to her for thirty years." Lorene got in the car. "I told her I'd be there."

The Craighead County Courthouse in Jonesboro is a big old limestone building with a clock that runs four minutes slow. On the morning of April sixteenth, the hallway outside Courtroom B was fuller than I'd ever seen it for a temporary hearing. Half of First Methodist had found reasons to be there. Clay Hester was there in a clean shirt. Mrs. Pinkley was there with her purse on her lap and a Tupperware of lemon bars, in case it went long.

Marcus Teague was there too.

It was the first time I'd seen him since the Chamber of Commerce mixer. Fifty-two, good haircut, navy blazer, a Rolex he'd probably bought with somebody's school-district retirement. He sat on the bench beside Darcy like a man waiting for a table at a restaurant, scrolling on his phone. When I walked past, he looked up at me and nodded, polite, like we were neighbors.

"Ernest," he said.

"Marcus."

"No hard feelings, I hope. These things happen."

"They do," I said. "Mostly on the twentieth."

His thumb stopped on his phone screen.

Inside, Judge Nolan Hartsell took the bench at nine-oh-six by the slow clock. He was seventy and had a hearing aid in one ear and a reputation for not liking lawyers who talked longer than his coffee stayed hot.

Clint Ostrander went first. He talked about thirty years of marriage. He talked about a vindictive husband who'd turned a holy moment into a public humiliation. He showed a still frame from the livestream, my hand on the fader, blown up on poster board. He talked about a Board of Pharmacy complaint "still under review." Then he asked for temporary support of six thousand a month, exclusive use of the house and an order "restraining Mr. Cobb from encumbering, selling or transferring the real property at 402 South Main pending final division."

"He wants you frozen," Frankie whispered to me. "So you can't move the building before the twentieth. So it's still sitting there when Marcus needs it."

"Can he get that?"

"Watch."

Clint called Darcy. She was very good on the stand. She cried once, right on time. She said I'd been cold for years. She said she'd been afraid of what I'd do with the business. She said the conversation in the chapel was a private joke between sisters, maliciously broadcast.

Then Clint said, "Your Honor, Mrs. Cobb's sister is here today and prepared to confirm that this was a joke between two siblings, and that Mr. Cobb has been emotionally cruel for decades. We call Lorene Pruett."

Darcy turned in her seat and smiled at her sister. A real smile. Grateful.

Lorene walked up to the stand in her Sunday dress, raised her right hand and swore to tell the truth.

"Ms. Pruett," Clint said, "the conversation the court heard on that video. Was it a joke?"

"No, sir."

Clint actually took a half step back. Darcy's smile froze on her face.

"I'm sorry?" Clint said.

"It wasn't a joke. My sister meant every word." Lorene folded her hands on the rail. "And I can prove it, because I recorded the twenty-two minutes before it, in the bride's room."

"Objection," Clint said. "Your Honor, I wasn't—"

"You called her, Counselor," the judge said. "She's your witness. Sit down."

Frankie stood up with the thumb drive in one hand and a laptop under her arm.

"Your Honor, the recording was made in Arkansas, which is a one-party consent state, by a party to the conversation. I've provided a copy to opposing counsel. It runs twenty-two minutes. I'll play four of them."

The judge waved a hand. "Four. I'm timing you."

She pressed play.

My wife's voice filled that courtroom, clear as the chapel speakers, laughing.

"Once Ernie signs it at the reception, Marcus records it Monday and puts it on the papers for the investors. The closing's the twentieth. Eight hundred thousand. Marcus pays back the ones who are screaming, including you, Lorene, so stop worrying, and then I file. Clint Ostrander says a gift to a wife is hers. He says Ernie won't even know what hit him. He thinks I love him, Lorene. Thirty years and he still thinks I love him."

Then Lorene's voice on the tape. "And if he won't sign?"

"Then Marcus has a friend at the pharmacy board," Darcy said. "One complaint and Ernie's license is under review, and nobody wants to rent a building from a pharmacist who sells pills out the back door. He'll sign. He signs everything I put in front of him. He's a sweetheart."

Frankie stopped it there.

Nobody in that courtroom moved. Mrs. Pinkley set her lemon bars down on the bench very slowly.

"Your Honor," Frankie said, "I'd also like to hand up two documents. The first is a letter from the State Board of Pharmacy, dated last Wednesday, finding Mrs. Cobb's complaint unsubstantiated. My brother's Schedule II count was off by zero. Over two years. The Board has referred the false statement for review. The notary on that complaint is Marcus D. Teague, who is sitting in the second row."

Every head in the courtroom turned to the second row. Marcus sat very still with his phone face down on his knee.

"And the second document," Frankie said, "is the deed my client's wife left on the altar for him to sign as an anniversary present. Also notarized by Mr. Teague. Notarized blank. On a signature line with nothing on it."

She held it up. The cream-colored folder. The gold ribbon. The yellow sticky arrow in Darcy's handwriting. Sign here, baby.

The signature line was still empty.

Darcy stood up at the counsel table. Clint grabbed her arm and she shook it off.

"But he signed it," she said. "Ernie, you said okay. At the house. You said okay. Marcus already sent it to the bank, he—"

She stopped. It was too late. It was already in the room.

The judge took off his glasses and set them down very carefully on the bench.

"Mrs. Cobb," he said. "Sit down. You are still under oath, and you are not helping your lawyer." He looked at the second row. "Mr. Teague, are you a commissioned notary in the State of Arkansas?"

Marcus stood up halfway. "Your Honor, I—"

"That's a yes or no."

"Yes, sir."

"Not for long, I imagine." The judge put his glasses back on. "Here's my ruling on the temporary motions. Motion for temporary support, denied. Motion for exclusive use of the marital home, denied. The parties will share it, or Mrs. Cobb may live elsewhere, as she seems to have options. Motion to restrain Mr. Cobb from transferring the property at 402 South Main, denied, because the court can find no evidence it was ever anybody's but his." He shuffled the papers. "And I'm referring this transcript, and that recording, to the prosecuting attorney for Craighead County. And I'm sending a copy to the Arkansas Securities Department, because I'd like somebody who understands investment funds to take a close look at Main Street Medical Properties. Before the twentieth, preferably."

Out in the gallery, someone stood up fast. It was Denise Teague, in her scrubs, straight off a night shift. She walked down the aisle to the second row and set a large brown envelope on the bench next to her husband.

"That's from my lawyer," she said. "You've been served, Marcus. And the investors have my number now. All four of them."

Marcus looked at the envelope. Then he looked at Darcy. Darcy was looking at him, and for the first time since the chapel, she looked exactly like what she was, a woman who'd bet a building she didn't own on a man who didn't have one either.

"You said he'd sign," Marcus said to her. Not quiet. Loud enough for the whole room. "You promised me he'd sign. Eight hundred thousand dollars, Darcy."

"Don't you dare put this on me."

"Who else would I put it on?"

"Order," the judge said, banging the gavel once. "Take it to the hallway. Or better yet, take it to your separate lawyers."

We were adjourned at nine-forty-one by the slow clock.

In the hallway, Darcy caught me by the drinking fountain. Her mascara was running for real this time. Clint Ostrander was nowhere to be seen. Marcus was down by the elevators talking very fast into his phone with his back to everybody.

"Ernie," she said. "Ernie, please. Thirty years. We can still fix this. I'll tell them it was all Marcus. I'll tell them anything you want. Just don't let them take me in front of all these people."

I looked at her for a long time. I didn't blink.

"You told your sister I sign everything you put in front of me," I said.

"I know. I know I did."

"I never signed anything I hadn't counted, Darcy. I counted you."

I handed her the cream-colored folder with the gold ribbon. She took it without thinking, the way you take something a man hands you.

"Happy anniversary," I said.

Then I walked down the limestone steps of the Craighead County Courthouse into the April sunshine, with my sister on one side of me and my sister-in-law on the other, and behind me I heard Mrs. Pinkley say, to nobody in particular, "Well. Lemon bar, anyone?"
