"You're a disgrace, Frank. Don't embarrass us tonight."

My wife said it in our front hall at five o'clock on a Saturday, with her sister Renita holding the car keys and our daughter Celina halfway down the stairs in a green dress. Three people heard it. In two hours there would be two hundred and forty more people in one room, and Pamela wanted to make sure I understood my place before any of them saw me.

She held out an index card. I took it.

It was written in her neat teacher handwriting, the kind she'd used on report cards for twenty-two years. Number one, don't talk about plumbing. Number two, stand near the bar, not near Daddy's table. Number three, if anyone asks what you do, say "supply business" and change the subject. Number four, no stories about the Army.

"Thirty-one years," I said. "And this is the first card you ever wrote me."

"Don't be cute."

I folded the card once and put it in my inside jacket pocket. Then I reached into the little dish on the hall table, took out a tarnished brass pin I hadn't worn in years, and fixed it to my lapel.

Pamela stared at it. "What is that?"

"A pin."

"Take it off. It looks like something from a cereal box."

I didn't take it off. I picked up my truck keys and opened the front door for her, like I had every time for thirty-one years.

Two hours later, the man who built half the steel buildings in western Arkansas would cross a ballroom in front of two hundred and forty people, take my hand in both of his, and call me sir. And my wife would set her champagne glass down so hard it would crack at the stem.

What she didn't know was what else was in that inside pocket, folded right behind her rules card. A loan commitment letter, signed and dated, for three million six hundred thousand dollars.

My name is Franklin Rios. I'm fifty-eight years old, and I sell pipe, fittings, water heaters and valves out of a counter on Towson Avenue in Fort Smith, Arkansas. That's what my wife's family thinks I do, anyway, and for most of my marriage I let them think it.

Thirty years at a supply counter teaches you one thing above everything else. You learn who pays on time and who just talks like they do. Hold on to that.

Renita cleared her throat behind me. "Pam, your ride's here."

I looked out past the porch. A silver Lexus was idling at the curb, and a man in a gray suit and sunglasses was leaning on the driver's door like he owned the street.

"I'm riding with Nolan," Pamela said. "He has to set up the slideshow for Daddy. You can bring Celina."

"Nolan," I said.

"Nolan Hendrix. Daddy's business broker. You've met him."

I had met him. Twice. Both times he'd shaken my hand without taking his sunglasses off, which tells you most of what you need to know about a man.

"Sure," I said. "Drive safe."

She walked out without looking back. Nolan opened the passenger door for her, and his hand rested on the small of her back while she got in. It stayed there one second longer than it needed to.

Celina came down the last three stairs and stood next to me.

"Dad," she said quietly. "Did you see that?"

"I saw it."

"What are you going to do?"

I patted the pocket with the card in it. "Follow the rules, honey. Every single one."

The Hardscrabble Country Club had pulled out every white tablecloth it owned for Lyman Hammack. He'd founded Hammack Steel Fabrication in 1979 with one welding truck and a loan from his brother-in-law, and over forty-five years he'd built it into the outfit that put up barns, gyms, church halls and warehouses from Van Buren to Poteau. Tonight he was retiring at seventy-one, and the whole town had come out to clap.

There was a banner over the stage. There was a slideshow. There was a George Strait cover band warming up in the corner, and a bar with a line twelve deep.

I stood near the bar. Rule number two.

Celina stood with me for a while, then drifted off to find her cousins. I nursed a club soda and watched my wife work the room. She was good at it. Pamela had always been good at rooms. She touched elbows. She laughed with her head back. She introduced Nolan Hendrix to every banker and every county commissioner in sight, and every time, Nolan took a business card and slid it into his breast pocket like he was collecting poker chips.

Then she saw a couple from the Methodist church heading my way and cut them off at the pass.

"Oh, that's just Frank," I heard her say. "He works a counter over on Towson. He's not much for crowds."

The wife from church gave me a little sympathetic wave. I waved back.

Renita slid up next to me with a glass of white wine. She was four years younger than Pamela and about four times as honest, which had never made her popular at family dinners.

"She's been introducing you like the help all night," she said.

"I noticed."

"You're taking it pretty well."

"I've got a card," I said, and tapped my pocket. "Rules."

Renita looked at me sideways, like she was trying to decide something. Then a murmur went through the room, and every head turned toward the main doors.

Truman Beal had arrived.

If Lyman Hammack was the founder of Hammack Steel, Truman Beal was the reason it was still standing. He'd bought into the company as a fifty-fifty partner in 1994, and he ran the shop floor while Lyman ran the handshakes. He was sixty-eight years old, built like a fire hydrant, and he hadn't missed a day of work since the Clinton administration. Half the men in that ballroom had learned to weld under him.

He shook a few hands near the door. Then he looked across the room, and he stopped.

He started walking. Not toward Lyman. Not toward the stage. Straight through the crowd, past the commissioners and the bankers and the slideshow screen, all the way to the bar, until he was standing in front of me.

The band had stopped between songs. It got quiet in a way that a room of two hundred and forty people almost never does.

Truman took my hand in both of his.

"Good to see you, sir," he said.

You know that feeling when a whole room realizes at the same time that it had something wrong? Yeah. It sounds like nothing. Nobody said a word. I heard a fork hit a plate somewhere in the back.

Over by the slideshow, Pamela set her champagne glass down on a table so hard that the stem cracked. Nolan Hendrix took his sunglasses off for the first time all night.

"Truman," I said. "You didn't have to do that."

"Yes, I did." His voice was gravelly and loud enough to carry. "I've been wanting to do it in front of people for thirty-four years."

"Not tonight. It's Lyman's night."

He held my hand a second longer. Then he looked at the brass pin on my lapel, and his jaw worked, and he nodded.

"It's Lyman's night," he agreed. Then he raised his voice a notch, so the people around us could hear him fine. "But I'll say this much. This man is the reason there's a party tonight. And that's all I'll say."

He clapped me on the shoulder and walked off toward the head table.

The room started breathing again. People started talking, too loud and too fast, the way they do. Renita was staring at me with her wine glass halfway to her mouth.

"Frank," she said. "What the heck was that?"

"That was Truman being sentimental."

"Truman Beal is not sentimental. Truman Beal fired his own nephew for showing up four minutes late."

"Five minutes," I said. "And the nephew had it coming."

Pamela was crossing the room toward me. She was smiling for the guests, but she was walking like a woman who wanted to put her heel through a floorboard.

"What did you say to him?" she said, low.

"Hello."

"Don't play games with me. Truman Beal doesn't call anybody sir. He doesn't call my father sir."

"Maybe he should."

"Frank."

"You told me no Army stories," I said. "Rule number four. I'm following the rules."

She opened her mouth, and then Lyman tapped his microphone up on the stage, and two hundred and forty people turned to face him. Pamela had no choice but to turn too.

Lyman had a speech written on four sheets of yellow legal paper, and he went through every sheet. He thanked his late wife, Doris. He thanked his daughters. He thanked the welders and the drivers and the office girls, who were all past sixty and liked being called girls by Lyman and nobody else. Then he got to the business part.

"Most of you know," he said, "that on Monday morning, I'm signing my half of Hammack Steel over to my partner Truman. Truman's buying me out, fair and square, and the company stays right here in Fort Smith with every one of its eighty-one jobs."

The welders whooped. Truman lifted his glass.

"And I want to thank the lender who made that possible," Lyman said. "They asked me not to say their name tonight, so I won't. But they know who they are."

I kept my eyes on the stage. I could feel Renita looking at me.

At the slideshow table, Nolan Hendrix had his phone out and was typing fast.

I went looking for a men's room about ten minutes later. The hallway behind the ballroom was carpeted and quiet, and it bent around a corner by the coat check. I was almost at that corner when I heard Nolan's voice coming the other way.

"Calm down, Pam. Calm down. It changes nothing."

"He's hiding something from me. Truman Beal does not call a counter clerk sir."

"Who cares what Truman calls him? Listen to me. Monday is still Monday. Arkoma's offer is still on the table, and your dad still hasn't signed anything. If Truman's money gets shaky, your dad takes Arkoma's number, I get my fee, and you get your side letter."

"And Frank?"

"Frank signs your paper by Monday. Then you're free and clear, and the hundred and fifty is all yours. Your house, your money, your life."

Something rustled, like a jacket against a dress.

"Get his signature," Nolan said. "That's all you have to do."

I stepped back around the corner before they came past, and I stood there by the coat check with my hands in my pockets until their footsteps faded into the noise of the ballroom.

Then I took out the index card, turned it over, and wrote two words on the back with the little golf pencil from the coat check counter.

Side letter.

Sunday morning, I drove out to Chaffee Crossing before church let out.

Hammack Steel's main shop sits on twelve acres out where Fort Chaffee used to be, a long tan building with a gravel lot and a flagpole. Truman's truck was the only one in the lot. It always was, on Sundays.

He was in his office with a pot of coffee that could have stripped paint. He poured me a cup without asking.

"I'm sorry about last night," he said. "No, I'm not. I'm sorry I put you on the spot. I'm not sorry about what I said."

"You promised me thirty years ago you'd keep it quiet."

"I promised I'd keep the money quiet. I never promised to keep quiet about Danny."

He turned his coffee cup around on the desk. His son Danny had been nineteen years old in February of 1991, a private in my squad in Iraq, and I was a twenty-three-year-old staff sergeant who thought he knew everything. When an artillery round landed forty yards from our vehicle, Danny took shrapnel in both legs, and I carried him four hundred yards to the aid station. Danny lived. He runs a hardware store in Siloam Springs now, with two grandkids and a bad knee he blames on me.

Truman had never let it go. And when the steel business hit the floor in 2009 and the bank wouldn't renew his line of credit, it was my supply company that quietly loaned Hammack Steel enough to make payroll for eight months. That was the first loan. The one I had in my pocket last night was the second.

"Pamela doesn't know," Truman said. "About either loan."

"Nobody knows. Lyman doesn't even know who the lender is. Just that it's a company called Towson Holdings."

"And Towson Holdings is you."

"Towson Holdings is Rios Plumbing Supply," I said. "Five stores. Fort Smith, Van Buren, Alma, Greenwood and Poteau. Pamela thinks it's one counter."

Truman shook his head slowly. "Thirty-one years and she never asked."

"She asked once. In 1998. I told her the business did okay. She said that was nice and changed the subject to her sister's kitchen."

He laughed, one short bark. Then he got serious.

"Frank, I need to tell you something about that broker."

"Nolan Hendrix."

"He's been in Lyman's ear since March. He's got a buyer out of Tulsa, Arkoma Capital. They want Lyman's half, and then they want mine, and then they want to sell the land at Chaffee Crossing to a distribution warehouse and shut this shop down. I've seen their plan. Eighty-one jobs, gone in a year."

"And Lyman?"

"Lyman's loyal to me. But Lyman's seventy-one and tired, and Arkoma's number is four hundred thousand higher than mine. And Pamela's been in his ear right behind Nolan. Every Sunday dinner. 'Daddy, think about your retirement. Daddy, think about your grandkids.'"

"She's not thinking about the grandkids," I said.

I took out the index card and laid it on his desk, back side up. He read the two words.

"Side letter," he said. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning Arkoma pays somebody on the side if the deal goes their way. I heard Nolan say 'your side letter' to my wife. A hundred and fifty thousand of it."

Truman went red from the collar up, which on him takes a lot.

"That's a kickback."

"That's what we call it at the counter, too."

"What are you going to do?"

I picked up the card and put it back in my pocket.

"First thing," I said, "is I'm going to go home, and my wife is going to hand me a piece of paper. And I'm not going to sign it."

She handed it to me at the kitchen table that night, right after the dishes.

It was a manila folder with a yellow sticky note on the front that said SIGN BY MONDAY in her handwriting. Inside were nine pages with a law firm's name across the top.

"Daddy's lawyer needs this for the estate," Pamela said. "It's just a formality. With the sale going through, they need both spouses to sign off on what's separate and what's not."

"Okay," I said.

I opened the folder and started reading. She watched me read the first page and shifted in her chair.

"You don't have to read the whole thing, Frank."

"I read every invoice that comes across my counter. Thirty years. Never got burned once."

"It's not an invoice."

"No," I said, turning a page. "It's a postnuptial agreement."

She didn't say anything.

"It says I give up any claim to the house on Free Ferry Road. It says I give up any claim to any money you receive from your father, now or later. It says if we divorce in the next five years, I pay you four thousand dollars a month for six years." I turned another page. "It's got a nice blue border. Somebody paid good money for this."

"It's a formality."

"Pamela, who is Nolan Hendrix to you?"

She laughed, too fast. "He's Daddy's broker."

"He opened your car door last night."

"He's a gentleman. Something you might want to try."

"His hand was on your back."

"Oh, for heaven's sake, Frank. You're jealous of a business broker? Is that what this is?"

"I'm asking you a question."

"And I'm answering it. He's Daddy's broker. He's helping us with the sale. That's all." She tapped the folder. "Are you going to sign this or not?"

I closed the folder and slid it back across the table to her.

"Okay," I said.

"Okay you'll sign it?"

"Okay, I heard you."

She stared at me for a long moment. Then she picked up the folder and walked out of the kitchen, and a few seconds later I heard the bedroom door shut. Not slam. Pamela never slammed doors. She closed them so you'd hear the click.

I sat there and finished my coffee, and my phone buzzed on the table.

It was a text from Renita.

"We need to talk. Not at your house. Not at Daddy's. Ed's Diner on Garrison, 7 a.m. Come alone, Frank. It's about Pam."

Ed's Diner has been on Garrison Avenue since before I was born, and the booths still have the same red vinyl patched with the same red tape. Renita was already in the back booth when I got there at ten till seven, with two coffees and no food.

"Sit down," she said. "Before I lose my nerve."

I sat down.

"I love my sister," she said. "I want you to know that first. I've loved her since she used to braid my hair before church and pull it too tight on purpose. But I'm not going to watch her do this to Daddy."

"Do what?"

She took out her phone and slid it across the table.

It was a photo of a document, taken at an angle on what looked like Pamela's kitchen counter. Our kitchen counter. I recognized the crack in the tile. At the top it said Arkoma Capital Partners, Tulsa, Oklahoma, and below that, Consulting Agreement.

I read it twice. Arkoma agreed to pay Pamela Hammack Rios one hundred fifty thousand dollars for "family transition consulting services," payable within ten days of Arkoma's acquisition of Lyman Hammack's interest in Hammack Steel Fabrication. It was signed by somebody at Arkoma and by Pamela. Nolan Hendrix had signed as witness.

"Where'd you get this?" I asked.

"She sent it to me. Thursday night. With a text." Renita swiped to the next screen. "Read it."

The text from Pamela said: "Don't tell a soul. Daddy sells to Arkoma, I get this, and I'm out. Finally. Nolan says by Christmas we'll be in Destin."

"We," I said.

"We." Renita wrapped both hands around her coffee cup. "She thought I'd be happy for her. She's been telling me for two years she's miserable. That you're boring. That you're a counter guy with no ambition. And then last night Truman Beal walked across that ballroom, and I watched her face, and I thought, my sister has no idea who she's married to."

"She never asked."

"So I'm asking. Who are you, Frank?"

I told her. The Army, Danny Beal, the supply company, the five stores, the two loans. It took about ten minutes. Renita didn't interrupt once, which for a Hammack is some kind of record.

When I finished, she sat back in the booth.

"You financed Daddy's retirement," she said. "And Pam's trying to kill the deal so a company from Tulsa can pay her a kickback and shut the shop down."

"That's about the size of it."

"Does Daddy know you're the lender?"

"No. He knows the name Towson Holdings."

"You have to tell him."

"Not yet."

"Frank, he's going to sign Arkoma's paper if Nolan scares him enough. You know that."

"I know Nolan's going to try." I pushed the phone back to her. "Can you send me that photo and the text?"

"Already did." She picked up her phone. "What are you going to do with it?"

"Show it to a lawyer first," I said. "Then to the people who need to see it. In front of the people who need to hear it."

Sybil Kearney's office is on the third floor of an old bank building downtown, and she's been my company's lawyer for nineteen years. She's sixty-three, she wears reading glasses on a beaded chain, and she has a saying for everything, most of which she claims she got from her grandmother.

She read the photo on my phone and took her glasses off.

"Well," she said. "My grandmother used to say the cow always kicks the bucket she's standing next to."

"What does that mean?"

"It means your wife signed a contract with her own name on it. People never think the paper will come back around."

"Is it illegal?"

"For her? Probably not illegal. Grubby, but not illegal. For Nolan Hendrix, it's a different story. He's your father-in-law's broker. He owes Lyman a duty to get him the best deal and to disclose any conflict. Witnessing a secret side payment to the seller's daughter, from the buyer, on the buyer's side of the deal?" She tapped the phone. "That's the kind of thing that gets a broker sued into the ground, and reported to the state on top of it."

"And the divorce?"

"Is there going to be a divorce?"

I thought about the hand on the small of her back. About "we" and Destin by Christmas.

"Yes," I said.

"Then here's what you do. Don't touch her money. Don't empty anything. Move your own paycheck to your own account, which you're allowed to do. Rios Plumbing Supply was incorporated in 1993?"

"March of '93. We got married in June of '95."

"Then it's separate property under Arkansas law. She can argue about whatever it grew while you were married, and she'll get some of the marital portion of that, but she's not getting half the business. And you didn't sign that postnup, right?"

"I didn't sign anything."

"Good man." She put her glasses back on. "My grandmother also used to say, never sign anything in a kitchen."

My phone buzzed. It was Truman. I put it on speaker.

"Frank. Lyman just called me. He wants to push the closing from this morning to Wednesday."

"Why?"

"Because Nolan Hendrix was at his house at seven o'clock this morning with a letter from Arkoma's lawyers. The letter says Towson Holdings has a lien problem and can't fund. It's a lie, but Lyman doesn't know that, and he's scared."

Sybil raised her eyebrows at me across the desk.

"There's no lien problem," I said.

"I know that. You know that. Lyman doesn't know who Towson is, so he can't call and ask." Truman's voice dropped. "And there's more. Lyman's having the whole family over for supper tonight to 'talk it through.' Nolan's invited. Pamela set it up."

"Am I invited?"

"No. Pamela told Lyman you had to work late."

"I don't work late on Mondays."

"That's what I figured," Truman said. "Six o'clock. I'll be there. Lyman can't keep me out of a supper about my own company."

Lyman Hammack lives on a hill off Hendricks Boulevard in a brick house with white columns, the kind of house a man builds when he's made it in steel and wants the whole town to know. I parked my truck at the bottom of the driveway behind Nolan's silver Lexus a few minutes after six.

Pamela opened the front door before I could knock. She'd seen my truck from the window.

"What are you doing here?" she said through her teeth.

"Your sister invited me."

"Renita doesn't live here."

"Then I guess your father will have to decide," I said, and I stepped around her into the front hall.

They were all at the long dining room table. Lyman at the head. Truman on his right with his arms crossed. Renita and her husband, Ed. Celina, who looked up at me with surprise and something like relief. "Frank," Lyman said. He looked tired. "Pam said you were working."

"Pam was mistaken."

"Well. Sit down. There's pot roast."

"Not one time," he said.

Tuesday morning, a little after eight, my banking app pinged.

I keep a joint checking account with Pamela at First Western for the house bills. Mortgage, power, water, the gas bill, the property tax escrow. I've put my paycheck into it every two weeks for thirty-one years. Pamela puts her teacher's salary in her own account, and she has since 1997, when she decided it was "simpler." Remember that.

The app said: Transfer out, $38,000.00, to Hendrix Advisory LLC.

"Not that I know of."

She's asking for half of Rios Plumbing Supply, temporary support of four thousand a month, and the house." I heard pages turning. "And she moved the money after filing. Which means she moved it after the standing order took effect. Hold on, Frank. I'm going to call First Western."

I sat in my office behind the Towson Avenue store, which Pamela had never once set foot in, and listened to my counter man out front telling a contractor that no, he could not have a water heater on credit until he paid for the last two. Thirty years at a supply counter. You learn who pays.

Sybil called back before ten.

"The bank put a hold on the transfer. It hadn't cleared Hendrix Advisory's account yet. The thirty-eight thousand is frozen until the judge says otherwise, and the judge is not going to look kindly on a wife who emptied the house account an hour after she signed a sworn statement saying she wouldn't."

"What happens to her?"

"Today? Nothing dramatic. Her debit card for that account will stop working. That's all."

That was all. And it was enough.

Late that morning, my phone rang. Pamela.

I let it ring four times, and then I answered.

"What did you do?" Her voice was shaking. In the background I heard a woman saying, "Ma'am, I'm sorry, would you like to try another card?"

"Where are you?"

"I'm at Bliss on Garrison. With two teachers from school. We were picking out a dress for the Destin trip, and my card just got declined in front of both of them. What did you do to the account?"

"I didn't do anything. You moved thirty-eight thousand dollars after you filed for divorce, and the bank froze it. Your lawyer should've told you about the standing order."

"That's our money."

"It's the house money. It pays the mortgage on the house you're asking the judge to give you."

She was breathing hard. I heard her walk away from the counter, the bell on the door, traffic on Garrison.

"My lawyer called me an hour ago," she said. "He pulled something. A UCC thing, like Nolan said. Towson Holdings. He says the registered agent is Sybil Kearney. Your Sybil Kearney. And the principal address is the store on Towson Avenue. Your store."

"That's right."

"Frank." Her voice dropped. "You're the lender? You're Truman's money?"

"Towson Holdings is owned by Rios Plumbing Supply."

"Rios Plumbing Supply is a counter."

"Rios Plumbing Supply is five stores, forty-four employees and a warehouse in Van Buren," I said. "It does about eleven million a year in sales. It was incorporated in March of 1993, twenty-seven months before our wedding, and Sybil says that makes it separate property. You can ask the judge for part of what it grew while we were married. You'll probably get some of that. You won't get half the business."

She didn't say anything for a long time.

"Thirty-one years," she finally said. "You lied to me for thirty-one years."

"I told you in 1998 the business was doing okay. You said that was nice and asked me what I thought of Renita's new countertops. I remember because I've got the card you gave me that Christmas in a drawer. You signed it 'Love you, counter man.'"

"You think this is funny?"

"No," I said. "I think it's the first time you've asked."

She hung up.

Fifteen minutes later, Renita texted me: "Pam just called Daddy screaming. Says she's coming to the closing tomorrow to stop it. Says she'll never speak to him again if he signs. Daddy didn't say anything. Then he called me and asked me what time it was. Frank, I think he's going to sign."

The title office is in a one-story brick building on Rogers Avenue, with a long conference room that has a walnut table, twelve chairs and a box of complimentary pens with the company's name on them. At ten o'clock Wednesday morning, eight of those chairs were full.

Lyman sat at the head with his own lawyer, a tall careful man named Pierce Bancroft. Truman sat on his right with his reading glasses and his own pen, because Truman doesn't trust free pens. The closer from the title company, a brisk woman named Vonda Ellsworth, sat at the corner with three stacks of documents. Sybil and I sat on the other side, across from Truman. Renita sat by the window.

The wire from Towson Holdings had landed in the title company's escrow account at eight-thirty that morning. Three million six hundred thousand dollars, to the penny. Vonda had confirmed it twice.

Lyman looked at the confirmation slip for a long time. Then he looked at me.

"Towson Holdings," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Sybil Kearney's the registered agent."

"Yes, sir."

"And Sybil's your lawyer."

"For nineteen years."

Lyman put the slip down. He looked at Truman, and Truman nodded once, and that was all the explaining anybody needed to do.

"Two thousand nine," Lyman said slowly. "The payroll loan. That was you, too."

"That was me."

"Eight months of payroll. My men's paychecks. And you never said a word. Not at one Thanksgiving. Not when I made jokes about you selling toilets."

"They were pretty good jokes, Lyman."

He laughed. It came out rough. He wiped his eye with his thumb, quick, like there was dust in it, and picked up the first document.

That's when the door opened, and Nolan Hendrix walked in.

He wasn't wearing sunglasses this time. He had his leather folder and a fresh letter on Arkoma letterhead, and he walked right up to the table like he'd been invited.

"Lyman. I'm glad I caught you. Arkoma has authorized me to raise their offer to four point two million, all cash, closing Friday. That's six hundred thousand more than—"

"Mr. Hendrix," Pierce Bancroft said, without standing up. "You've been terminated as my client's broker. I sent you that letter by email at seven o'clock last night. Did you read it?"

"I read it, and frankly, it's premature—"

"It also says we've reported the undisclosed side agreement to the Arkansas Securities Department and to Arkoma Capital's general counsel." Bancroft slid a sheet of paper across the walnut table. "Arkoma's general counsel replied early this morning. I'll read you the last line. 'Arkoma Capital withdraws all interest in Hammack Steel Fabrication, has terminated its engagement with Mr. Hendrix, and was not aware of any agreement with any member of the seller's family.'"

Nolan picked up the paper. He read it. I watched his face do what Pamela's had done at the party, cycle through about six things in two seconds, and land on none of them.

"They're throwing me under the bus," he said.

"They're throwing you off the bus, Nolan," Truman said. "There's a difference."

"Lyman, I've worked for you for eight months—"

"You worked for Tulsa," Lyman said. "And you used my daughter to do it. Get out of this room before I ask Vonda to call the police and tell them you're trespassing."

Nolan stood there for another second. Then he dropped the Arkoma letter on the table and walked out, and he left the door open behind him.

Pamela was standing in the doorway.

I don't know how long she'd been there. Long enough. She had on the green dress she'd bought for the party Saturday night, and her eyes were red, and she walked past Nolan in the hall like he was a coat rack.

"Daddy," she said. "Don't sign that."

"Pamela, sit down."

"Don't sign it. Please. If you sign it, everything Frank's been hiding comes out in court, and I look like a fool in front of the whole town."

"You signed a paper to sell me out for a hundred and fifty thousand dollars," Lyman said. "You did that in front of the whole town yourself."

"That was Nolan's idea."

"Your name's on it."

"Daddy, I'm your daughter. If you sign this today, I will never speak to you again. I mean it. Not at Christmas. Not on your birthday. Never."

The room was very quiet. Vonda looked down at her stacks of paper. Truman looked at the ceiling. Lyman looked at his oldest daughter for a long moment.

"Pamela Jean," he said. "I've got one question for you."

"What?"

"Who paid for my retirement party?"

She blinked. "What?"

"The club. The band. The bar. The banner with my name on it. Two hundred and forty people. Who paid for it?"

"You did. The company did."

"No, honey. I didn't. The company didn't." He tapped the wire confirmation. "The man you told not to embarrass you paid for it. Every dollar of this money is his. My retirement is his. Truman's company is his. And he sat by the bar all night with a club soda and let you call him a counter clerk."

Pamela turned and looked at me.

"Frank's—" she started, and then she stopped, because she'd finally heard the whole sentence in her own head.

Footsteps came down the hall, quick, and Celina came around the doorframe, out of breath, still in her nursing scrubs from her shift at Mercy. She'd come straight from work. She had something in her hand, and she held it out to her mother.

It was the index card. The rules card. I'd left it on the kitchen counter Monday morning, front side up, and Celina had picked it up.

"Mom," Celina said. "You told Dad not to embarrass us. Look around this room."

Pamela took the card. She looked at her own handwriting. Don't talk about plumbing. Stand near the bar. Say "supply business." No Army stories. Her hand was shaking, and the card shook with it.

Lyman picked up Truman's pen, the one Truman didn't trust anybody else with, and held it out to me instead.

"You want to do the honors, Frank? Your money."

"It's your company, Lyman," I said. "Sign it."
