"I'm only marrying him because my ex won't commit!"

My fiancée shouted it into the microphone at her little sister's wedding reception, in a rented ballroom at the Grand Sierra in Reno, in front of two hundred guests who had all just raised their champagne glasses for the maid of honor's toast.

Except Nancy wasn't the maid of honor. She'd just grabbed the mic when the maid of honor finished, the way she grabs the last dinner roll, and now she was laughing at her own joke with a glass of white wine sloshing in her other hand.

I was sitting at table six with a plate of prime rib going cold. Our own wedding was five weeks away, at a lodge up at Lake Tahoe, and the eighteen-thousand-dollar deposit had come out of my savings account in January. I'd also paid nine thousand four hundred dollars for the ring she was waving around up there like a flashlight.

A few people laughed. Most of them didn't. The bride, her sister Corinne, put her hand over her mouth.

I folded my napkin, set it beside my plate, and stood up.

The DJ was a kid in a vest, maybe twenty-five, and he was already reaching for the microphone like he wanted to take it away from Nancy before she said anything else. I got there first. I took it out of her hand, gently, the way you take car keys from a buddy at a barbecue.

"Let me fix that for you," I said into it.

Ninety seconds later, two hundred people were on their feet applauding, and Nancy was the only person in that ballroom who wasn't clapping.

What she didn't know was that the ex she'd just named was standing at the back bar in a gray suit, holding a beer, close enough to hear every word.

My name is Mario Gentry. I'm 56 years old, and I've been a commercial roofer in Reno, Nevada, for thirty-four years. Churches, warehouses, the flat roofs behind the casinos that tourists never see. I lost my first wife, Teresa, to cancer in 2019, and I didn't think I'd ever stand at an altar again. If you do roofing long enough, you learn one thing better than anything else. A roof doesn't fail in one storm. It fails one nail at a time, for years, and the storm just tells you about it.

Hold on to that.

So there I was, with the microphone, and two hundred faces turned toward me, and Nancy's wine glass frozen halfway to her mouth.

"Good evening, everybody," I said. "For those who don't know me, I'm Mario. I'm the him."

That got a laugh. A real one, mostly from the guys near the bar.

"First, Corinne and Felipe, congratulations. You two are the real thing, and I'm sorry this part of your night is about me. That's the last thing I wanted." I nodded at the head table. Felipe nodded back, slow, like a man watching a ladder start to tip.

"Second," I said, "I want to help Nancy out. She just told you she's only marrying me because her ex won't commit. I believe her. I've never known her to lie when she's had four glasses of chardonnay."

More laughter. Nancy's face went red from the neck up.

"Mario," she said, not into the mic. "Mario, give me that."

"So let me fix that for you, honey. The wedding on June fourteenth at Edgewood Lodge is canceled. Nobody needs to buy a gift. Nobody needs to book a room. If you already bought a gift, keep it, or give it to Corinne and Felipe, because they actually earned it."

Somebody near the dance floor started clapping. Then somebody else. It spread across the room the way a fire spreads across dry cheatgrass, and pretty soon two hundred people were on their feet, and I honestly don't know what all of them were clapping for. Some of them were clapping for me. Some were clapping because the guy next to them was. A couple of Felipe's cousins were whistling.

I handed the microphone back to the DJ.

"Sorry, son," I said. "Play something with a beat."

He played "Hotel California," which was either the worst choice or the best one, depending on who you ask.

I walked back to table six, picked up my jacket, and kissed the bride's mother on the cheek, because Marjorie Fenton had always been kind to me. Then I walked out the side door of the ballroom with the Eagles playing behind me.

Nancy didn't follow. Not right away. I heard later from about nine different people that she stood there in the middle of the dance floor holding her empty wine glass while her father, Ray, walked up to her and said something nobody could hear.

I made it all the way across the parking lot to my truck. I had the key in the door when I heard heels clattering on the asphalt behind me, fast.

It wasn't Nancy.

It was Becky Delaney, the maid of honor herself, in a lavender dress, her shoes in one hand and her phone in the other. Becky had been Nancy's best friend since they were twelve years old in Sparks. She was out of breath.

"Mario. Wait. Did you see him?"

"See who?"

"Kevin." She pointed back at the hotel. "He's here. He's been here all night."

I took my hand off the truck door.

"Kevin Ashby," I said. "The ex."

"Felipe works with him. They sell timeshares at the same resort up at the lake. Felipe invited half the sales floor." Becky pressed her hand to her chest. "Nancy knew he was coming. She's known for a month. Mario, she spent the whole cocktail hour standing next to him at the bar."

I thought about that. I'd seen her at the bar during cocktail hour. I'd figured she was getting a drink. I'd brought her a plate of those little crab cakes and she'd waved me off.

"She was talking to him," I said.

"She was performing for him." Becky's voice cracked. "That whole thing on the mic, that wasn't a drunk joke. That was a message. That was for him."

I leaned against the truck. The night was warm, and you could hear the freeway and the casino sign humming over the parking lot.

"How do you know that, Becky?"

She looked down at her bare feet.

"Because she told me. She's been telling me for a year." She held up her phone, but she didn't hand it to me. "She texts me after she texts him. Every time. 'Kevin liked my picture.' 'Kevin called me at work.' 'Kevin says he's thinking about it.' Thinking about it, Mario. Like he's considering a car."

"A year."

"Since about the time you gave her the ring."

I laughed. One short laugh, standing there in a parking lot in my good suit. Becky looked at me like I'd lost it.

"Sorry," I said. "I'm a roofer. I'm used to finding out the leak started a long time before anybody saw the water."

Becky started crying then, real crying, with mascara, and I handed her my handkerchief, which I'd only brought because Teresa always made me carry one.

"I should've told you," she said. "I should've told you months ago."

"Yeah," I said. "You should have."

She flinched. I let it sit a second, because it was true and she needed somebody to say it. Then I put my hand on her shoulder.

"But you ran across a parking lot barefoot to tell me now. That counts."

She wiped her face. "What are you going to do?"

"Go home."

"She lives in your house, Mario."

"I know where she lives."

I drove home with the radio off. It's funny what comes back to you when the storm finally hits. All those little nails.

Like the Christmas party at her dental office, where she introduced me to the new dentist as "Mario, he does roofs," the way you'd say "he collects bottle caps."

Like her fifty-first birthday, when I spent four months building her a cedar planter box for the backyard, and she thanked me in front of everybody and then said, "Kevin used to take me to Napa for my birthday. Now that was a man who knew how to celebrate."

Everybody laughed. I laughed too. Ha ha, Kevin.

Like when we picked the date. She wanted June fourteenth. I asked her why, since it was a Saturday in peak season and cost four thousand dollars more than a Sunday in May. She said it was just a pretty date. I found out later, from Becky, that June fourteenth was the day Kevin Ashby had taken her to dinner nine years ago and told her he didn't believe in marriage.

She'd picked our wedding day so she could make a point to another man.

One nail at a time.

I got home a little after eleven. The house was quiet. Nancy's reading glasses were on the kitchen counter next to her coffee mug, and her slippers were by the couch where she always kicked them off. She'd moved in fourteen months ago, after her lease ran out, and I'd cleared out half the closet Teresa used to use, which wasn't easy.

I stood there in the kitchen for a long time.

Then I went out to the garage and got the boxes.

I didn't throw anything. I didn't break anything. I folded her sweaters the way she likes them folded, in thirds, and I wrapped her coffee mug in a dish towel. I labeled each box in black Sharpie in my best block letters, like I label materials on a job. "Nancy, Closet." "Nancy, Bathroom." "Nancy, Books."

My phone was face up on the counter, buzzing every couple of minutes.

At midnight it said seven missed calls from Nancy.

At twelve-thirty it said fourteen.

At one in the morning, it said twenty-two missed calls and one text from a number I didn't know.

"This is Kevin Ashby. Felipe gave me your number. I think we should talk. Not about her. About what she's been telling people."

I didn't answer Kevin. I didn't answer anybody. I put the last box by the front door, set my alarm for six, and slept on top of the covers in my clothes.

At seven-fifteen on Sunday morning, somebody leaned on my doorbell.

Nancy was standing on the porch in yesterday's dress with a hotel bathrobe over it and her heels in her hand. Her mascara was gone, either cried off or wiped off, and she had the gray look of somebody who'd slept three hours in her parents' guest room and drunk a lot more than four glasses of chardonnay.

She didn't have her key. Her purse was still at the Grand Sierra, I found out later, under table two.

I opened the door. She looked past me and saw the boxes.

"Oh, my God," she said. "Oh, my God, Mario."

"Morning."

"You packed my stuff? You packed my stuff in the middle of the night like some kind of psycho?"

"I folded your sweaters in thirds," I said. "You want coffee?"

She stared at me. Then she came inside, because she didn't know what else to do, and sat down at the kitchen table where she'd sat every morning for fourteen months. I poured her a cup in a different mug, since hers was wrapped up in a dish towel in the box marked "Nancy, Kitchen."

She held it with both hands.

"Baby, I was drunk," she said.

"I know."

"I didn't mean it. You know I didn't mean it. It was a stupid joke."

"Okay."

"It was a joke, Mario. Everybody makes jokes at weddings."

"Everybody laughed except your sister," I said. "And your mother. And me."

She put the mug down. "You can't cancel a wedding over a joke."

"I didn't cancel it over a joke. I canceled it because you told two hundred people the truth, and I believed you."

"It's not the truth!"

"Then why'd you pick June fourteenth?"

That landed. I watched it land. She opened her mouth and nothing came out, and then she shut it, and her eyes went to the boxes by the door like she was doing math.

"Who told you about that?"

"Doesn't matter."

"It was Becky. It was Becky, wasn't it? That little—"

"It doesn't matter who told me, Nancy. What matters is it's true."

She got up and walked to the window and walked back, which is what she does when the computer system at the dental office goes down on a Monday.

"Okay. Okay. So I used to date Kevin. So what? You were married for twenty-six years before me. You've got Teresa's pictures all over this house. I never said a word."

"You said plenty of words. You asked me to take the one in the hallway down last Christmas."

"That's different."

"It is different," I said. "Teresa's dead. Kevin was at the back bar last night with a beer."

She stopped walking.

"You saw him?"

"Becky saw him. I didn't have to."

That was the first time I saw her look scared instead of mad. It went across her face quick, like a chip in a windshield, and then she smoothed it over, because Nancy has always been good at smoothing.

"Okay, so you humiliated me," she said. "In front of my whole family. In front of my father. You took the microphone at my sister's wedding and you made me a joke. You think that's what a man does?"

"I think a man doesn't marry a woman who's waiting on somebody else."

"I'm not waiting on anybody!"

"Then what was the microphone for?"

She sat back down hard. She put her face in her hands. When she looked up, she was crying again, and I'll be honest with you, part of me wanted to reach across the table. Fourteen months is fourteen months. But the storm had already told me what I needed to know.

"Please," she said. "Please, Mario. I'll do anything. I'll go to counseling. I'll call every single person at that wedding and tell them I was wrong. I'll tell my dad. Please don't do this."

"It's done."

"It's not done! The lodge doesn't even open until nine. You can still call them."

"I called them at six-thirty," I said. "The weekend manager picks up early. They're keeping eight thousand of the deposit and sending me back ten. I'm going to eat the eight."

She just looked at me. She'd planned the whole wedding, every napkin and every centerpiece, and she knew exactly what that eight thousand dollars had bought. It had bought her a lesson, and she knew it.

"Where am I supposed to go?" she said, very quietly.

"You can stay here thirty days," I said. "That's fair. I'll be at Tito's. After thirty days, I'll help you load the truck wherever you want."

"Thirty days. Like an eviction."

"Like a man who doesn't want you sleeping in your car."

She laughed, ugly and wet. "You're so calm. You're so freaking calm about everything. You know that? Kevin used to say you'd be like this. He said you were a guy with no fire in him."

"Kevin texted me at one o'clock this morning," I said.

She sat back like somebody had pulled the plug on her.

"What did he say?"

"Said he wanted to talk."

"Don't," she said. "Mario, don't talk to him. He's a liar. Whatever he tells you, he's lying."

"Funny," I said. "That's what he said about you."

That wasn't strictly true. All Kevin had said was that we should talk about what she'd been telling people. But I wanted to see what her face did, and her face told me plenty.

My phone buzzed on the counter. We both looked at it.

It wasn't Kevin. It was Ray Fenton, her father, and Ray doesn't text, so it was a call. I picked it up.

"Mario." Ray's voice was gravel, seventy-eight years of union carpentry and Pall Malls. "Is my daughter there?"

"She's here, Ray."

"Good. Come to dinner tonight. Six o'clock. Both of you." He paused. "Corinne wants a word with her sister. And I want a word with you."

He hung up before I could say anything. Nancy was staring at me.

"What did he want?"

"Dinner," I said. "Six o'clock."

She looked at the boxes by the door, and then she looked at me, and for the first time all morning she didn't say anything at all.

Ray and Marjorie Fenton live in the same little brick house in Sparks they bought in 1974. Ray built the back porch himself and the kitchen cabinets and the little shelf in the hallway where Marjorie keeps her angel figurines. When Ray Fenton tells you to come to dinner at six, you're in the driveway at ten till.

Nancy rode with me. She didn't have a choice, since her car keys were in her purse under table two at the Grand Sierra. She didn't say one word the whole drive.

Corinne and Felipe were already there. They were supposed to leave for their honeymoon in Cabo on Tuesday, and Corinne was still wearing her hair the way the stylist had done it for the wedding, half falling down now.

She was sitting at the dining room table with her arms crossed, and when Nancy walked in, she didn't get up.

"Hi, Corinne," Nancy said.

"Don't."

"Sweetie, I just want to say—"

"Don't, Nance. Sit down."

Marjorie brought out pot roast like nothing was happening, because that's what Marjorie does. Ray sat at the head of the table and said grace, short. Then he picked up his fork and pointed it at nobody in particular.

"Somebody tell me what happened at my daughter's wedding."

Nancy jumped in first. Nancy always jumps in first.

"Daddy, I had too much to drink, and I said something stupid, and Mario completely overreacted. He took the microphone right out of my hand. He humiliated me in front of everybody. He canceled our wedding in front of two hundred people, Daddy. Who does that?"

Ray chewed. He looked at me.

"Mario?"

"That's about right," I said. "Except for the part where I overreacted."

"You think canceling a wedding into a microphone is a normal reaction?" Nancy said.

"I think announcing why you're getting married into a microphone isn't a normal reason."

Corinne slapped her hand flat on the table, hard enough to make the gravy boat jump.

"You know what nobody's talking about? My wedding. My wedding, Nancy. I waited forty-four years to get married. I had one night. One. And my whole reception is going to be remembered as the night my sister screamed about her ex into the microphone and her fiancé dumped her in front of the cake."

"He didn't have to—"

"He was the only one who handled it with any class!" Corinne's voice cracked. "He congratulated us. He apologized to us. He kissed Mom. He told the DJ to play something with a beat. You know what you did? You stood in the middle of my dance floor and cried until Becky took you to the bathroom."

Nancy turned to her mother. "Mom."

Marjorie passed the green beans to Felipe. "I'm staying out of this, honey."

Felipe took the beans and set them down without taking any. He looked at me, then at his new wife, then at his plate.

"There's something else," he said. "And I'm sorry, Corinne, I was going to tell you on the plane."

"Tell me what?"

He rubbed the back of his neck. "Kevin came up to me at the bar after Mario left. He was pretty upset. He said Nancy had texted him during the toasts. Before she grabbed the mic."

The whole table went still.

"What did she text him?" Ray said.

"He showed me." Felipe wouldn't look at Nancy. "It said, 'Say the word and I'll call it off. Tonight.' And he didn't answer. And then she went up and grabbed the microphone."

Nancy's chair scraped back. "That's a lie. That's a lie, Felipe, he's making that up, he's always been—"

"I saw the phone, Nancy."

"He's jealous! Kevin's jealous, he's always been—"

"Then why'd you text him?" Corinne said.

Nancy stood there with her napkin in her hand, and for once in her life, she had no smooth thing to say.

Ray set his fork down. He looked at his older daughter for a long time. Then he looked at me.

"Mario," he said, "when I was coming up as a carpenter, my old foreman told me something. He said you never frame a house on a crooked foundation, because no matter how pretty you build it, it's going to pull every wall out of square." He took his glasses off and rubbed his eyes. "You did the right thing. I'm sorry it was my kid who made you do it."

"Daddy!"

"Sit down, Nancy."

She didn't sit down. She grabbed her phone off the table and walked out the back door onto the porch Ray had built, and we all heard her dialing.

"Kevin," she said, loud enough for the whole table to hear. "Kevin, it's me. Call me back. Tell them. Tell them you're going to marry me."

Kevin didn't call her back. Not that night, anyway.

I drove home alone. Corinne gave Nancy a ride to the Grand Sierra to get her purse, and Nancy slept in my house that night with the boxes by the door, and I slept in Tito Escamilla's spare room with a NordicTrack at the foot of the bed. Tito has been my foreman for nineteen years. He didn't ask one question when I showed up at nine o'clock with a duffel bag. He just handed me a beer and said, "Lupe made enchiladas. Eat or she'll be offended."

I ate. Lupe was not offended.

Monday morning my phone said thirty-one missed calls and forty-four unread texts. I read the top one.

"I can't believe you're doing this to me. After everything."

I read the next one.

"Becky's a liar and Felipe's a liar. Call me."

Tito read it over my shoulder in the truck on the way to the job, because Tito has no shame.

"Forty-four texts," he said. "My wife's never sent me forty-four texts in her whole life."

"Lupe doesn't need to. You do what she says."

"That's true." He chewed on that. "You gonna answer any of them?"

"One."

"Which one?"

"Not one of hers."

He looked at me sideways. "Boss, I've seen you walk across a forty-foot roof with a busted ankle so you wouldn't miss a deadline. I've never seen you this calm. It's scaring the guys."

"Tell the guys the storm already came through," I said. "Now it's just cleanup."

I stopped reading after that and texted Kevin back, finally. Four words. "Thursday. Job site. Noon."

I sent him the address of a strip mall off South Virginia Street where we were tearing off forty squares of old built-up roof and putting down new membrane. It's a good place to meet a man. There's a lot of noise, and nobody can hear you if you raise your voice, and nobody's tempted to.

He showed up Thursday at ten after twelve in a white Lexus with a resort parking sticker on the windshield. He was 53, tan, with a nice haircut and sunglasses he kept on even after he walked into the shade of my truck. That should tell you most of what you need to know about Kevin Ashby.

I climbed down the ladder and wiped my hands on a rag.

"Mario," he said. He didn't offer to shake. Smart man.

"Kevin."

"I'm not here to fight with you."

"Good. I don't fight on job sites. Liability."

He almost smiled. Then he took off the sunglasses, which I'll give him credit for.

"Look," he said. "I've been getting calls from Nancy for four days. Like, forty a day. She's telling people at the resort I proposed to her. She told Felipe's whole sales team I bought her a ring. My boss asked me about it in a meeting."

"Did you propose?"

"No." He said it fast and flat, like a man who'd practiced saying it. "I never proposed. I'm never going to propose. Not to her, not to anybody. I told her that nine years ago at a steakhouse in Sparks, and I told her again every time she called me this past year. I'm fifty-three. I like my condo. I like my boat. I don't want to be married."

"June fourteenth," I said.

He winced. "She told you about that."

"Somebody did."

"That was our anniversary. Our first date. I told her that night I didn't believe in marriage, and she threw a whole steak at me." He laughed, but not happily. "And then she picked it for your wedding. I swear to God, man, when Felipe told me the date, I almost didn't go to his wedding."

"Why'd you go?"

"Free bar." He shrugged. "And honestly? I wanted to see if she'd really do it. Marry a guy to get back at me. I didn't think anybody would do that."

"She almost did."

"Yeah." He looked at the parking lot. "Then she texted me during the toasts. 'Say the word and I'll call it off.' And I didn't answer. And then she went and grabbed the mic."

"Why didn't you answer?"

He looked at me like I'd asked him why he didn't jump off the roof.

"Because I didn't want the word," he said. "I've never wanted the word. I just liked that she wanted me to."

That's the most honest thing Kevin Ashby ever said to me, and I believe it's the most honest thing he ever said to anybody.

"Kevin," I said. "Have you told her that? In those words?"

"I told her Monday. On the phone. She hung up on me."

"Tell her again."

"Why?"

"Because she's living in my house for twenty-six more days," I said, "and she thinks you're coming to get her, and you're not. Somebody ought to make sure she knows it before she plans another wedding around you."

He put his sunglasses back on. He nodded once.

"Yeah," he said. "Okay. I'll tell her."

He walked back to his Lexus. Halfway there he turned around.

"For what it's worth," he said, "that thing you did with the microphone? Felipe's cousins are still talking about it. One of the guys on the sales floor wants to know if you do motivational speaking."

"I do roofs," I said.

He laughed and drove away. I went back up the ladder. Forty squares of membrane don't put themselves down.

Here's where Nancy's ladder started to climb. I counted every rung.

Thursday night, after Kevin called her, the phone said fifty-two missed calls.

Friday morning, Becky texted me. "She called me crying at 6 a.m. Kevin told her. She called me a traitor and then asked me to come over and help her do her hair. I said no."

Friday afternoon, Corinne called from the airport in Cabo, of all places, on her honeymoon. "Mario, she's calling me on my honeymoon. She wants me to tell you she was confused. I'm not telling you that. I'm telling you she called me on my honeymoon."

Friday night, a little before eleven, it was Nancy again, and this time I picked up, because the voicemail she left before it was just her breathing hard.

"Mario. Mario, my car died. I'm on the Mount Rose Highway. It's dark and there's no shoulder and I think somebody's following me. Please. Please come get me."

Being a roofer, you get a lot of calls in the middle of the night. Water coming through a ceiling at a nursing home, a skylight blown out at a church. After thirty-four years, you learn to ask one question before you get in the truck.

"Where exactly?" I said.

"I don't know. Past the turnoff. Just come."

"Which turnoff?"

"Mario, I'm scared!"

"Okay," I said. "Stay in the car. Lock the doors."

I hung up and called the roadside assistance number on the back of my insurance card, because Nancy's Honda was on my policy, the same as she was on my health insurance. I'd added her when she moved in. I gave them her plate number and asked them to use the car's GPS to find it.

The dispatcher called me back eleven minutes later.

"Sir? The vehicle's not on Mount Rose. It's showing in a parking lot at an apartment complex on Lakeside Drive. The engine was running as of four minutes ago."

I knew that complex. It's where Kevin Ashby keeps the condo he likes so much.

"Thank you," I said. "Cancel the tow."

I called Nancy back. She picked up on the first ring.

"Are you coming?"

"Nancy," I said. "You're in Kevin's parking lot. The engine's running. Go home."

There was a long silence. Then she hung up.

She didn't call again that night. That was the first night in a week she didn't.

On Saturday morning, a little after nine, she knocked on Tito's door.

Tito answered in his bathrobe. He looked at her, and he looked back at me sitting at his kitchen table with a cup of coffee, and he said, "Lupe, we're going to the store," and the two of them walked out to their car in their bathrobes and drove away. Lupe was still holding her coffee.

I don't know how Nancy found Tito's house. Then I remembered. Two years ago at Christmas, she'd made me drive over a tray of her lemon bars, because Lupe had brought us tamales and Nancy keeps score of everything, and she wasn't going to owe anybody a tamale.

She sat down across from me. She'd done her hair. She'd put on her good blouse.

"I need a favor," she said.

"Okay."

"My lease application got turned down. The apartment on McCarran. They said my income doesn't qualify on its own, and they need a co-signer." She slid a paper across the table. "It's just a signature, Mario. It's just a formality. I'll pay every month."

I looked at it. Twelve months, nineteen hundred a month. Co-signer, jointly and severally liable. I read it twice, because I read everything twice, the way I read a job spec before I bid it.

"No," I said.

"Mario."

"No."

"It's one signature! After all the money you've thrown away on this wedding, you can't—"

"I didn't throw it away. I paid for it. That's different."

"You owe me this. You humiliated me. My whole family hates me. Corinne won't talk to me. My dad won't look at me. Kevin—" She stopped.

"What about Kevin?"

She looked down at the paper.

"He says I need to stop calling him," she said, very small. "He says he never wanted to marry anybody. He said he only liked that I wanted him to."

"He told me the same thing."

"You talked to him?"

"Thursday. On a roof. Well, under one."

She was quiet for a long time. Then she pushed the lease back toward me again, slower this time.

"Please," she said.

"I'll give you the thirty days I promised," I said. "I'll help you load the truck. I'll take you off my insurance the day you move, not before, so you're covered. That's what I'll do. I won't sign that."

"Why not?"

"Because I'm not on your foundation anymore, Nancy. I'm not going to be a wall you build on."

She stood up. She took the paper. She stopped at Tito's front door with her hand on the knob.

"You think you won," she said.

"I don't think anybody won."

She left. I sat there with my coffee until Tito and Lupe came home with a bag of groceries they didn't need and asked if it was safe to come in.

Nancy kept her word on the thirty days, I'll give her that. On the tenth of June, a Tuesday, I pulled up to my own house at eight in the morning with a borrowed box truck from Tito's brother-in-law, and Ray Fenton was already sitting on my porch step in his work boots.

"Figured you could use a carpenter," he said. "She's moving back into her old room. Marjorie's putting new sheets on."

"You don't have to do this, Ray."

"Yeah, I do." He stood up, slow, with a hand on his knee. "I raised her. I can carry her boxes."

Nancy came out in jeans and an old Wolf Pack sweatshirt with her hair pulled back. She looked at her father, and she looked at me, and she didn't say anything. She just picked up the box marked "Nancy, Books" and carried it to the truck.

We loaded for two hours. The three of us, not talking much. Ray told one story about the time he framed a house for a dentist who wanted his garage built facing the wrong way, and Nancy almost laughed, and then caught herself.

When the last box was in, she stood in my driveway and looked at the house for a while.

"You took me off the insurance?" she said.

"Today," I said. "Like I told you. Your dental office has the paperwork for the individual plan. I called them yesterday to make sure."

"You called my office."

"I called Gloria in billing. She's the one who does the forms."

She nodded slowly. She turned the ring on her finger, around and around, the way you'd tighten a bolt you weren't sure about.

"I'm keeping this," she said. "Until you ask for it."

"I'm not going to ask for it."

"Then I'm keeping it."

Ray started the box truck. She climbed in next to her father, and they pulled out, and I stood in my driveway in front of a house that was quieter than it had been in fourteen months.

That night Becky called me.

"Mario. She's been asking everybody what you're doing on the thirteenth."

"Why the thirteenth?"

"Because that's the night of your rehearsal dinner," Becky said. "At the Basque place on Fourth Street. She knows you never canceled the reservation. She knows you're still going."

She was right. I hadn't canceled it. I'd paid a five-hundred-dollar deposit on the back room at the Basque restaurant in April, and when I called to cancel, the owner, a big man named Arturo Etcheverry who's been feeding Reno since before I had my license, told me he couldn't refund it but I could still come eat it.

So I'd invited my crew.

"She's going to show up," Becky said. "She's going to try something in front of everybody."

"Then I guess I'll see her there," I said.

June thirteenth was a Friday. I took the guys to the Basque place at six. Tito and Lupe, the four other men on my crew and their wives, and Ray Fenton, who'd called me Monday to ask if the invitation was still open to a man who'd raised the wrong daughter. Corinne and Felipe came too, back from Cabo, tan as two saddles. Becky came last, in a blue dress, and sat at the end next to Lupe.

The Basque place does family style. Long tables, big bowls of soup, bean stew, salad, then lamb and steak, and a little glass of Picon punch if you're brave. You sit elbow to elbow with whoever's next to you. The front room was full of people who'd never met each other, passing bread down the line like a family.

We were on the lamb when the front door opened.

Nancy walked in alone. She'd done her hair the way the stylist had done it for her sister's wedding. She was wearing the ring.

The whole front room saw her come in, and a few people stopped eating, because she walked straight through the long tables toward our back room like she was walking down an aisle.

She stopped in the doorway. My crew went quiet. Ray put his fork down.

"Mario," she said. "Can I please say something?"

"You can say anything you want," I said.

She took a breath. Behind her, the front room had gone quiet too. Thirty or forty strangers, holding their spoons, waiting.

"I was wrong," she said. "I was wrong, and I was drunk, and I was stupid, and I said a terrible thing in front of everybody, and I'm saying it in front of everybody now. I don't want Kevin. I never really did. I wanted him to want me. And that's... that's sick, I know that. I'll get help. I'll go to counseling every week. I'll do whatever you want. Tomorrow was supposed to be our wedding day. It can still be. We can go to the courthouse. We can do it small. Just you and me."

She held out her left hand with the ring on it, like she was offering it back to me to put on her again.

"Please," she said. "Let me fix this."

The whole place was so quiet you could hear the soup simmering in the kitchen.

I stood up. I put my napkin beside my plate, the same way I did at her sister's wedding.

"Nancy," I said, "you told two hundred people why you were marrying me. You don't get to fix that. I already did."

I walked past her, through the long tables, between the strangers holding their spoons. Behind me, Ray Fenton said, "Arturo, another round of Picon for my son-in-law's crew. On my tab." Nobody corrected him.

Arturo Etcheverry was standing by the front door with a dish towel over his shoulder. He pulled it open for me.

"Leaving before dessert, Mario?"

"Save mine for Ray," I said. "He earned it."
