"You never notice anything anymore, Frank."

My wife said it at the Moose Lodge on New Year's Eve, at a long table with about forty people around it, ten minutes before the ball dropped on the TV over the bar. She said it loud enough for the whole table, and she said it smiling, the way you say something mean when you want everyone to think it's a joke.

The thing I hadn't noticed was her earrings. New ones. Little gold hoops with a blue stone. She'd had them on since six o'clock, and I hadn't said a word, and now forty people knew it.

Thirty years in December, and I'd missed a pair of earrings.

"You're right, honey," I said.

Everybody laughed. Heather laughed. Her sister Paula laughed so hard she had to put her wine down. I picked up my bottle of Coors and held it up.

"To noticing more in the new year," I said.

"Ha!" Heather clinked my bottle with her glass. "I'll believe it when I see it."

She would. Eleven months later she'd be sitting under a sixty-inch screen at our thirtieth anniversary party, watching herself walk into a hotel lobby, and the man in the chair next to her would be looking for the fire exit.

What she didn't know, while she laughed, was what I'd found in the pocket of her good wool coat an hour earlier, when she asked me to go get it from the car because she was cold. It was a parking stub. Little America Hotel, December twenty-eighth, in at 11:42 a.m., out at 2:15 p.m. On the twenty-eighth, she'd told me, she was at the dental office all day doing year-end billing.

I'd folded it in half and put it in my wallet behind my driver's license.

My name is Frank Delmonico. I'm fifty-six, and I'm a home inspector in Flagstaff, Arizona. People pay me four hundred and fifty dollars to crawl through their attic and under their house and tell them what the seller didn't mention. A stain on a ceiling. A crack in a foundation somebody painted over. A brand new water heater in a house where the floor around it is soft. Twenty-two years of that teaches you something: when somebody tells you there's nothing to see, look harder. Hold on to that.

"Partner!"

A big hand clapped me on the back hard enough to slosh my beer. Troy Gamble pulled out the empty chair beside me and dropped into it with a plate of cocktail shrimp. Troy manages the golf course at Ponderosa Ridge. We've played every Saturday morning at seven for about six years, him and me and two other guys, and he cheats on his scorecard and everybody lets him.

"Happy New Year, buddy," he said. "Where's my girl Ivy? Ivy! Get over here, they're doing the countdown."

His wife Ivy waved from the other end of the table. She's a librarian at one of the middle schools, a quiet woman with reading glasses on a chain who always seems to be watching the room from about two feet behind her own face.

"Nice earrings, Heather," Troy said across me. "New?"

"Thank you, Troy," Heather said, and she smiled at him in a way I'd seen her smile at me about twenty years ago. "See, Frank? Somebody notices."

Ten. Nine. Eight. The whole lodge was counting. Heather put her hand on my arm and kept her eyes on the TV.

Troy leaned over to me with shrimp on his breath. "She's a keeper, partner," he said. "Don't you ever forget it."

Three. Two. One.

Heather kissed me. It lasted about as long as it takes to read a parking stub.

On January second, I sat at my brother Vinnie's kitchen table in his house off Butler Avenue with a yellow legal pad and a cup of coffee and the parking stub flattened out between us.

Vinnie is sixty-one and spent twenty-eight years with Flagstaff PD, the last nine as a sergeant. He has a face like an old catcher's mitt and a laugh like a truck downshifting. He picked up the stub with two fingers and turned it toward the window.

"Little America," he said. "Nice place. Big rooms. Good breakfast buffet."

"She was at work."

"Says who?"

"Says her."

"Uh huh." He put it down. "And you found this in her coat."

"She asked me to get the coat."

"So you didn't go digging."

"I didn't go digging."

"Good. Keep it that way." He tapped the legal pad. "What's this?"

"It's my report."

He read the top of the page. I'd written it the way I write all my reports, because that's the only way I know how to write anything. Item one. Location. Observation. Recommendation.

"Item one," Vinnie read out loud. "Parking stub, Little America Hotel, twelve twenty-eight, two hours thirty-three minutes. Subject stated she was at the office. Recommendation: further evaluation by a qualified specialist." He looked up at me. "Who's the qualified specialist?"

"I don't know yet."

"It's you, Frankie. You're the specialist." He leaned back and the chair creaked. "Here's what I'd tell anybody who sat in that chair. Don't confront her. Not yet. You confront her with one parking stub and she's got a story for it by dinner. A friend in from Phoenix. A girls' lunch. A massage. And then she knows you're looking, and she gets careful, and you get nothing."

"So what do I do?"

"What you do for a living. You walk the house. You write down what's there. Dates, times, money. You don't hack anything, you don't steal anything, you don't follow anybody into a bathroom. You use what's already yours." He held up a thick finger. "Joint credit card. Whose name?"

"Both."

"Then the statement's yours. Bank account?"

"Joint."

"Yours. Phone plan?"

"Mine. I pay it."

"Then the bill's yours. Read it. Don't read her phone. Read the bill." He pushed the pad back to me. "And Frankie? Get a lawyer before you do anything else. Arizona's a no-fault state. A judge isn't going to care that she's sleeping around. But if she's spending marital money on it, a judge cares about that."

That night, after Heather went to bed at nine thirty with her book, I sat at the kitchen table with the laptop and logged into our Visa account. I'd never looked at it. Heather paid the bills. Heather had always paid the bills, because I was bad with paperwork, she said, which is a funny thing to say to a man who writes forty-page reports.

Little America Hotel. December twenty-eighth. $289.

Little America Hotel. December fourth. $289.

Little America Hotel. November nineteenth. $289.

Room service on the nineteenth. $64. Two entrees and a bottle of something.

I wrote it all down. Item two. Item three. Item four.

Then I clicked over to the family photo account. Heather set it up years ago so our daughter Gina could see pictures of the house and the garden while she was in nursing school down in Phoenix, and every picture any of us takes on our phones uploads to it automatically. Heather forgot about that the way people forget about the smoke detector until it starts chirping at three in the morning.

The newest picture had been uploaded that afternoon. It was a selfie of Heather in a white robe by a pool, steam coming off the water, red rocks behind her. She was laughing at whoever was holding the phone just past the edge of the frame. I could see a man's hand on her shoulder. A big hand, with a big gold class ring on it from Northern Arizona University, class of '93.

I knew that ring. I'd watched it pick up a golf ball off the seventh green every Saturday for six years.

The date on the picture was today. January second. Heather had told me that morning that she was driving to Prescott for a dental billing seminar.

From the bedroom, I heard her laugh at something on her phone.

By March, my report was eleven pages long.

I'd learned more about my wife in ten weeks than I had in the ten years before. She went to Sedona on the first Tuesday of the month. She went to Little America every other Thursday at lunch. She bought a new set of lingerie at Dillard's on our Visa in February, which I never saw, and a $140 bottle of Scotch that I also never saw, and I don't drink Scotch, and Troy Gamble does.

And twice a week, without fail, she told me I never noticed anything anymore.

"You didn't notice I cut my hair," she said one Sunday in March at her sister Paula's house, with Paula and Paula's husband and their two grown boys all sitting around the dining room table eating pot roast. "Did you, Frank? Be honest."

"No," I said.

"Three inches." She held up her fingers. "Three inches, and he didn't say a word."

"I'm sorry, honey. It looks nice."

"Too late now." She rolled her eyes at Paula. "Thirty years, and I could walk in here bald and he'd ask me to pass the potatoes."

Everybody laughed again. They always laughed. It had gotten to be a family joke. Poor Frank. Frank doesn't notice. You could set the kitchen on fire and Frank would ask what's for dinner.

Paula's husband Ed slapped my shoulder. "Get your eyes checked, Frank."

"I'll do that, Ed."

The thing is, I had noticed. She'd cut it on a Thursday, at a salon in Sedona, because that's where the charge came from on the card, eighty-five dollars at a place called Red Rock Salon and Spa, on a day she was supposedly at the office. And on Saturday at golf, Troy had told the whole foursome his wife was on him about money and he was going to have to cut back on the "extras."

I noticed everything. I just didn't say it out loud yet.

That's when I almost blew it.

Driving home from Paula's, Heather was in the passenger seat of my truck going through the center console looking for gum, and she pulled out the yellow legal pad. My report. I'd been working on it in the truck between inspections, and I'd stuffed it in there that morning and forgotten.

"What's this?" she said.

I kept my eyes on the road. There was snow coming down on Route 66, the wet kind, and the wipers were going.

"Work."

"It's got a lot of pages." She flipped the top sheet up. "Item fourteen."

My hands were on the wheel at ten and two. I didn't move them.

"Location," she read. "South elevation. Observation, moisture intrusion at the..." She stopped and squinted. "I can't read your handwriting. Nobody can read your handwriting, Frank."

"That's the Hendricks house on Cedar," I said. "They've got water coming in behind the stucco. It's a mess."

"Sounds boring."

"It is."

She dropped the pad back in the console like it was a dirty napkin and found her gum.

I'd copied the top page from the Hendricks job that morning onto the front of my notebook so I'd have something to show a client. That's the only reason my wife read about moisture intrusion on Cedar Street instead of about Little America on December twenty-eighth.

I didn't breathe right for about a mile.

When we got home, my phone buzzed while I was hanging up my coat. It was a number I didn't recognize, with a Flagstaff area code.

"Mr. Delmonico? This is Ivy Gamble. Troy's wife." Her voice was very quiet and very even, the way a librarian talks. "I'd like to hire you to inspect a house. Just me. Please don't mention it to Troy."

The house Ivy Gamble wanted inspected was a little two-bedroom on the east side, off Fourth Street, built in 1962, with a carport and a juniper in the front yard that hadn't been trimmed since the Clinton administration. She met me there on a Tuesday morning in April, standing in the driveway in a cardigan with her arms crossed, and she didn't say anything at all until I'd finished the roof.

I came down the ladder. She handed me a cup of coffee from the gas station.

"How's the roof?" she said.

"Five years left on it. Maybe six. There's a soft spot by the swamp cooler."

"I can live with a soft spot."

"Most people can. Till it rains."

She nodded. Then she said, "How did you find out?"

I stood there with the coffee.

"Find out what, Mrs. Gamble?"

"Ivy. Please." She uncrossed her arms. "You've been different at golf since January. Troy noticed. He thinks you're having trouble at work. He told me you shanked four drives in a row and didn't even swear." She almost smiled. "Troy doesn't notice much. But he notices when somebody beats him at golf, and when somebody stops."

"How did you get my number?"

"It's on the side of your truck. Delmonico Home Inspections, in big red letters. I've been looking at it in the parking lot of the golf course every Saturday for six years."

"Okay."

"So how did you find out?"

"A parking stub," I said. "New Year's Eve. Little America."

She closed her eyes for a second.

"December twenty-eighth," she said. "He told me he was at a superintendents' meeting in Phoenix." She opened her eyes again. "I found out in October. He left his club credit card statement on the printer at home. The club card. Ponderosa Ridge pays it. It's for business. Equipment, food for the tournaments, the vendors."

She reached into her purse and pulled out a folder. Of course she had a folder. She was a librarian.

"Little America," she said, putting one page on the hood of my truck. "Twice a month since last August. Enchantment Resort in Sedona, once a month. Room service. The spa. A necklace from a jewelry store in Scottsdale. All on the club card. All marked 'vendor meeting.'"

I read the pages. Then I took out my legal pad and laid it on the hood next to hers. She looked at it.

"Item one," she read. "Parking stub." She flipped. "Item twenty-two. Selfie, family photo account, Enchantment Resort, January second." She looked up. "You've been keeping a report."

"It's what I do."

"I've been keeping a folder."

"It's what you do."

For a second we just stood there in a stranger's driveway, two people with paperwork on the hood of a truck, and then Ivy Gamble laughed. It was short and surprised, like she'd forgotten she still could.

"Why are you buying a house, Ivy?"

"Because I'm leaving him. In December, after the holiday tournament. My lawyer says wait till I've closed on this place so he can't say I'm hiding money. I've saved the down payment out of my own check for two years." She took her pages back. "Troy doesn't know. Troy thinks I'm going to grade papers in the den for the rest of my life."

"Does the club know about the card?"

"Not yet." Her mouth went thin. "The owner's a man named Leo Varga. He comes up from Tucson twice a year to look at the books. He's coming in December, for the holiday tournament. He's the kind of man who reads every single line."

"So am I."

"I know. I've seen your handwriting on your scorecard." She tucked the folder under her arm. "There's one more thing you should know, Frank. In August, for her birthday, he's taking her to Las Vegas. Four nights at the Bellagio. He told me he's going to a turf conference."

"Who's paying?"

"Your wife, I think. Troy told her the club card was getting too hot." She looked at me. "So she's going to put it on yours."

The Vegas trip never happened. Something better happened instead, and I almost got killed finding it.

The second week of August, Heather told me she was going to Phoenix to help Gina move apartments. Gina, as far as Gina knew, was not moving anywhere. I'd called her to check. Gina is twenty-seven and a nurse at Banner, and she's always been smart enough to hear what you don't say.

"Dad," she'd said. "What's going on?"

"Nothing yet. Don't say anything to your mom."

"Dad."

"Gina. Please. In December. I'll tell you everything in December. Just come to the party."

"I'm coming to the party."

"Good."

On a Thursday night, with a monsoon storm coming in over the peaks, Heather threw a bag in her Highlander and kissed me on the cheek in the doorway.

"Don't eat crap while I'm gone," she said. "And remember to water the tomatoes."

"I'll remember."

"You won't. You never remember anything." She went down the steps with her keys. "See you Sunday."

I gave her ten minutes. Then I called Vinnie.

"She's not going to Phoenix," I said.

"How do you know?"

"Ivy says Troy took Friday off. The Vegas trip's dead because she put the deposit on our Visa and I froze the card Monday morning. Told the bank it got lost. Which it did, kind of."

Vinnie laughed his downshifting laugh. "So where are they going?"

"I think Sedona."

"In this weather? Down 89A? You'd have to be crazy to drive that canyon tonight."

"Or in love," I said.

"Frankie, don't follow her down that canyon."

"I'm not following her. I'm going to a public parking lot at a hotel I already know they go to, and I'm going to sit in my truck."

"In a monsoon."

"It's Arizona. It'll stop in an hour."

It didn't stop in an hour.

Highway 89A going down Oak Creek Canyon is a two-lane road cut into the side of a cliff, with switchbacks so tight the trucks have to swing wide into the other lane, and that night the rain was coming down so hard my wipers couldn't keep up. I drove with both hands at ten and two and my high beams off, because high beams in that kind of rain just light up the water.

About halfway down, at the bottom of the switchbacks, I came up behind a big silver Ram truck doing about twenty, and I knew that truck. Ponderosa Ridge Golf Club, it said on the back window in green vinyl letters. Troy Gamble didn't own a truck of his own. He drove the club's.

I dropped back. Way back. I let a pickup with a camper shell get between us.

At the turnoff for the resort, the silver Ram turned right. So did I, about a minute later. The road up to the resort winds through the red rocks, and with the rain running across it in sheets it was like driving up a creek. By the time I pulled into the guest lot, the Ram was parked near the lobby, and I parked at the far end under a pine with my lights off and the engine running for the defroster.

I took out my phone and waited.

They came out of the Ram together under one umbrella. Troy held it. Heather was laughing and holding onto his arm with both hands, and when they got to the lobby doors he stopped and kissed her, right there in the light of the entrance, with the umbrella tipped back and the rain coming down on both of them.

I took eleven pictures. I'm a home inspector. I take a lot of pictures.

Then I made my mistake.

I put the truck in reverse to back out and go home, and at exactly the same moment, Troy came running back out of the lobby without the umbrella, because he'd forgotten his bag in the Ram. And instead of walking to his truck, he jumped in, started it, and threw it in reverse to pull up under the awning.

His backup lights hit my windshield. He was coming straight back at me, fast, in the rain, looking at his own mirror and not at the lot.

I laid on the horn and cut the wheel hard. My truck slid on the wet asphalt and the passenger side bumped the curb, and the Ram's tailgate stopped about two feet from my door. Close enough that I could read the little sticker on his bumper from the course's charity tournament.

Everything stopped. The rain hammered on my roof.

Troy's door opened. He got out in the rain and stood there squinting at my truck in his headlights.

I had my hood up, on a sweatshirt, and my truck was white like half the trucks in Arizona, and my company sign is on a magnet, and I'd taken the magnet off that afternoon and put it behind my seat. Remember that.

"You okay, man?" Troy called. "Sorry! Didn't see you!"

I raised a hand through the windshield. Didn't roll the window down. Didn't say anything.

"Crazy night!" he yelled. "Drive safe!"

He climbed back in the Ram and pulled forward under the awning, and I backed out of that lot as slowly as I've ever backed out of anywhere and drove up the canyon in the dark with my heart going like a jackhammer and eleven pictures in my phone.

When I got home a little after one, I sat in the driveway for a long time with the engine off.

Then I texted Ivy. "Enchantment. Tonight. Eleven pictures. He almost backed into me and never knew who I was."

She wrote back in less than a minute, which meant she was awake too.

"Good. Now get a lawyer, Frank. A real one. Before she does."

Marian Echols has an office on Leroux Street above a bakery, and the whole place smells like cinnamon rolls, which she says keeps her clients calm. She's fifty-eight, with glasses on top of her head and a second pair around her neck, and she read my legal pad from front to back without saying anything for twenty-five minutes.

Then she said, "Mr. Delmonico, do you know how many husbands sit in that chair with a report like this?"

"No, ma'am."

"None. They come in with a feeling. You came in with an appendix." She set the pad down. "All right. Here's the law in plain English. Arizona is a no-fault state. The judge won't care about the hotel. But money from your joint accounts spent on another man is called waste, which just means she threw away money that was half yours, and a judge will give you credit for every dollar of it."

"How much is that?"

"By your numbers?" She ran her finger down the page. "Nine thousand four hundred and twelve dollars since November. Not counting the Vegas deposit you stopped."

"Okay."

"What else is in the joint accounts?"

"Savings. About thirty-one thousand."

She was writing. "Check it this week. Women who are about to leave usually move money first."

I checked it that afternoon from her waiting room, on my phone, with a cinnamon roll I didn't want going cold on a napkin.

Twelve thousand dollars had moved out of savings the Friday before. Into a checking account with only Heather's name on it.

I called Heather from the truck. I shouldn't have, but I did.

"Twelve thousand out of savings?" I said.

"Oh, that." She sounded bored. "That's for the party, Frank. The anniversary. You know, our thirtieth? December first? I knew you'd forget, so I'm handling it. The Moose Lodge, the caterer, a DJ, the cake. I booked it all. I invited a hundred and twenty people."

"Twelve thousand dollars for a party at the Moose Lodge."

"It's thirty years. I want it nice. Somebody has to." She sighed through the phone. "You never notice anything, so I'm doing it all myself. You can do one thing, okay? Make a slideshow. The kids would love it. Thirty years of pictures. They've got a big screen at the lodge. Can you handle that?"

I looked at the eleven pictures on my phone, the umbrella and the rain and the lobby lights.

"Yeah," I said. "I can handle that."

"Good. Oh, and I invited Troy and Ivy, obviously. And I'm putting Troy at our table. He's done so much for us."

"He sure has," I said.

I spent September, October and November building that slideshow.

I want to be honest about that, because people have asked me since how a man can sit at his kitchen table night after night scanning pictures of his own wedding while he knows what he knows. The answer is that the first ninety percent of that slideshow was real. Thirty years of real. Heather at twenty-three in a dress her mother sewed. Gina in a car seat. The camping trip to the Grand Canyon when the tent blew down. Our first house, the one with the bad furnace I didn't know how to inspect yet. Heather laughing with flour on her nose at Christmas.

I put every one of those in. I put them in order. I put music under them, Bob Seger, "We've Got Tonight," because it was our song in 1994 when I couldn't afford a real honeymoon and we drove to San Diego instead.

And then, after the last real picture, I put in item one.

Marian Echols filed the divorce petition the week before Thanksgiving and held it. In Arizona the other side has to be served, and she'd hired a process server, a retired schoolteacher named Mabel who does it on the side and who, Marian said, "could serve papers on the Pope and he'd thank her for it." Mabel was coming to the party. So was Leo Varga, who had come up from Tucson for the Ponderosa Ridge holiday tournament that same weekend. Ivy had asked him, very politely, if he'd like to see how Flagstaff throws a party, and Leo, a widower who likes cake, said yes.

Heather noticed none of it. She was too busy noticing me.

"You didn't notice the new tablecloths," she said at Thanksgiving, at Paula's again, in front of everybody. "I bought new tablecloths for the party. I showed you on my phone."

"I'm sorry, honey."

"Thirty years," she told the table. "Thirty years, and I'm married to a piece of furniture."

Paula's husband Ed laughed. "Get your eyes checked, Frank."

"I'm getting them checked Saturday, Ed," I said. "At the party. Everybody's going to get their eyes checked."

Ed laughed even harder, because he thought it was a joke.

The Moose Lodge on December first looked like a wedding. White tablecloths, the new ones I hadn't noticed. Silver balloons shaped like a three and a zero. A sheet cake from Fry's that said "30 Years Of Love, Frank and Heather" in blue frosting. A DJ named Rocky in the corner playing Shania Twain. And a hundred and twenty people in their good clothes, most of whom had heard Heather say I never noticed anything at least once, and half of whom had laughed.

Gina got there at six with her boyfriend. She came straight across the room and hugged me harder than she had since she was twelve.

"Dad," she whispered. "Is it tonight?"

"It's tonight."

"Is it bad?"

"It's true," I said. "That's all it is."

She held on a second longer. Then she let go and went to kiss her mother, and I watched her do it, and she did it right. My daughter should get an award.

Troy and Ivy came at six thirty. Troy was in a blue blazer with a golf club pin on the lapel, and he was already loud.

"Partner!" He grabbed my hand and pumped it. "Thirty years! You lucky son of a gun. How'd you ever land a woman like that?"

"I've been asking myself the same thing all year."

He laughed and slapped my shoulder. Ivy stood behind him in a gray dress with her reading glasses on the chain, and she looked at me over his shoulder for exactly one second. Then she said, "Happy anniversary, Frank," in her library voice, and went to find her seat.

Heather had made the seating chart herself. The head table had six chairs. Heather and me in the middle. Gina and her boyfriend on my side. And on Heather's side, right next to her, with his name card in her handwriting: Troy.

Ivy's card was at table four. With Leo Varga.

At seven thirty, after the prime rib and before the cake, Rocky the DJ turned the music down and handed Heather a microphone. She stood up in a red dress I'd never seen. She'd had her hair done that morning. She looked beautiful. I noticed.

"Thank you all for coming," she said. "Thirty years! Can you believe it?"

Applause. Whistles. Somebody yelled "Go Heather!"

"I want to thank everybody who helped make tonight happen. Paula. My girl Gina. Troy, who has been such a good friend to both of us this year."

Troy raised his glass from the chair beside her. People clapped for him. Somebody at table four didn't.

"And Frank." She turned and smiled down at me. "My husband. Who, as you all know, never notices anything." The laughter came, warm and easy, a hundred and twenty people who'd heard the joke before. "I planned this whole party, and I'll bet you he couldn't tell you what color the napkins are."

"Navy," I said.

That got a bigger laugh. Heather put her hand on her chest.

"Well, look at that. A miracle." She held the microphone out toward me. "Frank made a slideshow. His one job. Let's see if he remembered to plug it in."

I stood up. I took the microphone. My hand was steady. I'd been on my back under a lot of crawlspaces with black widows two inches from my face, and my hand was steady for those too.

"Heather's right," I said. "For eleven months now she's been telling me I never notice anything anymore. At New Year's. At her sister's. At Thanksgiving. Most of you were there for at least one of them."

Heads nodded around the room. Some people smiled.

"So I want to show you what I noticed," I said.

I pushed play.

Bob Seger started up through the lodge speakers. On the big screen, Heather at twenty-three appeared in her mother's dress, and the whole room went "aww." Then Gina in the car seat. The tent blowing down. The flour on her nose. People laughed and pointed and wiped their eyes. Heather put her hand on my arm, and for about four minutes my wife and I watched thirty years of our life together go by on a screen at the Moose Lodge, and she leaned into me, and I let her.

Then the music stopped. The screen went white.

Black letters, in the same typeface I use on every report I've ever written.

"Item one. December twenty-eighth. Little America Hotel. Two hours, thirty-three minutes. Subject stated she was at work."

A photo of the parking stub.

The room didn't know what it was looking at yet. A couple of people laughed, unsure, thinking it was part of the joke.

"Item four. Visa statement. Little America Hotel, three charges, $289 each. Room service for two, $64."

"Item nine. January second. Family photo account."

The selfie by the pool. The white robe. The steam. The red rocks. And on her shoulder, very clear on a sixty-inch screen, a big hand with an NAU class ring, class of '93.

Somebody at table four made a sound.

I didn't look at Heather. I looked at the man next to her. Troy Gamble was looking down at his own right hand on the white tablecloth, at the class ring on it.

"Item seventeen," the screen said. "Red Rock Salon and Spa, Sedona. Eighty-five dollars. A haircut she says I didn't notice."

"Item twenty-six. Dillard's. Scotch. Frank doesn't drink Scotch."

"Item thirty-one. August fourteenth. Enchantment Resort. Ponderosa Ridge Golf Club truck."

And then the last picture, the eleventh one I took that night in the rain, the one I'd cropped and blown up until you could count the raindrops. The umbrella tipped back. Heather's face turned up. Troy's face coming down to meet it, lit up gold by the lobby doors.

Thirty years of love, the cake said, in blue frosting.

The lodge was so quiet I could hear the soda machine in the hallway.

Heather stood up. Her chair scraped. "Turn it off."

"I'm almost done."

"Frank, turn it off! This is... you can't..." She looked out at the room, at a hundred and twenty faces, at her sister Paula with her hand over her mouth. "He's lying! That's not even... those are fake! He made those!"

"The Visa statement's real," I said. "So's the hotel. So's the photo account. You set it up yourself, so Gina could see the garden."

"Mom," Gina said. Just that. Quietly. From two chairs down.

Heather turned on Troy. "Say something!"

Troy stood up. His face had gone the color of the frosting on the cake. He pointed a finger at me, and his voice came out about twice as loud as it needed to.

"This is a private matter, pal, and you've got no right..."

He came around the table toward the laptop on the DJ stand. I don't think he knew what he was going to do when he got there. He grabbed at the laptop and I put my hand flat on top of it, and he shoved my shoulder with his other hand, hard enough to knock me back a step into the speaker. The speaker rocked. Rocky the DJ yelped and grabbed it.

I didn't shove him back. I didn't have to.

My brother Vinnie had been sitting at table two all night eating prime rib, and twenty-eight years of Flagstaff PD don't go away when you retire. He was out of his chair before the speaker stopped rocking, and he took Troy by the back of the blazer and the elbow and walked him three steps backward, gently, the way you'd move a drunk off a bar stool.

"Sit down, Troy," Vinnie said. "You're making a scene at your girlfriend's anniversary party."

Some people laughed at that. Not many. But some.

That's when Paula stood up at her table with her napkin balled in her fist and said the thing the whole room was thinking.

"Who invited him?" she said. "Who invited him to sit up there?"

"She did," I said. "Same as every other Thursday."

And the screen behind me, which was still running, clicked to the last page of my report.

"Item thirty-four. Recommendation: further evaluation by a qualified specialist."

A gray-haired man in a bolo tie stood up at table four. He had reading glasses on and a stack of papers in his hand, and Ivy was sitting very still beside him.

"Mr. Gamble," he said. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't have to. "Leo Varga. You know me. I own Ponderosa Ridge."

Troy turned around. You could see him remember who Leo Varga was.

"Mr. Varga. Sir. This is a misunderstanding."

"Little America, twice a month since last August," Leo said, reading off the page. "Enchantment Resort, nine visits. A necklace in Scottsdale. All on my club's card. All marked 'vendor meeting.'" He took his glasses off. "Mrs. Delmonico doesn't sell me fertilizer, does she, Troy?"

"I can explain..."

"You can explain on Monday to my lawyer. Leave the truck keys at the front desk of the club by nine tomorrow. You don't work for me anymore."

Troy looked at Heather. Heather looked at the floor. Ivy reached into her purse and took out a long white envelope and held it out to her husband across the table, and her hand did not shake at all.

"And those are from my lawyer," she said. "I closed on the house Thursday, Troy. It's got a soft spot by the swamp cooler. I can live with it."

Troy took the envelope like it was hot.

At the back of the room, a small white-haired woman in a church dress got up from the last table, walked all the way up the center aisle between the balloons, and stopped in front of my wife.

"Heather Delmonico?"

"What?"

"You've been served, dear." Mabel put the papers in her hand and patted it. "Lovely party."

Heather stood there in her red dress holding the divorce papers, with the Moose Lodge staring at her and the cake behind her saying thirty years of love. She looked at me. Her lips were moving, but nothing came out for a second. Then it did.

"You noticed," she said. "All this time. You noticed everything."

"Navy napkins," I said. "Earrings with a blue stone. Three inches off the back. I noticed, Heather. I just didn't say anything."

"Frank, please." Her voice dropped so only the head table could hear it. "Not like this. Not in front of everybody."

"You picked the everybody," I said. "A hundred and twenty people. You picked the room and the chairs and the man in the chair next to you. All I picked was the pictures."

She looked down the table at Gina. Gina looked back at her mother, and then she stood up and came around and stood beside me, and she didn't say a word. She didn't have to.

Troy was already backing toward the side door with Ivy's envelope in one hand and his car keys in the other. He got the door halfway open and stopped, because Leo Varga was standing in the hallway holding out his palm.

"The club truck keys, Troy," Leo said. "Not tomorrow. Now."

Troy put them in his hand. Then he walked out into the parking lot without a coat, and the door swung shut behind him.

I took the microphone back off the DJ stand.

"Thanks for coming, everybody," I said. "Don't let the cake go to waste. I paid for it."
