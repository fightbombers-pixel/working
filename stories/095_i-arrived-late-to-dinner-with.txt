"I'm divorcing him. He's too much of a loser for me."

My wife said it at the big round table in the back of the Branding Iron Steakhouse in Casper on a Friday night, in front of our five oldest friends and a restaurant full of people eating prime rib. I was standing about six feet behind her chair with my coat still on. She hadn't seen me come in.

Everybody laughed. Phil Burnett slapped the table. Russ Kincaid, our insurance man, tapped his ring on his water glass, tink, tink, tink, the way he does. His wife Courtney laughed a beat late, like she'd had to check first.

I'd been 22 minutes late because I was in my truck in the parking lot reading eight months of bank statements. Heidi and I had been married 19 years. I'd just found out she thought I was worth $1.8 million.

I walked up to the table, slid my wedding ring off my finger and set it down on her bread plate, right next to the butter.

Four minutes later, our insurance man was standing out in the parking lot with his own wife screaming at him under the light pole, and half the restaurant was watching through the window.

What Heidi didn't know was that her first husband still had the space heater.

My name is Emmett Weller. I'm 55, and I own Weller Propane, three delivery trucks and a bulk plant on the east side of Casper, Wyoming, that my dad started in 1971 with one truck and a loan from his brother. I've been around gas my whole life, and the first thing my dad taught me was this. Gas doesn't leak by itself. If you smell it, somebody loosened a fitting, or somebody didn't tighten one. Remember that.

Heidi turned around in her chair. She looked at the ring on the bread plate, then up at me, and her smile stayed on her face a second too long, like a television nobody turned off.

"Emmett. We were just kidding around."

"I know. It was funny."

"Sit down. You're making a scene."

"I'm standing still, Heidi. You're the one making the scene."

Phil stopped laughing. Tammy put her napkin down. Russ stopped tapping.

"Look, buddy," Phil said, "she was just..."

"I know what she was doing, Phil." I kept my eyes on my wife. "Go ahead, Heidi. You're divorcing me. I'm too much of a loser. Tell them the rest."

"There isn't any rest."

"Sure there is." I pulled out the empty chair next to her and sat down, and I took off my coat and hung it on the back of the chair, slow, the way you do when you're planning to stay a while. "Before you divorce me, tell our friends about the $1.8 million life insurance policy you took out on me in June."

You know how a room sounds when the air conditioning cuts off and you didn't know it was running? That's what that table sounded like.

"What are you talking about?" Heidi said.

"Plains Mutual Life. Policy number ends in 4417. Face amount one million eight hundred thousand dollars, double for accidental death. You're the owner. You're the beneficiary. It comes out of our checking account on the 3rd of every month, $412, and the bank statement calls it 'PMLIC PREM,' which I guess you figured I'd never read." I looked across the table. "Russ wrote it."

Every head at that table turned to Russ Kincaid.

Russ is 47, has very good teeth and a very good haircut, and drives a black Lexus with a vanity plate that says PLN AHEAD. He'd been our "money guy" for six years. He'd done our retirement, our truck insurance, our umbrella policy. He'd sat at our kitchen table more times than my brother had.

"That's key-person coverage for the business," Russ said quickly. "Totally standard. Emmett, you signed the application yourself. In June. At your kitchen table."

"I signed a stack of papers at my kitchen table in June," I said. "You told me they were for the SBA loan on the new bulk tank. Heidi brought me the pen."

"Well, then, you signed it." Russ spread his hands to the table, a man explaining weather. "Paramedical exam and everything. The nurse came out to the house."

"Heidi told me the nurse was for the loan, too."

"That's not my problem, Emmett."

"Key-person coverage on a business," I said, "gets paid to the business. Not to the owner's wife personally, at double for an accident." I turned to Courtney Kincaid. "Courtney, does Russ write a lot of million-dollar policies where the wife's the owner and the husband doesn't know about it?"

Courtney's face had gone very still. She was looking at her husband's hand on the tablecloth, the one with the ring.

"Russ," she said quietly. "You told me in June you were in Gillette for a week. For a conference."

"Honey, not here."

"You were at their house?"

"Courtney, it's a client..."

She stood up. She picked up her purse. She looked at Heidi, and then at Russ, and something in her face put the whole thing together faster than I ever could have.

"Outside," she said to her husband. "Now."

Russ followed her out. Through the front window, all of us watched them go, across the lot to the light pole by his Lexus, and we watched her start to yell, and we watched him put both his hands up like a man being robbed.

Heidi hadn't moved. She was looking at me the way you look at a dog that's never bitten anybody and just did.

"Who told you?" she said, very low.

"Does it matter?"

"Who told you, Emmett?"

I picked up my coat and stood.

"Glen did," I said. "He said to tell you hello."

Her face did something I'd never seen it do in 19 years. All the color didn't go out of it, the way they say in books. It went a kind of gray-yellow, like an old bruise. Tammy Burnett saw it too, because she made a little sound and put her hand on Phil's arm.

I left my ring on her bread plate. I walked out past the hostess stand, and past the window where Courtney Kincaid was still yelling under the light pole, and I got in my truck.

My phone buzzed before I had the key in. A text from Heidi.

"You just made the biggest mistake of your life."

I sat there and read it twice. Then I put the truck in gear, and I didn't go home.

I slept that night on the cot in my office at the bulk plant, because the house wasn't mine to sleep in anymore and because I needed to think. I didn't do much sleeping. I did a lot of thinking about a phone call I'd gotten that afternoon from a number in Cody.

The man on the phone had said his name was Glen Prater, and that he'd been married to Heidi from 2006 to 2013, and that he'd found my business number on the side of one of my trucks in a picture Heidi posted on Facebook.

"I'm not calling to make trouble," he'd said. "I'm calling because my sister saw the picture and said you looked like a decent man. And because I lie awake some nights wondering if she's doing it again."

"Doing what?"

"She did this to me too," Glen Prater said. "Check the insurance."

That's all he'd said before he hung up. Then I'd gone back through eight months of bank statements in my truck in the Branding Iron parking lot, and there it was, $412 a month, and I'd called the number for Plains Mutual off the statement, and the young man on the phone wouldn't tell me much. But he'd told me that I was the insured, and the face amount, and that the owner was not me.

At six the next morning I was on Highway 20 headed northwest to Cody, a little under four hours, with a thermos of coffee and the radio on a Chris LeDoux song I'd heard a thousand times.

Glen Prater lived in a single-wide trailer on two acres outside of town, with a horse that looked about as old as he did. He was 61 and walked with a cane, and when he opened the door he looked at me a long time before he said anything.

"You came," he said.

"You said check the insurance. I checked it. Now I want to know why."

He let me in. He made coffee in a dented percolator and set a cup in front of me, and he sat down across the little table and put both his hands flat on it.

"Winter of 2012," he said. "We had a cabin up the South Fork. Heidi's idea. She wanted a getaway, she said. Weekends. Just the two of us." He looked at his hands. "She took a policy out on me the spring before. Seven hundred and fifty thousand. Said it was for the mortgage. I didn't read it. Who reads them?"

"Nobody," I said.

"February. A Saturday. She said she had to drive back to Cody early for a closing at the title company, and she left me up there alone with a fire in the woodstove and the propane space heater going in the bedroom." He tapped the table. "You know what carbon monoxide feels like?"

"I've seen what it does."

"Feels like the flu. Then like being real tired. Then nothing." He looked up at me. "A snowmobiler named Arlo Gentry stopped to ask for directions about four in the afternoon. Saw me through the window on the floor. Busted the door. The doctors said another hour and I'd have been gone."

I didn't say anything. The horse outside nickered.

"The sheriff called it an accident," Glen said. "Bad heater. Old cabin. Happens every winter up here. Heidi cried in the hospital. Brought me magazines." He laughed, one short ugly laugh. "Eight months later she filed for divorce and walked off with half of everything, and I never could prove a thing."

"But you kept the heater."

He looked at me a long second. Then he got up, slow, with the cane, and went to the closet by the door and pulled out a cardboard box, and set it on the table between us.

Inside was a little blue propane wall heater, the kind you'd find in a hunting cabin, with about a foot of copper line still attached. He turned the fitting toward me.

I'm a propane man. I took one look at that flare nut and I didn't need him to explain anything.

There were bright crescent marks on two flats of the brass, fresh at the time, still shiny under the tarnish. Somebody had put a wrench on that fitting and backed it off about half a turn. Not enough to hiss. Just enough to leak slow into a closed room all afternoon.

"Gas doesn't leak by itself," I said.

"No," Glen said. "It doesn't."

"Did you show the sheriff?"

"I showed him. He said I could have done it myself, moving the heater. Said I was a bitter man going through a divorce." He sat back down. "Maybe I was. But I kept it."

I sat there looking at those wrench marks for a long time. Then I asked the question I'd driven four hours to ask.

"Was she alone, Glen? Back then? Did anybody help her?"

He rubbed his face with both hands.

"There was a fellow," he said. "Sold insurance out of Riverton back then. Came around the cabin once that fall to 'look at our coverage.' Heidi said he was a friend from college." He frowned, trying to pull it up. "Russ. That was his name. I never caught the last name. Tall. Real good teeth. Kept tapping his ring on my kitchen table the whole time he talked."

I drove back to Casper with that blue heater riding in a box on my passenger seat like a passenger, and I didn't turn the radio on once.

Somewhere past Shoshoni, with the sun going down red over the Owl Creek Mountains, my phone rang through the truck speakers. HEIDI, the dash said. I let it ring four times. Then I answered.

"Where are you?" she said. No hello.

"Driving."

"Driving where? You didn't come home last night. You didn't come home today. Tammy says your truck was at the plant at seven this morning and then it was gone."

"Tammy's got good eyes."

"Emmett, I'm your wife. I have a right to know where you are."

"You told a table full of people Friday night you were divorcing me."

"I was joking! Everybody knew I was joking!"

"Everybody laughed. That's not the same thing."

She took a breath, and I heard her switch gears. Nineteen years, you know the sound of it, like a truck dropping into low.

"Okay. Okay. You're upset about the insurance. I get it. I should have told you. Russ said it was smart, with the business and the new tank loan and everything, and I didn't want to worry you. That's all it was. Protection. For me. In case something happened to you, God forbid."

"At double for an accident."

"That's just how they write them!"

"Glen's policy said the same thing. Double for an accident."

The line went quiet. All I could hear was the road noise and the blinker clicking for a turn I wasn't making.

"You went to Cody," she said finally.

"I went to Cody."

"Glen is a bitter, sick old man who's been trying to ruin my life for ten years. He drinks, Emmett. He told everybody in Park County I tried to kill him because he couldn't stand that I left him. The sheriff laughed at him. Did he tell you that? The sheriff laughed at him."

"He told me."

"Then why are you listening to him?"

"Because he kept the heater, Heidi."

Another quiet, longer. I passed a hay truck. When she came back, her voice was different, flat and low and careful, the voice she used on the phone with lenders when a closing was going bad.

"What heater?"

"The one from the cabin."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Sure you do."

"Emmett, listen to me. You're tired. You're upset. You've been sleeping on a cot in that freezing office. You're not thinking straight. Come home tonight. We'll cancel the policy tomorrow, first thing, I'll call Russ myself. We'll go to the counselor at the church like you wanted last year. Okay? Just come home."

"Who said I was sleeping at the office?"

She didn't answer that.

"Heidi," I said. "Who told you I was sleeping on a cot at the office? I didn't tell anybody but Tomas."

"Tammy," she said, too fast. "Tammy said."

"Tammy saw my truck at seven in the morning. She didn't see a cot."

"I'm hanging up."

"You do that."

"You're going to regret this, Emmett. You have no idea what you're doing. You're going to throw away nineteen years over a crazy old drunk and a piece of paper."

"Nineteen years," I said. "You told our friends I was a loser."

"You are a loser! You smell like gas and you iron your blue jeans and you've never once in your life taken me anywhere that wasn't Rapid City!"

"Then you'll be happier with the $1.8 million," I said. "Oh, wait. You only get that if I die."

She hung up. I drove the rest of the way to Casper with my hands at ten and two and the heater in the box beside me, and the whole way I kept turning one thing over in my head. Somebody had known exactly where I'd slept Friday night. And it wasn't Tammy Burnett.

Tomas Leyva was waiting for me at the bulk plant when I pulled in a little after dark. Tomas is 29, drives my number two truck, and is the only man on my payroll who understands the security cameras I paid a fellow from Douglas to put up two winters ago after somebody stole a pallet of tank regulators.

"Boss," he said. "You need to see something."

"Can it wait till morning?"

"No, sir. I don't think it can."

He had the camera app pulled up on his phone. He scrolled back to that afternoon, while I'd been sitting at Glen Prater's kitchen table, and stopped on the camera that looks down the side of the office building, where the propane line runs from the little 100-pound cylinder out back to the wall heater in my office.

The time stamp said a little after three. Saturday, plant closed, nobody in the yard. A figure in a dark hooded jacket came along the wall, keeping low. Couldn't see a face. Couldn't see much of anything but a shape, a pair of gloves and something shiny in one hand. The figure crouched by the cylinder for maybe forty seconds. Then it was gone, back the way it came, out toward the alley.

I stood there in my own yard and felt the hair on my arms come up.

"I swung by around six to check the gate like you asked," Tomas said. "Office smelled like rotten eggs. Strong. I shut the cylinder and opened the windows and figured one of the trucks was venting." The rotten-egg smell is mercaptan, the stuff they put in propane so you can smell a leak. "Then I checked the camera."

"Show me the fitting."

We went around back with a flashlight. The cylinder was where it always was. I knelt down by the regulator and put the light on the pigtail where it threads into the tank valve.

Fresh wrench marks. Two flats. Bright brass. Backed off maybe a quarter turn.

The same marks I'd been looking at on Glen Prater's kitchen table six hours ago.

I sat back on my heels in the gravel and didn't say anything for a while.

"Boss?" Tomas said. "Boss, if you'd come back tonight and shut that door and lit the heater and laid down on that cot..."

"I know."

"With that heater running? In that little office?"

"I know, Tomas."

"That's not a leak. That's somebody trying to kill you."

I tightened the fitting myself, a quarter turn, with my own wrench, and I shut the cylinder valve, and I stood up.

"Get me a copy of that footage," I said. "On a thumb drive. And on your phone. And on mine. And email it to your sister, too."

"My sister?"

"Somebody who doesn't work here. In case something happens to the computer." I looked at the dark alley where the figure had come and gone. "Or to me."

Lieutenant Denise Archuleta of the Natrona County Sheriff's Office was 52, wore her hair in a gray braid down her back and had been working crimes against persons for 17 years. She sat in my office Monday morning with the window open, because I wasn't running the heater anymore, and she watched Tomas's footage three times without saying a word.

Then she looked at Glen's blue heater on my desk, and at the wrench marks on my own fitting, and at the bank statement with $412 highlighted in yellow on it.

"Here's what I've got," she said. "I've got a policy you say you didn't know about, but your signature's on it. I've got a man in a hood we can't identify. I've got a heater from a 2012 accident in Park County that the Park County sheriff already ruled an accident. And I've got two fittings with marks on them that a defense lawyer is going to say came from you."

"From me?"

"You're a propane man, Mr. Weller. You work on fittings all day. Your wrench marks are on every fitting in this county." She held up a hand before I could say anything. "I'm not saying I believe that. I'm saying that's what a lawyer says."

"So it's not enough."

"It's a lot. It's not enough." She leaned forward. "Who knows you've been sleeping out here?"

I thought about it.

"Tomas. My brother in Riverton. And everybody at that table Friday night saw me leave the restaurant without going home."

"So your wife knows. And whoever your wife tells."

"Russ Kincaid."

"I'm going to pull the Plains Mutual file with a subpoena," Archuleta said. "That takes a week, maybe two. I'm going to call Park County about that cabin. And I'm going to tell you something, and you're not going to like it." She stood up. "Don't confront them. Don't call them. Don't go near that house. Because right now the only thing between you and a second try is that they think you're scared."

She was right about one thing. I didn't like it.

And she was wrong about one thing, because I didn't have to go near that house. The house came to me.

About three that afternoon a young man in a Wranglers cap walked into my front office and asked for Emmett Weller, and when I said that was me, he handed me a manila envelope and said, "You've been served, sir. Have a good day."

I opened it at the counter while Tomas watched.

It was an ex parte protection order out of Natrona County district court. Heidi had gone in that morning and sworn out a petition saying I'd threatened her at the Branding Iron in front of witnesses, said I'd told her "you'll pay for this" and slammed my ring down on the table. The judge had granted a temporary order. I was to stay 500 feet away from her, the house and her office at High Plains Title until a hearing in two weeks.

At the bottom of the petition, under "Witnesses," she'd listed one name.

Russell Kincaid.

"She's lying," Tomas said, reading over my shoulder. "You didn't say any of that. Phil and Tammy were right there."

"Phil and Tammy aren't on the list."

"Why not?"

"Because they'd tell the truth."

I folded the order back up, and something in my chest went cold and slow and clear, the way it does when you're up on a tank in January and you realize the valve you're standing next to is frozen open.

They hadn't just tried to kill me Saturday afternoon. Now they'd fixed it so that if I died in an accident next week, everybody would remember I was the angry man who'd threatened his wife in a steakhouse and been ordered to stay away from her. A man like that, people would say, maybe he was drinking. Maybe he was careless with his own heater. Maybe he did it to himself.

And I couldn't go near my own house to get my own clothes.

"Boss," Tomas said. "There's a lady in the lot asking for you. She's been sitting in her car for twenty minutes."

I looked out the window.

It was a white Tahoe. And behind the wheel, with both hands on the steering wheel and her eyes swollen up like she'd been crying since Friday, sat Courtney Kincaid.

She wouldn't come inside. She said she didn't want anybody to see her car parked in front of my office for long. So I walked out and got in on the passenger side, and she handed me a red folder before I'd even shut the door.

"I found that in Russ's desk at home," she said. "Saturday morning, after he left for the office. He keeps the drawer locked. I know where the key is. I've known for twelve years."

I opened the folder.

The first thing in it was a copy of my Plains Mutual application, with a yellow sticky note on the front in a woman's handwriting. "Pages 3 and 7, have him initial, don't let him read 7." The handwriting was Heidi's. I've been looking at her grocery lists for 19 years.

Page 7 was the beneficiary designation.

The second thing in the folder was a letter on old Plains Mutual letterhead, dated 2012, to a Mrs. Heidi Prater of Cody, Wyoming, regretting to inform her that the accidental death benefit on her husband Glen's policy could not be paid, because her husband had not, in fact, died.

Somebody had circled "not, in fact, died" in pen and written one word next to it.

"Unlucky."

"That's Russ's writing," Courtney said, and her voice broke on it. "He's been doing her insurance since before I met him. I thought she was just a client. I thought... I don't know what I thought."

"Why are you giving me this?"

She turned and looked at me, and her mascara was all down her face, and she didn't care.

"Because he came home Saturday around suppertime," she said, "and he took a shower, and he put his jacket in the washing machine by itself. The dark one with the hood. Russ has never run the washing machine in his life." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "And because Tammy Burnett called me Sunday and said Heidi told her you'd be dead by Christmas and she'd be in Scottsdale. Tammy thought she was joking."

I sat there in Courtney Kincaid's Tahoe with the folder in my lap.

"Mrs. Kincaid," I said, "would you be willing to tell that to a sheriff's lieutenant?"

"I'll tell it to anybody." She took a breath. "But Emmett, Russ thinks you're going to the lieutenant. He thinks you're going to the lieutenant with whatever Glen gave you. He told Heidi on the phone last night, in the garage, when he thought I was asleep." She looked straight ahead out the windshield at my bulk tank. "He said, 'We have to do it before the hearing. After the hearing, it looks like revenge.'"

Lieutenant Archuleta read the red folder standing up at the counter of my front office, and when she got to the word "Unlucky" circled in pen, she said something under her breath in Spanish that I don't think was a prayer.

Courtney sat in my desk chair with a cup of my bad coffee and gave a statement for an hour and ten minutes into the lieutenant's recorder. When she was done, Archuleta clicked it off and looked at me.

"This is good," she said. "This is real good. The sticky note, the letter, the jacket in the wash, the remark to Tammy Burnett. It's a pattern. It's motive. It's two women's statements." She tapped the folder. "It's still not an attempt. Not one I can put in front of a jury with a straight face. A defense lawyer says the note means 'don't bore him with the fine print.' He says 'unlucky' was a joke in bad taste."

"So what do you need?"

"I need him with a wrench in his hand."

Nobody said anything. Tomas, who'd been leaning in the doorway, stood up straight.

"You want to use the boss as bait," he said.

"I want to catch a man in the act of doing something he's already decided to do," Archuleta said. "He told Heidi it has to happen before the hearing. The hearing's in thirteen days. He's going to try again, Mr. Weller, with or without me. I'd rather it was with me."

I thought about Glen Prater on the floor of a cabin up the South Fork, four in the afternoon, with an hour left. I thought about my office with the door shut.

"How?" I said.

"Who's the biggest mouth in your circle?"

"Tammy Burnett," Courtney and I said at the same time.

Tuesday afternoon I called Phil Burnett and asked if I could buy him a beer at the Wonder Bar, and I made sure he brought Tammy.

I told them I was sorry about the scene Friday. I told them the lawyer said I had to stay away from the house, so I was living at the plant till the hearing. And I told them, like it was nothing, that I'd let my third driver go for the season and I'd be running the Bar Nunn ranch route myself Monday morning in truck number three, starting at five, which meant I'd be at the plant alone Sunday night loading it.

"By yourself?" Tammy said. "Out there? At night?"

"Tomas is off. It's just me and the coyotes."

"Emmett, that's not safe."

"It's a propane plant, Tammy. What's going to happen?"

She put her hand on my arm, all sympathy, and I watched her eyes go far away, filing it. Phil just looked at his beer. I don't think Phil liked any of it, but Phil was the kind of man who'd let his wife carry the conversation for 30 years because it was easier than carrying it himself.

Tammy went to the ladies' room, and Phil turned his glass around on the bar a couple of times before he said anything.

"Emmett," he said. "About Friday."

"Forget it, Phil."

"No. I laughed. I slapped the table like an idiot. We've known you twenty years and I laughed when she called you a loser in front of the whole restaurant." He shook his head. "Tammy says Heidi's been saying stuff like that for a year. At book club. At the lake. I never said a word to you."

"Why not?"

"Because it was easier to laugh." He looked at me straight for the first time all night. "And because Heidi's the kind of person you don't want mad at you. You know?"

"I'm starting to."

"Is it true? What you said about the insurance?"

"Every word."

Phil was quiet a while. Then he leaned in, low.

"She asked Tammy last month what kind of heater you had in your office out there. Tammy thought she was going to buy you a new one for Christmas." He swallowed. "I didn't think anything of it till right now."

"Tell that to Lieutenant Archuleta, Phil. Tomorrow. Not Tammy. You."

"Okay." He nodded slowly. "Okay. I owe you that much."

Tammy came back, and Phil sat up and smiled like nothing had happened, and I paid for the beers.

Tammy called Heidi from the parking lot before Phil had even started the car. I know because Phil told me, years before any of this, that Tammy couldn't hold a secret long enough to get it to her car.

Sunday night it was 19 degrees and the wind was coming off Casper Mountain hard enough to rock the trucks.

I was in the cab of number three with the engine off and the dome light off, parked under the loading rack, the way I'd be if I were loading for an early run. Tomas was in the dark inside the office, with the camera app open on the computer and the door locked. Lieutenant Archuleta and two deputies were in an unmarked pickup a block down Industrial Avenue with the lights off, and two more were parked behind the feed store on the alley side.

I had a radio clipped inside my coat and a 24-inch pipe wrench on the seat next to me, because I'm not stupid.

A little before midnight, the radio clicked.

"Vehicle in the alley," Archuleta said, very quiet. "Dark SUV. Lights off. Stopped by the back fence. One occupant getting out. Second occupant staying in the vehicle, engine running."

"Can you see who's driving?" I whispered.

A long pause.

"Female. Blond. It's her, Emmett."

I'd known it would be. I'd known since Courtney said the word "we." It still went through me like cold water, knowing my wife was sitting in a running car in the alley behind my dad's propane plant, waiting for a man to walk out and tell her I'd be dead by morning.

The figure came over the back fence at the low spot by the cylinder cage. Dark hooded jacket. Gloves. Something long in one hand. He moved along the fence line, staying out of the yard light, and came up behind number three where I couldn't see him in the mirror.

Then I heard it.

Tink. Tink. Tink.

Metal on steel. A ring tapping on the side of the tank, the way a man taps when he's nervous and doesn't know he's doing it.

He crouched at the back of the truck by the hose reel and the delivery valve. I heard the wrench go on.

"He's at the truck," I said into the radio. "He's on the valve."

"Wait," Archuleta said. "Let him work. We need him working."

I counted. Ten seconds. Twenty. I heard the wrench slip and catch. I heard him breathing, short and fast. I smelled it then, faint and sharp, rotten eggs in the cold air.

Gas doesn't leak by itself.

"Now," Archuleta said.

I opened the cab door and stepped down into the yard light.

"Evening, Russ," I said.

He came up off his knees so fast he hit his head on the hose reel. The hood fell back. Russ Kincaid, with his good haircut flattened by a stocking cap and an eighteen-inch pipe wrench in his right hand and his face as white as the frost on the tank.

"Emmett," he said. "Emmett, I... I saw the gate open. I thought somebody was..."

"Over the back fence?"

"I was checking..."

"You were backing off the delivery valve on a truck I'm supposed to drive at five in the morning with a full load," I said. "One spark from the starter and they'd have found pieces of me in Bar Nunn."

His eyes went to the alley. To the running SUV he couldn't see from here.

"She's not going to help you, Russ."

"You don't know anything!"

"I know she wrote 'don't let him read page seven' on a sticky note. I know you wrote 'unlucky' on Glen Prater's letter. I know you put your jacket in the wash Saturday night, because your wife told the sheriff."

"Courtney?" His face crumpled and then went hard. "Courtney did this?"

"No. You did this."

He came at me with the wrench.
