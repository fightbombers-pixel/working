"Finally, a real man," my wife announced, and she said it with her arms still around his neck.

She said it in the back room of the Elks Lodge in Mankato, Minnesota, at her own 40th birthday party, in front of 70 people I had personally fed. I know it was 70 because I paid the caterer by the head, $22 a plate, and I'd signed the check that morning.

The man she was holding onto had walked in the side door two minutes earlier with a bouquet of red roses the size of a car tire. He'd crossed the room like he owned it, and she'd met him halfway, and she'd kissed him. Not a peck. A long one, the kind you don't do in front of your husband's auction customers and your mother's church friends.

Then she turned around, flushed and happy, and told the whole room what she thought of the man she'd been married to for eleven years.

I was sitting at the head table with a slice of sheet cake in front of me. Vanilla, with purple frosting that said HAPPY 40TH EILEEN in cursive. I looked at the cake and I looked at my wife and I thought about the $48,000 that had left our home equity line in October.

Then I stood up.

I reached inside my sport coat, took out a plain white envelope, and walked across the dance floor. Seventy people watched me do it. Somebody's chair scraped. The DJ, a kid named Corey who'd been playing Prince all night, actually turned the music down.

I held the envelope out to the man with the roses.

"Congratulations," I said. "She's all yours."

He looked at me with this little smile, the kind a guy gives you when he's sure he already won. He was wearing sunglasses indoors, at night, in November. He took the envelope with the hand that wasn't holding roses, and he slid one finger under the flap like he was opening a fan letter.

He read the first page. Then he read it again.

The roses hit the floor.

Ninety seconds later, he wasn't in the building anymore, and my wife was standing in the middle of the dance floor in a sequined dress, holding a man's sunglasses, screaming my name.

What she didn't know was that there was a second envelope in my inside pocket, and it had her name on it.

My name is Homer Cash. I'm 52, and I run Cash Auction Company out of a pole barn on Highway 22, just south of Mankato. Estate sales, farm sales, the occasional storage unit somebody stopped paying for. I've been calling bids for 26 years.

Here's what that job teaches you. People lie with their mouths all day long, but their hands tell you the truth. A man who says he's not interested in the walnut dresser is already holding his bid card a little too tight. Hold on to that.

Because the man with the roses had very busy hands.

He'd gone out the kitchen door. I heard it bang against the dumpster, and then I heard an engine turn over in the back lot, and I knew the sound of that engine, because I'd been paying for it for three years.

"That's my car," Eileen said. Then louder. "Homer, that's my car! What did you give him? What did you put in that envelope?"

"His name," I said.

"I know his name!"

"No," I said. "You don't."

Her sister Nita came pushing through the crowd. Nita is four years older than Eileen, works nights at the hospital, and has never once in her life pretended to like a man just to be polite. She grabbed my arm.

"Homer," she said, and she was keeping her voice low. "Her suitcases are in that car."

I looked at her.

"Two suitcases and the blue garment bag," Nita said. "I watched her load them this afternoon. She told me she was leaving with him tonight. After the cake."

Out in the back lot, tires squealed on the gravel, and then they weren't squealing anymore, because they were on the highway.

Three weeks earlier, a widow named Velma Fitch had backed her Buick up to my loading dock with a trunk full of her late husband's duck decoys.

It was a Tuesday, cold and gray, the kind of October day when the corn's mostly in and everybody in the county is thinking about deer season. Velma was 71, wore a red quilted vest, and had the sharpest eyes I'd seen on anybody in a long time.

"Harold carved every one of these," she said, lifting a mallard out of a cardboard box. "Fifty-one years. I want them sold to people who'll put them on a shelf, not in a dumpster."

"I'll put them in the spring sale," I said. "Decoys do well in March. Guys have money after tax refunds."

"You know your business."

"I know my crowd."

My ringman, Arlo Pinkney, was stacking boxes behind me. Arlo is about six-foot-four and thirty years old and has a voice that can carry across a fairground without a microphone. He also eats like a combine.

"Mrs. Fitch," he said, "you want coffee? Homer makes it so strong you could stand a spoon in it."

"I'll take some," Velma said. "I drove from Dubuque this morning. My sister's place. I've been down there a month."

"Dubuque," I said. "That's a haul."

"I had to help her with the lawyers." Velma set the mallard down and looked at it for a second. "Somebody took her for ninety thousand dollars."

Arlo stopped stacking.

"Took her how?" I asked.

"Art," Velma said. She said the word like it tasted bad. "A man came to her church's fall bazaar with paintings. Very charming. Very handsome. He told her she had an eye. He told her original paintings appreciate faster than the stock market and she should buy into his gallery. Ruth's eighty. She doesn't know art from wallpaper. By August her savings were gone and so was he."

"Did the police get him?"

"They know who he is. A detective named Calvert has been after him all summer. But Ruth had a stroke in September." Velma's mouth went tight. "She can't testify now. She can barely hold a fork. Detective Calvert says without a victim who can sit in a courtroom, he walks."

She went to her car and came back with a folded newspaper clipping from the Dubuque Telegraph Herald. She handed it to me like it was evidence, which I suppose it was.

The headline said, POLICE SEEK MAN IN SENIOR ART SCAM.

There was a photo underneath. A man at a church folding table, holding up a painting of a red barn and smiling at the camera. Good teeth. Silver at the temples. Expensive haircut.

I knew that face.

I knew it because it was on a flyer stuck to my refrigerator with a magnet shaped like an ear of corn. JULIAN BOOTH STUDIO, OLD TOWN MANKATO. EVENING PAINTING CLASSES FOR THE SOUL. Eileen had brought it home in June.

"Homer?" Arlo said. "You okay? You look like somebody bid you up on a lot you didn't want."

"What name did he use?" I asked Velma.

"In Dubuque? Jules Bodine. Calvert says his real name is Gerald Bodine. He's used a few."

I read the caption again. I read it a third time, because I've learned in my business that the third read is the one where you actually see the thing.

It said the man in the photo had ties to galleries in Des Moines and Rochester.

"Mrs. Fitch," I said, "do you have Detective Calvert's number?"

She looked at me for a long time. Then she opened her purse.

"You've seen him," she said.

"I think my wife takes painting lessons from him."

Velma Fitch put her hand flat on the tailgate of her Buick, the way you steady yourself on a fence post.

"Then you'd better check your bank account," she said, "before you do anything else."

I didn't call the detective right away. I drove home first.

Eileen's car was gone, which on a Tuesday night meant painting class. The spare fob for her Acadia wasn't on its hook by the garage door either, which I noticed the way you notice a missing lot number, and then forgot about. Tuesday and Thursday, six to nine, for the last five months. She'd come home with canvases of sunsets and covered bridges and once, God help me, a portrait of me that made me look like a surprised walrus. She'd been so happy about those classes that I'd been happy for her. I'd framed the walrus.

I stood in the kitchen and looked at the flyer on the fridge. Julian Booth smiling over a stack of canvases.

Then I opened the laptop at the kitchen table and logged into our accounts.

Checking was fine. Savings was fine. My business account was fine, because Eileen doesn't have the password and never asked for it.

Then I opened the home equity line.

We took that line out four years ago to put a new roof on the barn and redo the bathroom. It had a $60,000 limit. Last time I'd looked, in the summer, the balance was about $11,000.

The balance was $59,212.

There was one draw on October 9th. Forty-eight thousand dollars, by check. I clicked on the image of the check, and there it was, in my wife's round handwriting.

Pay to the order of Booth Fine Art LLC. Forty-eight thousand and 00/100. In the memo line she'd written: Partnership. Our future!

With an exclamation point.

I sat there and looked at that exclamation point for a long time. The furnace kicked on. The fridge hummed. Somewhere out on Highway 22 a grain truck downshifted.

I'll tell you something about auctions. The worst moment isn't when a lot sells for nothing. The worst moment is when you realize a lot you thought you knew was never what you thought it was. You've been carrying it around, cataloging it, putting it in the sale bill, and it was a reproduction the whole time.

I took my phone out and dialed the number Velma had written on the back of her receipt.

It rang four times.

"Calvert."

"Detective, my name is Homer Cash. I'm an auctioneer in Mankato, Minnesota. Velma Fitch gave me your number."

There was a pause. I heard a chair creak on the other end.

"Ruth Kemper's sister," Calvert said. Her voice was flat and tired, a woman who'd been on the phone with a lot of people who didn't have anything useful to say. "What can I do for you, Mr. Cash?"

"The man in your newspaper photo," I said. "He's calling himself Julian Booth. He runs a painting studio in Old Town Mankato. He's been teaching my wife for five months."

The chair stopped creaking.

"Say that again."

"Julian Booth. Booth Fine Art LLC. And three weeks ago my wife wrote him a check for forty-eight thousand dollars off our home equity line."

I heard her breathe out. Then I heard her pick up a pen.

"Mr. Cash," Detective Renee Calvert said, "is your name on that home equity line?"

"It's my house. I bought it before I married her."

"Then listen to me very carefully." She paused. "Don't tell your wife anything yet. Don't go near him. And don't, under any circumstances, let that money move again."

That was when I heard Eileen's car pull into the driveway, an hour before her class should have let out.

I closed the laptop. I had about four seconds, and I used them to put the flyer back exactly the way it was, one corner under the corn magnet.

Eileen came in through the garage door with a canvas under her arm and her cheeks pink from the cold. She was wearing the paint-spattered denim shirt she called her studio shirt. She smelled like turpentine and something else, a cologne I didn't own.

"You're home early," I said.

"Julian had to cut it short. Some gallery person from Minneapolis." She leaned the canvas against the wall. It was a covered bridge again. "What are you doing on the computer?"

"Looking at sale bills."

"At the kitchen table?"

"The barn's cold."

She looked at me a second longer than she needed to. Then she kissed the top of my head, the way you'd pat a dog on the way past, and went upstairs to shower.

I sat there and listened to the water run, and I thought about how many times she'd showered the second she got home from painting class, and how I'd never once thought about it.

The next morning I was in the office of Ada Kerrigan by eight o'clock.

Ada has been my lawyer since I bought the barn. She's in her sixties, wears reading glasses on a beaded chain, and talks like every word costs her a nickel. Her office is above a jewelry store on Front Street, and you can hear the store's door chime every time somebody walks in downstairs.

She read the printout of the check. She read the HELOC statement. She took her glasses off and let them drop on the chain.

"Your house," she said.

"Bought it in 2009. Two years before we got married."

"Line's in both names?"

"The bank wanted both signatures."

"So the debt's joint," Ada said, "but the collateral is your separate property. She borrowed forty-eight grand against a house that isn't hers and handed it to a man who isn't her husband."

"That about sums it up."

"Minnesota is no-fault, Homer. The judge isn't going to care who she slept with." The door chime rang downstairs. "But the judge is going to care very much about forty-eight thousand dollars of marital debt spent on a boyfriend. That's dissipation. It comes out of her side of the ledger."

"I don't care about the ledger right now. I care about the money."

"Then call the bank today and freeze that line. Both signers can freeze it. Only both can unfreeze it." She wrote something on a yellow pad. "And this detective. What does she need?"

"A victim who can testify."

Ada tapped her pen.

"You're a co-borrower on the line he got paid from," she said. "You're out forty-eight thousand dollars on a note with your name on it. You understand what that makes you?"

"Mad," I said.

"A victim," Ada said. "With a pulse and a good memory. Does your wife know you know?"

"Not yet."

"Keep it that way as long as you can stand it." She put her glasses back on. "And Homer. Don't hit him."

"I've never hit anybody in my life."

"That's what they all say right before," Ada said.

I called First National from her parking lot and froze the line. The woman at the bank asked me if I wanted to notify the other borrower. I said no, thank you, she'd find out on her own.

She found out on Friday.

I was in the barn, tagging lots for a farm sale in Waseca, when Eileen came through the side door with her phone in her hand and her face red.

"Did you do something to the home equity line?"

I set down my tag gun.

"I froze it."

"You froze it." She said it like I'd told her I set fire to the house. "Why would you freeze it?"

"Because somebody took forty-eight thousand dollars out of it in October and didn't mention it to me."

That stopped her. Just for a second, but it stopped her.

"That was an investment," she said.

"In what?"

"In Julian's gallery. Booth Fine Art. He's opening a second location in Minneapolis, in the North Loop, and he offered me a partnership. A real one, Homer. My name on the door."

"Your name on the door."

"Eileen Cash Fine Art," she said, and her chin came up. "Do you know what that means to me? I've spent eleven years watching you sell dead people's junk out of a pole barn. This is the first thing in my life that's mine."

"It's not yours. It's forty-eight thousand dollars of my house."

"Our house."

"My house. I bought it in 2009. You moved in in 2013. Your name's on the loan, not the deed."

She looked at me like she'd never seen me before.

"He said you'd be like this," she said.

"Did he."

"He said men like you can't stand it when a woman finds something of her own. He said you'd try to control the money."

"Eileen, the money's gone. I'm not controlling it. I'm trying to find it."

"It's not gone! It's in paintings!"

"Which paintings?"

"Originals! Twelve original oils. He's going to sell them in the North Loop for six, eight thousand apiece. That's how a gallery works, Homer. You buy inventory, you sell it for more."

"Have you seen these twelve originals?"

"I've seen three of them. They're beautiful."

"Can I see them?"

"Why? So you can put a price tag on them?"

"That's my job," I said. "I put price tags on things. I've put price tags on art for 26 years. I know what an original oil looks like and I know what a print with a coat of varnish looks like."

She laughed. It wasn't a nice laugh.

"You think you know art? You sell decoys and tractors."

"I've sold four estates in this county with real paintings in them. A Dewey Albinson went for nineteen thousand in my barn in 2016. I know what I'm looking at."

"Julian has a master's degree from the Art Institute of Chicago."

"Does he."

"Yes, he does!"

"Did you see the diploma?"

She opened her mouth and nothing came out.

I want to be fair to her. Eileen wasn't stupid. She was lonely, and she was 39 when she started those classes, and she'd told me more than once that she could've been something if she hadn't gotten married young the first time and then married me. A man who tells a woman like that she has a gift doesn't have to be a genius. He only has to be the first one to say it.

"I'm not doing this," she said. "Unfreeze the line."

"No."

"Homer."

"No."

"Then I'll go to the bank myself."

"Go ahead. It takes both signatures to unfreeze it. Ask them."

She stood there breathing hard. Then she said the thing she'd come out to the barn to say.

"I'm inviting Julian to my party."

"Your birthday party."

"Two weeks from tomorrow. At the Elks. I already told him. He's bringing a guest, and he's going to say a few words about my work, and you're going to be polite about it, because if you're not, everybody in that room is going to see exactly what kind of small, jealous man you are."

She walked out. The barn door banged behind her and rattled on its track.

Arlo came around the end of the tag table, where he'd been the whole time pretending to count folding chairs.

"Boss," he said. "I counted the chairs four times."

"How many are there?"

"No idea." He scratched his jaw. "You want me to go down to Old Town and have a conversation with this Picasso?"

"No," I said. "I'm going to go have one myself."

Julian Booth Studio was on the second floor of an old brick building on North Riverfront Drive, over a vacuum repair shop. I went up the next Tuesday at a quarter to six, before class started. I wore my auction clothes, khakis and a barn coat, and I brought one thing with me in my pocket: a jeweler's loupe, ten-power, the one I use on signatures and hallmarks.

The studio was nice. I'll give him that. Big windows over the river, track lights, a dozen easels in a semicircle. The walls were covered in paintings with little white cards next to them. RED BARN AT DUSK, $4,800. MINNESOTA RIVER, OCTOBER, $6,200. SILO STUDY NUMBER 3, $3,900.

Julian was in the back, setting out brushes. He was taller than I expected. He turned around and he knew who I was before I said a word. I watched his hands. He put the brushes down slowly and then put his hands in his pockets.

"Mr. Cash," he said. "Eileen's husband. What a nice surprise."

"I came to look at the inventory."

"The inventory?"

"The twelve originals. The ones my wife bought into."

He smiled. Good teeth, like the newspaper picture.

"I'm afraid that's a matter between me and my business partner," he said.

"Your business partner borrowed the money against my house, so I figure I'm a silent partner. I'd like to see what I paid for."

"Those pieces are in storage in Minneapolis."

"All twelve?"

"All twelve."

"Then let's look at these." I walked over to RED BARN AT DUSK, $4,800, and took the loupe out of my pocket.

"Please don't touch the work," Julian said.

"I won't touch it. I'm just going to look."

I put the loupe to my eye and leaned in close to the corner of the barn, where the red paint met the gray sky.

There it was. I didn't even have to hunt. Underneath the shiny brushstrokes on top, there was a grid of tiny colored dots. Cyan, magenta, yellow, black. The pattern you get from an inkjet printer. Somebody had printed a photograph of a barn onto canvas and then gone over the high spots with clear gel so it'd catch the light like paint.

I moved to the river painting. Same dots.

I moved to the silo. Same dots.

"Mr. Cash, I'm going to have to ask you to leave."

"These are giclees," I said. "Prints. Somebody ran them through a wide-format printer and dabbed some gloss on top. You'd get maybe forty bucks apiece for them at my sale, and that's with a nice frame."

"You're an auctioneer," Julian said. The smile was gone now. "You sell used lawn mowers."

"I sell whatever people bring me. And I know the difference between a painting and a picture of one." I put the loupe back in my pocket. "My wife thinks she bought twelve originals for forty-eight thousand dollars. What did she buy, Julian? Or should I say Gerald?"

His hands came out of his pockets.

That's the thing I noticed. Not his face. His hands. They came out of his pockets fast, and they were fists.

"Get out of my studio."

"I'm going. Tell my wife I said hello when she comes in at six."

I turned toward the door, and he shoved me. Two hands, square in the back, hard. I went into an easel and the easel went into another easel and three canvases slapped on the hardwood floor.

I'm not a fighter. I've never been one. But I've spent 26 years loading dressers and gun safes and cast-iron cookstoves onto trailers, and when I came up off that easel I had my hands on his coat, and I walked him backward until his shoulders hit the wall under SILO STUDY NUMBER 3.

He swung at me once with his right. I saw it coming a mile off. I caught his wrist, turned it, and he went down to one knee with a sound like a stepped-on accordion.

I held it there for a second. Long enough for him to understand.

Then I let go and stepped back with my hands open.

"You pushed me," I said. "Remember that, if anybody asks."

He stayed on one knee, holding his wrist, breathing through his teeth. The sunglasses had come off his head and skittered under an easel.

"You're making a mistake," he said. "You have no idea who you're dealing with."

"Gerald Bodine. Jules Bodine. Julian Booth. I've got a pretty good idea."

He looked up at me then, and he laughed. It was the first honest sound I'd heard out of him.

"You really think she'll believe you?" he said. "Your wife is in love with me, Mr. Cash. She told me she's leaving you. Right after her birthday. In front of everybody." He got to his feet, slowly, still holding his wrist. "I'm going to be at that party. And on Saturday night, when I leave, I'm taking your wife with me."

Behind me, on the stairs, I heard footsteps. Light ones. A woman's.

I turned around, and Eileen was standing in the doorway with her canvas bag over her shoulder, looking at the paintings on the floor.

"What happened?" she said. "Homer, what did you do?"

"He came in here and started breaking things," Julian said, before I could open my mouth. He was holding his wrist up so she could see it. "I asked him to leave and he attacked me."

"He shoved me into an easel," I said.

"Look at his hands, Eileen," Julian said. "Look at mine."

She looked at my hands. They were big and scarred and had a smear of somebody's cheap gel varnish on the knuckles. She looked at his, long and soft and curled up against his chest like a hurt bird.

That was the end of that.

"Get out," she said to me.

"Eileen, the paintings are prints. Take my loupe. Look at the corner of any one of them. You'll see the dots."

"Get out!"

"I'll leave the loupe on the table."

"I don't want your stupid magnifying glass! I want you out of here!" Her voice cracked. "You couldn't let me have one thing. One thing, Homer. Eleven years and I never asked you for anything, and the minute I find something I'm good at, you come down here and humiliate me in front of my teacher."

"He's not your teacher. He's not even Julian."

"Out."

I set the loupe on the paint table next to a coffee can full of brushes. I walked past her down the stairs. Behind me I heard Julian say something soft, and I heard her start to cry, and I heard him tell her it was all right, it was all right now, he was here.

She didn't come home that night. She texted me at eleven. Staying at Nita's. Don't call.

Nita texted me a few minutes later. She's not here. Just so you know.

I sat on the edge of the bed for a long time with both of those texts on the screen. Then I got up, went down to the kitchen, and took the flyer off the refrigerator.

Detective Calvert called me back Wednesday morning, and she sounded different. Less tired.

"I pulled the Booth Fine Art LLC filing with the Minnesota Secretary of State," she said. "Registered agent is a mailbox in Rochester. Same mailbox Bodine used for a company in Iowa last year. That's a pattern, Mr. Cash. Juries love patterns."

"Does it get you a warrant?"

"Ruth's bank records, plus your check, plus your statement, plus the mailbox. My county attorney thinks it does. He wants your statement in writing and notarized. Can you get to Dubuque?"

"I can be there tomorrow."

"Bring the check image and your HELOC paperwork. And Mr. Cash." She paused. "His lease on that studio is month to month. His apartment on Warren Street, the landlord says he gave notice last week. He's packing up. Whatever he's planning, it's soon."

"My wife's birthday is a week from Saturday," I said. "He told me he's coming. He told me he's taking her with him when he leaves."

Calvert was quiet.

"Then that's where we'll be," she said.

I drove to Dubuque on Thursday. Three and a half hours down 52 through all those little river towns. Velma Fitch met me in the lobby of the Dubuque County Law Enforcement Center wearing the same red vest, and she sat with me while I gave my statement to Calvert and a county attorney with a bow tie. When it was over, Velma took my hand in both of hers.

"Ruth asked me to tell you something," she said. "She can't say a lot of words right now, but she made me write it down." She unfolded an index card. In shaky block letters it said: HE CALLED ME GIFTED.

I folded the card and put it in my wallet. It's still there.

The warrant was signed the next Friday afternoon, the day before the party. Theft in the first degree and ongoing criminal conduct, State of Iowa. Calvert called me from her car.

"It's in the national system," she said. "Any cop in the country who runs his name gets a hit. I've talked to a Sergeant Hamby at Mankato PD. They don't know where Bodine's sleeping. The apartment's empty. The studio's dark. Your party is the only place anybody knows he'll be."

"What time?"

"Hamby will have a car at the Elks by nine. He asked me to ask you one thing. Don't spook him. Keep him in the building until they walk in."

"Understood."

"I mean it, Mr. Cash. Don't play hero."

That night I sat at the kitchen table and made two envelopes.

In the first one I put the clipping from the Telegraph Herald, a copy of Eileen's check with the exclamation point, and a page printed from the Iowa courts website showing a warrant case number next to the name Gerald Joseph Bodine. On top I put a note in my own handwriting: Detective Calvert says hello. Mankato police are on their way.

In the second one I put a petition for dissolution of marriage that Ada Kerrigan had drawn up, and a copy of the letter from First National confirming the line was frozen. On the front I wrote: Eileen.

I put them both in the inside pocket of my sport coat and hung it on the bedroom door.

So that's how I ended up at the head table at the Elks with purple frosting on my fork, watching Julian Booth walk in the side door at twenty minutes to nine with a bouquet of roses.

Twenty minutes early.

I watched my wife kiss him in front of my customers and my mother-in-law. I heard her call him a real man. I heard seventy people go so quiet you could hear the ice machine in the bar.

And I made the mistake that cost me the whole plan. I didn't wait. I couldn't. I got up and handed him that envelope, and when he read the words Mankato police, he did exactly what Calvert told me not to let him do.

He ran.

Sergeant Hamby's car pulled into the front lot of the Elks four minutes after Eileen's GMC Acadia went out the back. I was standing on the front steps when he got out. He was a heavyset man in his fifties with a gray mustache and the expression of a guy whose Saturday was not going the way he'd hoped.

"You Cash?"

"Yes, sir."

"Where is he?"

"Gone. Out the kitchen door in my wife's car. White GMC Acadia. Minnesota plate starting with EGR."

Hamby looked at me the way my father used to look at me when I backed the hay wagon into the fence.

"The detective told you not to spook him."

"I know."

"So you spooked him."

"I handed him an envelope. He spooked himself."

Hamby sighed through his mustache and got on his radio. While he talked, Eileen came out the front door behind me. Nita was right behind her. Eileen still had his sunglasses in her hand, and her mascara had run, and she'd lost one of her shoes somewhere between the dance floor and the steps.

"Where is he going?" she said. "Homer. Where is he going?"

"I don't know."

"He's got my bags! He's got my purse! My phone's in my purse!" She turned to Hamby. "Officer, that man has my car."

"Ma'am, did you give him the keys?"

She hesitated.

"I gave him my spare fob," she said. "Last month. So he could move his paintings."

Hamby made a note. I thought about the empty hook by our garage door, and the lot number I'd forgotten about.

"If you gave him the key, it's not stolen, ma'am," Hamby said. "It's a civil matter. We can still pick him up on the Iowa warrant, but we have to find him first."

"It's not her car," I said.

They both looked at me.

"The Acadia's titled to Cash Auction Company," I said. "I bought it through the business three years ago for the tax write-off. Company vehicle. I'm the sole owner of the company, and I never gave that man permission to drive anything of mine."

Hamby looked at me for a second. Then the corner of his mustache went up.

"Is it registered with OnStar?"

"Paid through the business account."

"Call them," Hamby said. "Right now. Tell them you're reporting a stolen vehicle and Mankato PD is standing next to you."

I called. A young woman named Tasha answered, and Hamby took the phone from me and read her his badge number, and four minutes later, she came back on the line.

"The vehicle is stationary," she said. "It's at the Kwik Trip on North Minnesota Avenue in St. Peter. Engine's running."

St. Peter is twelve miles north on 169. He was gassing up. He'd driven off with a woman's suitcases and her purse, and he'd stopped for gas, because a man like that always assumes he's got more time than he does.

Hamby was already on the radio to Nicollet County.

"I'm going," I said.

"You're going to stay here, Mr. Cash."

"Sergeant, that's my vehicle and my wife's purse and forty-eight thousand dollars of my house. I'm going to drive up there and sit in my truck across the street and I'm not going to get out until you tell me I can."

He looked at me for a long moment.

"Across the street," he said. "In your truck. Engine off."

"Yes, sir."

"I'm coming with you," Eileen said.

I turned around. She was standing on one shoe with Julian's sunglasses in her hand.

"Eileen, I don't think..."

"I'm coming," she said. "I want to hear him say it."

Nita drove. I rode shotgun in my own truck and Eileen sat in the back seat behind me and didn't say a word for twelve miles. The Minnesota River was black on our right. The radio was on low, Bob Seger singing about night moves, and Nita reached over and turned it off without asking.

When we came over the hill into St. Peter, I could see the lights before I could see the gas station. Blue and red, bouncing off the canopy over the pumps.

Nita pulled into the lot of the Dairy Queen across the street, and she shut the engine off, and the three of us sat there and watched.

There were two Nicollet County cruisers and Hamby's car, angled in around pump number six so the white Acadia couldn't go forward or back. The driver's door was open. Julian Booth was standing beside it with his hands on the roof and a deputy patting him down. He was still wearing his good blazer. One of the roses had gotten caught in the lapel when he ran, and it was hanging there upside down.

Hamby waved at us across the street. Then he held up his hand with all five fingers open. Five minutes.

Eileen was out of the truck before Nita could stop her.

She walked across North Minnesota Avenue in one high heel and one bare foot, in a sequined dress, in November, and nobody stopped her. I got out and followed her, because that's what you do.

The deputy had Julian cuffed by the time we got there. Another deputy had the back hatch of the Acadia open. Eileen's two suitcases were in there, and the blue garment bag, and next to them a black duffel bag that wasn't hers.

The deputy unzipped the duffel and whistled.

It was cash. Rubber-banded stacks of twenties and fifties, the way a bank gives it to you when you take out more than they like. Underneath the stacks was a manila folder.

"Sergeant," the deputy said, "you want to look at this."

Hamby came around the back. He flipped through the folder under the canopy lights. There was a stack of canvas-print invoices from a company in Iowa, forty-two dollars apiece. There was a printout of a boarding pass.

Hamby read it out loud, flat, like he was reading a parking ticket.

"Delta 1471. Minneapolis to Phoenix. Departing six a.m. One passenger. Bodine, Gerald J."

Eileen took the boarding pass out of his hand. She read it under the lights. She turned it over like there might be another name on the back.

"Where's mine?" she said.

Julian didn't answer. He was looking at the pavement.

"Julian." Her voice was very small. "Where's my ticket?"

"There isn't one," I said.

"I'm asking him."

She walked around the side of the car until she was standing right in front of him. The deputy holding his arm stepped back half a step, the way people do when they can tell something's about to happen that isn't their business.

"You told me Phoenix," she said. "You told me the gallery in Scottsdale. You told me we'd drive out after the party and fly out of Minneapolis in the morning and I'd never have to look at another corn field. You told me that."

"Eileen..."

"Where is my ticket?"

He looked up then. The sunglasses were gone and the smile was gone and what was left was a tired man in his late forties with a rose stuck upside down in his lapel.

"You were never getting on that plane," he said.

She made a sound. I don't know how to describe it. It wasn't crying. It was the sound a person makes when they reach for a step that isn't there.

"The bags," she said. "You let me pack."

"You'd have noticed if you didn't," Julian said. "I needed you to keep smiling until I got the car."

"How much is in there?" I asked the deputy.

He held up a stack and riffled it with his thumb.

"Rough count, thirty, thirty-one thousand. We'll do it right at the station."

"Thirty-one," I said to Julian. "Out of forty-eight. Where's the rest?"

He shrugged with his hands cuffed behind him. "Rent. Canvas. Roses aren't free."

Hamby took him by the elbow.

"Gerald Bodine," he said, "you're under arrest on a warrant out of Dubuque County, Iowa, and for possession of a stolen motor vehicle in the State of Minnesota."

"It's not stolen. She gave me the key."

"It's not her car," Hamby said, and he looked at me, and I swear the mustache went up again.

They put him in the back of the Nicollet County cruiser. He looked out the window at me as they shut the door, and for once I didn't need to see his hands to know what he was thinking.

Eileen was standing beside the open hatch of the Acadia, holding the boarding pass. The wind off the river had picked up and her hair was blowing across her face and she didn't move it.

"Homer," she said. "Please. Take me home."

I didn't answer right away. I reached into the inside pocket of my sport coat, and I took out the second envelope, the one with her name on the front in my handwriting.

She looked at it. She knew what it was. I watched her hands, and they were shaking.

"You knew," she said. "You knew all this before tonight. Why didn't you tell me?"

"I did tell you. In his studio. I left you my loupe."

She closed her eyes.

"The paintings," she said. "Were any of them real?"

"Not one."

"And me? Was I real to him? Even a little?"

I thought about Ruth Kemper in Dubuque, eighty years old, holding a fork with both hands. I thought about the index card in my wallet.

"He told a woman in Iowa she was gifted," I said. "She gave him ninety thousand dollars. He told you you could've been something. You gave him forty-eight."

I held out the envelope. After a long time, she took it.

"Homer, I'm begging you," she said. "I'll do anything. I'll go to counseling. I'll sell my car. I'll pay back every cent. Just don't do this here. Not in front of everybody."

Across the street, a few cars had pulled over. A couple of kids in the Dairy Queen lot had their phones up. Nita was leaning against my truck with her arms folded.

"You did it in front of seventy people," I said. "With a microphone and a cake."

"I was wrong."

"You were," I said. "You told everybody what a real man looks like, Eileen."

I nodded at the cruiser pulling out onto North Minnesota Avenue, its lights still going, with Gerald Bodine in the back seat and a red rose hanging off his collar.

"There he goes," I said, and I walked back across the street to my truck.
