"I put peanuts in your dinner to prove you're faking your allergy. You're just picky."

My fiancée said that, laughing, at my own dining room table on a Saturday night in Pensacola, in front of seven of her friends, while I was still chewing the second bite of her shrimp étouffée. She said it holding up her wineglass, like a toast. Two of her girlfriends clapped.

I stopped chewing.

I've been allergic to peanuts since I was four years old. I've carried an EpiPen for fifty-five years. I carried one through twenty-two years in the United States Navy, on four ships and two deployments, in a little zippered pouch that my chief made fun of until the day I used it on a seaman from Ohio who'd been stung by something in the Persian Gulf.

My lips started to tingle. Then my tongue. Then the back of my throat felt like somebody had put a hand around it and started to squeeze.

I spat the shrimp into my napkin. I stood up. My chair went over backward on the tile.

"Oh, here we go," Danielle said, rolling her eyes at her friends. "The big show."

I couldn't talk. I already couldn't talk. So I took my phone out of my shirt pocket and typed three words to my daughter Celia, who lives eight minutes away in Gulf Breeze.

Call 911. Now.

Then I went for the kitchen drawer by the stove, where my EpiPen has lived in the same spot for the three years I've owned this house.

The drawer was empty.

I turned around. Danielle was standing in the kitchen doorway in her apron, the one that said KISS THE COOK, with her wineglass in her hand. Behind her, her friend Shawna was holding up a phone, filming.

"I moved it," Danielle said. "Because you don't need it. You're going to see that you don't need it."

Two hours later, my fiancée would be sitting in the ER waiting room at Baptist Hospital, still in that apron, with a police officer standing over her and her hands behind her back.

What she didn't know, standing in my kitchen doorway, was that her friend Shawna's phone was still recording. Danielle had asked her to film the whole thing for their group chat. The moment I admitted I was faking. She'd wanted proof.

She got it.

My name is Phillip Alcorn. I'm 59 years old. I was a chief boatswain's mate in the Navy for twenty-two years, and for the last fifteen I've been a harbor pilot in Pensacola Bay. When a big ship comes into port, I climb up a rope ladder on the side of it in the dark and take it through the channel, because the captain doesn't know where the sandbars are and I do. The first thing you learn as a pilot is that you never trust what you can't see. You don't trust the water because it looks calm. You check the chart. You always have a backup.

Hold on to that.

My truck was in the driveway. My spare EpiPen was in the glove box, in the same little zippered pouch, where it's been since 1991. I always have two. Always.

I went out the side door. I don't remember the steps down to the driveway. I remember the sound of my own breathing, high and thin, like a teakettle. I remember the dome light coming on when I yanked the truck door open. I remember fumbling the glove box latch twice.

I got the pouch. I got the pen out. I pulled the blue cap with my teeth and jammed the orange end into my thigh through my khakis, the way I've taught a hundred sailors, and held it there and counted to three.

Then I sat down on the running board of my truck and waited to find out if I was going to live.

The breathing came back first. Not all the way. Just enough. Then I heard sirens on Scenic Highway, coming fast.

The EMTs were two young guys from Escambia County Fire Rescue. One of them was kneeling in front of me with an oxygen mask before the truck had stopped rolling. The other one was on the radio.

"Sir, what did you eat?"

I couldn't talk well yet. I pointed back at the house. Then I held up one finger, wait, and I reached up and took a plastic Tupperware container off the passenger seat of my truck.

I'd grabbed it off the counter on my way out the kitchen. The leftover étouffée she'd been planning to send home with her friends. I'd snapped the lid on with one hand while I was going out the door. I don't really remember doing it. Twenty-two years in the Navy. You bring the evidence. You always bring the evidence.

"She put peanuts in it," I said, in a voice like a rusty hinge. "On purpose. She said so. Seven witnesses."

The EMT looked at me. Then he looked at the house, where all seven of Danielle's friends were standing in the open front door like people watching a parade, and Danielle was in the middle of them in her apron, with her arms crossed.

"We're taking you to Baptist," he said. "Hang onto that container, Chief."

My daughter Celia met the ambulance at the emergency entrance. She's twenty-seven, a fifth-grade teacher, and she has her late mother's temper, which means she doesn't raise her voice. It just gets very flat.

"Dad. Dad, what happened?"

"Danielle," I said through the mask. "Peanuts. On purpose."

Celia's face didn't change at all. She just took my hand and walked next to the gurney all the way through the doors, and on the way, she took out her phone and started typing.

They gave me steroids, Benadryl and a second shot of epinephrine in a treatment bay behind a curtain, and a nurse named Pam put me on a monitor and told me I'd be there at least four hours, because sometimes the reaction comes back. I lay there breathing and listening to the monitor beep, and around nine-thirty a Pensacola police officer pulled the curtain back.

He was about thirty-five, with a crew cut and a notebook. His name tag said DWYER.

"Mr. Alcorn? Your daughter called us. She says you want to make a report."

"I do."

"Okay. Tell me what happened."

I told him. All of it, in a voice that sounded like I'd been gargling gravel. The dinner. The étouffée. The line about faking. The drawer. The truck.

He wrote it down. Then he looked at the Tupperware container, which Celia had set on the tray table next to my bed.

"That's the food?"

"That's the food."

"And you're saying she told you, out loud, that she put peanuts in it."

"In front of seven people. And she hid my EpiPen first."

He looked at me for a long time. I could see him doing the math. A man in his late fifties says his fiancée tried to poison him at a dinner party. Fiancée says the man's faking. Seven witnesses, all her friends.

"Sir," he said. "I'm going to be honest with you. This is going to come down to what those seven people say. And if they're her friends..."

"I want to file for assault with a deadly weapon," I said.

"In Florida we'd call it aggravated battery," Officer Dwyer said. "And for that to stick, I'd need..."

The curtain moved. My daughter pulled it back. Behind her stood a woman in her forties with long blond hair and a sundress, with her mascara run down to her chin, holding her phone in both hands like it was hot.

It was Shawna Ellery. The one who'd been filming.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm so sorry. I didn't know. I thought it was a joke. She said it was a joke. She said you'd been faking it for three years, and she just wanted to show everybody, and I thought..."

She held the phone out. Not to me. To the officer.

"It's all on here," she said. "Everything. She asked me to film it."

Officer Dwyer took the phone. He pressed play.

I heard it from the bed. I heard Danielle's voice, bright and loud, the way she sounds at open houses. I put peanuts in your dinner to prove you're faking your allergy. I heard my chair go over. I heard her say, Oh, here we go, the big show. I heard her friends laughing.

Then I heard the kitchen. The drawer. And my fiancée's voice, clear as a bell.

"I moved it. It's in my purse. Because you don't need it."

Officer Dwyer stopped the video. He looked at Shawna.

"Ma'am, where is Ms. Dupree right now?"

"She's here," Shawna said. "She's out in the waiting room. She drove over behind the ambulance. She's telling everybody he's overreacting."

"I want to be there," I said.

"Sir, you're on a monitor," the nurse said from behind Officer Dwyer. "You're not going anywhere."

"Ma'am, with respect, I've climbed a rope ladder up the side of a container ship in twelve-foot seas. I can sit in a wheelchair for five minutes."

Pam looked at Celia. Celia looked at Pam. Pam sighed, unhooked two of my leads, and went to get a wheelchair, muttering something about Navy men.

The ER waiting room at Baptist on a Saturday night is about forty plastic chairs, a TV playing the Weather Channel, and a vending machine that eats dollar bills. Danielle was standing in the middle of it in her KISS THE COOK apron, talking on her phone, loud. Three of her friends were sitting in a row behind her. And next to her, with his hand on the small of her back, was a tall, tanned man in a polo shirt and white tennis shorts. Gage Fowler. The tennis pro from the Bayou Country Club. He'd been at my dinner table two hours before, eating my étouffée.

His plate hadn't had any peanuts in it.

"...completely overreacting," Danielle was saying into the phone. "He does this. He does this every time he doesn't like something I cook. He grabs his throat and makes the noises. I've seen it a dozen times. I'm going to get him to admit it tonight if it kills me."

Celia pushed my wheelchair through the double doors. Danielle looked up and saw me, with the oxygen tube still under my nose, and she smiled. She actually smiled.

"There he is. Are you done, Phillip? Can we go home now?"

Officer Dwyer walked past my wheelchair, and a second officer, a woman, came in from the ambulance bay.

"Danielle Dupree?"

"Yes?"

"Ma'am, put the phone down, please. I need to ask you about your purse."

"My purse?" She laughed. "What about my purse?"

"May I see it?"

"No, you may not see my purse."

"Ma'am, I have a video of you stating that you removed Mr. Alcorn's EpiPen from his kitchen drawer and put it in your purse, before he had a reaction to food you've stated you contaminated on purpose. I'm asking you to show me the purse."

Danielle looked at Gage. Gage took his hand off her back.

"Danielle," he said quietly. "Don't say anything."

"I didn't do anything! He's faking! He's been faking for three years!"

The female officer took the purse off Danielle's shoulder, opened it, and took out a yellow EpiPen with my name printed on the pharmacy label. She held it up so Officer Dwyer could see it, and so could everybody in the forty plastic chairs.

The waiting room went dead quiet, except for the man on the Weather Channel talking about a tropical wave off Cuba.

"Danielle Dupree," Officer Dwyer said, "turn around and put your hands behind your back. You're under arrest for aggravated battery."

"You can't be serious."

"Turn around, ma'am."

She turned around. The handcuffs clicked. Her friends in the plastic chairs had their hands over their mouths. Somebody's kid with a bandaged finger was staring at her with his mouth open.

As they walked her past my wheelchair, she stopped and looked down at me.

"You did this," she said. "You did this to me over a little bit of peanut butter."

"You put it in my dinner, Danielle," I said. "And you put my pen in your purse."

They walked her out to the patrol car under the ambulance lights in her apron.

Gage Fowler didn't follow her. He stood in the middle of the waiting room with his hands in the pockets of his tennis shorts, looking at me. Then he walked over and bent down until his face was even with mine, and he smiled. Big white teeth. He's a good-looking man. He's made a good living out of it.

"You just made the biggest mistake of your life, Chief," he said, quietly, so Celia couldn't hear.

"I've made bigger," I said. "I asked her to marry me."

On Monday afternoon I sat in the office of my lawyer, Hollis Ware, on Palafox Street, with Detective Angela Crowell of the Pensacola Police Department sitting in the chair next to me. My voice was almost back. My throat still felt like I'd swallowed a sock.

Hollis is seventy, from Mobile, with a white mustache and suspenders, and he'd done my will after my wife passed in 2019. Detective Crowell was in her late forties, Black, with her hair pulled back tight and a steno pad on her knee.

"Ms. Dupree bonded out Sunday afternoon," the detective said. "Fifteen thousand dollars. The judge set a no-contact order. She can't come within five hundred feet of you or your house, and she can't contact you by any means."

"Who paid her bond?"

Detective Crowell looked at her pad. "A bondsman on Garden Street. The premium was paid by a Gage Fowler."

"The tennis pro," I said.

"The tennis pro." She looked at me. "Mr. Alcorn, I'm going to ask you some questions that are going to sound strange. Did Ms. Dupree know your allergy was real?"

"She's watched me read every food label in Publix for three years. She's seen the pen. She knows."

"Then why would she say you were faking?"

"She's been saying it for about two months. Since she started taking tennis lessons."

The detective wrote that down.

"Has anything changed in your finances recently? Your will, your insurance, your accounts?"

I started to say no. And then I stopped, because something came back to me, the way a sandbar shows up on the depth finder when you weren't expecting it.

"A month ago," I said slowly. "She asked me to change the beneficiary on my life insurance. I've got a policy through the pilots' association, seven hundred and fifty thousand. It's always gone to Celia. Danielle said since we were getting married in June, it ought to be split. She said it hurt her feelings that she wasn't on it. She cried about it."

"Did you change it?"

"I split it fifty-fifty. Celia and Danielle. I signed the form at the kitchen table. On a Sunday."

Hollis Ware put his pen down. Detective Crowell didn't put hers down. She wrote faster.

"Mr. Alcorn," Hollis said, in his slow Mobile voice, "I want you to call your insurance agent the second you walk out of this office and change that back. Today."

"Hollis, you don't think..."

"I don't think anything yet," Detective Crowell said. "I'm a detective. I write things down and I find out. But I'll tell you what I'm writing down. A woman tells a room full of people you're faking a deadly allergy. She hides your medicine. She feeds you the one thing that can kill you. And a month earlier she got herself on a three-quarter-million-dollar life insurance policy." She closed the pad. "I've seen a lot of stupid people do stupid things, Mr. Alcorn. I've also seen smart people do things that look stupid on purpose."

My phone buzzed on Hollis's desk. A number I didn't know.

I picked it up and put it on speaker.

"Chief," Gage Fowler's voice said. "We need to talk."

Detective Crowell held up one hand to me. Keep going.

"About what?" I said.

"About how this is going to go. Danielle's got a very good lawyer. And her lawyer's going to find out that you ate chicken satay at the club two Fridays ago. Peanut sauce. I served it to you myself. So either you've got the world's most selective allergy, or you set this whole thing up to get rid of a woman you didn't want to marry anymore." He let that sit. "Drop the charges, and I don't have to say that under oath."

"I've never eaten satay in my life, Gage."

"That's not how I remember it," he said, and hung up.

Detective Crowell looked at the phone for a long moment.

"Florida's a two-party state for recording, so we can't use that," she said. "But I heard it. And I wrote it down."

I called my insurance agent from the parking lot on Palafox Street, sitting in my truck with the engine running and the AC blasting, because Hollis had told me to and because my hands wanted something to do.

Her name is Joyce Whitley. She's handled the pilots' association policies for twenty years, and she knows my voice.

"Phillip! I heard about Saturday. Lord have mercy. Are you all right?"

"I'm all right, Joyce. I need to change my beneficiary back. All of it to Celia. Today."

"I can do that. I'll email you the form, you sign it on your phone, it's effective the minute I get it." I heard her typing. Then I heard her stop typing. "Phillip. Can I tell you something that's probably none of my business?"

"Go ahead."

"Your fiancée called this office. Two weeks ago. Wednesday, I think. She asked my assistant whether the change you made was 'in effect yet.' Those were her words. And then she asked whether the policy paid out the same amount for an accident as for anything else." Joyce paused. "My assistant thought it was strange. She wrote it down on a sticky note and stuck it on my monitor. It's still here."

I sat in my truck with the AC blowing on my face and I didn't say anything for a while.

"Joyce," I said. "Don't throw that sticky note away. A detective named Angela Crowell is going to call you."

"Oh, Phillip," she said. "Oh, honey."

That night, Shawna Ellery drove over to my house with her teenage son in the passenger seat for company and handed me a flash drive at the front door. She wouldn't come in.

"The whole video," she said. "The raw file, straight off my phone, so nobody can say I edited it. And screenshots of the group chat. Detective Crowell has copies. I wanted you to have them too."

"Danielle's girls. There's eight of us. It's called Gumbo Girls." She looked embarrassed. "We started it for a cooking club. It turned into, you know. Just talking."

The afternoon of the dinner, at a little after three, Danielle had written to the group: Tonight's the night, ladies. He's going to eat it and he's going to be FINE and then I'm finally going to hear him admit it. Shawna you're on camera.

Somebody had written back: Are you SURE he's faking tho? What if he's not?

Destin. Just the two of us.

She'd told her girlfriends before she told me.

On Thursday afternoon, Danielle's older sister Lynette came to pick up her things, with a Pensacola police officer standing in my driveway, because that's how it works when there's a no-contact order. The officer stands there with his thumbs in his belt and makes sure nobody throws a lamp.

Lynette was fifty-five, a hairdresser in Milton, with the same sharp chin as her sister. She'd brought two of her grown sons and a U-Haul trailer. She walked through my house with a clipboard, pointing. The sons carried. I stood in the kitchen with my coffee and stayed out of the way.

When they got to the kitchen, she stopped by the stove and looked at the drawer. The empty one. I hadn't put anything back in it yet.

"That's the drawer," she said.

"That's the drawer."

The next morning Shawna Ellery called me crying. She'd worked the front desk at the Bayou Country Club for nine years. That morning the general manager had called her into his office and let her go. Then I got in my truck and drove to the Bayou Country Club and walked into the Nineteenth Hole bar in the middle of a Saturday afternoon, and sat down at the end of the bar in front of the man who'd been pouring my drinks for eleven years.

Sunday night I had a ship.

A harbor pilot's schedule goes up on a whiteboard at the pilot station on the Port of Pensacola docks, and anybody who's ever ridden along with me knows how to read it. Danielle had ridden along with me twice. She thought it was romantic. ALCORN, 0200, M/V GULF STAR, INBOUND, BERTH 3.

I parked my truck at the station a little after one in the morning. There was fog coming in off the Gulf, the thick kind that turns the dock lights into fuzzy yellow balls. Our pilot boat was tied up at the end of the floating dock, a forty-foot orange launch with PILOT painted on the side, and the boat operator, a kid named Marco, wasn't there yet.

I walked out on the dock with my bag over my shoulder.

He was standing by the pilot boat, in a dark hoodie with the hood up, leaning on a piling. When he heard my boots on the planks, he pushed off the piling and turned around.

"Chief," Gage Fowler said.

"You're not supposed to be out here, Gage. This is a secure dock."

"Gate was open." He walked toward me. He had his hands in the front pocket of his hoodie. "I came to give you one more chance. Drop the charges. Tell them you made a mistake. Tell them you were confused. You're a fifty-nine-year-old man with a heart condition, nobody's going to blame you."

"I don't have a heart condition."

"After tonight you might."

He took his right hand out of the hoodie pocket. He was holding a tennis racket handle, just the handle, the grip end of a racket he'd sawed off, about a foot and a half of graphite with the tape still on it.

"Gage," I said. "There's a camera on that light pole behind you. It's been on that pole since 2016. It covers the whole dock."

He laughed. "In this fog?"

He swung. Overhand, like a serve. He's a strong man and he's fast, and if it had hit me on the head, I wouldn't be telling you this story.

But I've been walking on wet docks in the dark since I was nineteen years old, and he'd been walking on them for about two minutes. I stepped back and to the side, the way you step off a ladder onto a moving deck, and the racket handle whistled past my ear and hit the piling with a crack, and Gage's foot came down on a plank slick with fog and diesel.

He went over the edge of the dock and into Pensacola Bay.

It's about twelve feet deep at the end of that dock in October, and the water is about seventy-five degrees, and it's black as ink at one in the morning. He came up sputtering and grabbing at the side of the pilot boat, which was slick as glass, and he couldn't get a grip on it.

"I can't..." he gasped. "I can't swim good. Help me!"

The tennis pro at the Bayou Country Club couldn't swim good. That's the kind of thing you find out about a man in the middle of the night.

I took the orange life ring off its hook on the piling. It's been on that hook for as long as I've been a pilot. I threw it so it landed right in front of him, the way the Navy taught me, and I hauled on the line hand over hand until I had him at the floating section of the dock. Then I reached down and grabbed him by the hood of his sweatshirt and dragged him up onto the planks like a hundred-and-ninety-pound redfish.

He lay there on his back, coughing up bay water.

"Why'd you pull me out?" he said, when he could talk.

"Because I'm not you," I said, and I took out my phone and called 911.

Marco the boat operator came jogging down the dock right about then with his coffee, saw a soaking-wet man in a hoodie lying on the planks and a sawed-off racket handle floating by the pilot boat, and said, "Chief, I'm gone for ten minutes."

The Port of Pensacola police got there first. Then Detective Crowell, at two-fifteen in the morning, in a Pensacola PD windbreaker, with her hair down for once. She stood on the dock and watched the camera footage from the light pole on the port officer's tablet. The fog made it grainy. It didn't make it unclear.

"He swung first," she said.

"He did."

"And then you pulled him out of the bay."

"Yes, ma'am."

She looked at Gage, sitting in the back of a port police car wrapped in a gray blanket, dripping.

"Mr. Alcorn, I'm going to tell you something," she said. "I got a statement this afternoon from a bartender named Rudy Pena. I got the club's member records showing your allergy flag. I got a call log from Mr. Fowler's phone showing eleven calls to Ms. Dupree the night of your dinner, before and after. And now I've got this." She nodded at the tablet. "I'm going to the state attorney first thing in the morning."

"What about my ship?" I said. "Gulf Star's sitting out at the sea buoy waiting on me."

She looked at me like I'd lost my mind.

"Go," she said. "Go take your ship in, Chief."

So I did. I climbed the rope ladder up the side of a six-hundred-foot freighter at three in the morning in the fog, and I brought her through the channel and around Fort Pickens and into Berth 3 without touching a single sandbar, and when I climbed back down at dawn, my hands didn't shake once.

The Bayou Country Club Member-Guest Tournament Awards Dinner is the biggest night of the year at the club. It's always the second Saturday in October. They put white tablecloths in the main dining room, open the doors onto the terrace over the bayou, and the tennis pro hands out the trophies, because the tennis pro is always the best-looking man in the room, and the general manager knows it sells memberships.

Celia came with me. She wore her mother's pearls.

"Are you sure about this, Dad?"

"Detective Crowell asked me to be here," I said. "She said I'd want to see it."

There were about two hundred people in the dining room. The general manager, a man named Pat Gaffney, in a navy blazer with the club crest on it, was going from table to table shaking hands. Rudy was behind the terrace bar. He lifted his chin at me when I came in.

Gage Fowler was up at the microphone by the trophy table, in a white dinner jacket, with a bandage over his left eye where he'd hit the edge of the dock going in. He was making jokes about it.

"Little boating accident," he said into the mic, grinning. "You should see the other guy."

People laughed. He spotted me at a table near the back. The grin didn't move, but his eyes did.

Then, from the terrace doors, I saw Danielle.

She was in a red dress, holding a glass of white wine, with her real estate smile on. She was standing by the bar with two of the women who'd been at my dinner table. She didn't see me at first.

When she did, she stood very still.

"Dad," Celia said quietly. "She's not supposed to be within five hundred feet of you."

"I know."

"Is she crazy?"

"She thinks Gage has it handled," I said. "She thinks he's the respected member of the community."

At eight-thirty, Gage picked up the big silver Member-Guest trophy and lifted it over his head, and right then Detective Angela Crowell walked in through the main dining room doors with two uniformed officers, one of them Officer Dwyer.

She walked straight up the aisle between the tables to the microphone.

"Gage Fowler?"

Gage lowered the trophy. "Detective. We're kind of in the middle of something."

"So am I, sir." She took a folded paper out of her jacket. "I have a warrant for your arrest. Perjury, for the sworn statement you gave Ms. Dupree's attorney about serving Mr. Alcorn chicken satay. Tampering with a witness, for your conversation with Ms. Shawna Ellery. And aggravated assault, for the incident on the pilot dock at the Port of Pensacola last Sunday morning."

Two hundred people stopped chewing.

Pat Gaffney, the general manager, came halfway up the aisle. "Detective, there must be some mistake. Gage is one of our..."

"Mr. Gaffney," Detective Crowell said, without turning around. "Your own bartender gave me your own member records. Mr. Alcorn's account has a peanut allergy flag on it from your own chef. Nobody in your kitchen has ever served him peanuts. Your tennis pro lied under oath. You also fired the witness who filmed the poisoning, the same day your tennis pro was standing outside your office. You might want to call your club's lawyer tonight."

Gaffney sat down at the nearest table, in somebody else's chair.

Gage set the silver trophy down on the table very carefully. Then he turned and pointed across the dining room, at the terrace bar. At the woman in the red dress.

"She did it!" he said. "She put the peanuts in his plate, not me! I never touched his food! I was sitting at the other end of the table! Ask anybody!"

Every head in the dining room turned to the terrace.

Danielle's wineglass was halfway to her mouth.

"Gage," she said. "You told me he was faking. You told me you saw him eat satay. You told me you served it to him yourself."

"I never said that."

"You said it in my car! You said if I proved it, everybody at the club would finally see what a liar he was!"

"Babe," he said. Out loud. Into a live microphone. "Shut up."

Rudy Pena, behind the terrace bar, set down a glass.

"That's what he called her on the phone," he said, loud enough for half the room. "In the pro shop. 'If he croaks, it's three-quarters of a million, babe.'"

Danielle turned and looked at Rudy. Then at Gage. And I watched her finally understand what her tennis lessons had been about.

Officer Dwyer took Gage by the arm and turned him around at the trophy table and cuffed him in his white dinner jacket. Then the second officer walked out onto the terrace toward Danielle.

"Ms. Dupree, you're subject to a no-contact order with Mr. Alcorn. He's sitting about sixty feet from you. You're under arrest for violating a protective order. Put the glass down."

She put the glass down. Then she pulled at her left hand, and she got the ring off, the two-carat one I'd bought at a jeweler on Palafox Street for eleven thousand dollars, and she threw it across the terrace at my table.

It bounced off the tablecloth and landed in Celia's water glass.

My daughter fished it out with two fingers and set it on her napkin.

Danielle was crying now, real tears, the mascara going. They walked her past our table on the way out, and she stopped, and the officer let her.

"Phillip," she said. "I didn't know. I swear to God, I didn't know. He told me you were faking. I just wanted everybody to see it. I just wanted to be right."

I picked the wet ring up off Celia's napkin and held it out to the officer.

"Put that in her property bag," I said. "She'll need something to sell for a lawyer. Her boyfriend's got his own problems now."
