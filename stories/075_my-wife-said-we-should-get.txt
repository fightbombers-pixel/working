"We should get divorced while you still have your dignity."

My wife said it at Sunday brunch at the Dockside, at a table for eight, with her six closest friends holding their mimosas and listening to every word. Corinne said it the way she'd ask somebody to pass the butter.

I looked down at the check that had just landed in front of me. Two hundred and twelve dollars. Twenty-seven years of marriage, and she'd picked the morning I was paying for everybody's crab cakes.

So I took off my wedding ring, set it on top of the check, and slid the whole thing across the tablecloth to her.

"Then brunch is on you," I said.

Somebody laughed. Somebody else said, "Oh my God." Corinne just stared at that ring sitting on that little black folder like it was a bug that had crawled out of the bread basket.

Seventy-two hours later, her own lawyer was calling my cell phone four times in one morning, and the last time he called, he forgot to say hello.

What she didn't know, sitting there in her white linen blazer, was that one of the six women at that table had her phone in her lap, and the screen was open to a group chat called the Anchor Girls.

My name is Darius Woodard. I'm 55 years old, and I own Woodard Marine, a diesel repair yard on the Cape Fear River in Wilmington, North Carolina. I've been rebuilding boat engines since I was nineteen. The first thing my old boss taught me was this: never trust the gauge on the dash, because a gauge can lie. Trust the logbook. Somebody wrote that down when it happened. Hold on to that.

Corinne picked up the ring with two fingers and set it next to her plate.

"Darius, don't make this ugly," she said. "I'm trying to be kind."

"You're doing great," I said.

"I mean it. You'll get a fair deal. You won't have to go through some big humiliating thing in court."

"Like this?"

That was when a white Ram pickup pulled into the gravel lot outside the window, and a man got out. Tall, tan, maybe fifty, a blue fleece vest and the kind of sunglasses that cost more than they should. He didn't come in. He just leaned against the tailgate and waited.

Corinne stood up and slung her purse over her shoulder.

"Who's that?" I said.

"Somebody who appreciates me."

"Does somebody have a name?"

"Preston," she said. "Not that it's any of your business anymore."

She walked out. She didn't pay the check. Through the window I watched her climb into that pickup, and I watched the man named Preston look past her, at me, through the glass, and smile like he knew something I didn't.

I'd never seen him before in my life.

The table went quiet. Marla Pruett put her mimosa down. Ginny Haskell pretended to look for something in her purse. Tonya Reese, who'd been Corinne's maid of honor, said, "Darius, honey, I had no idea," which was a lie, because her face said she'd had every idea for months.

Only one of them didn't say anything at all. Gloria Abney, the quiet one at the end, who ran the front office at a dental practice off Oleander Drive. She put a twenty-dollar bill on the table, pushed back her chair, and walked out without looking at me.

I paid the two hundred and twelve dollars. I left the waitress forty on top, because none of it was her fault.

I was sitting in my truck in the lot with the engine off when my phone buzzed.

It was a text from a number I didn't have saved.

"It's Gloria. Don't call Corinne. Don't text her. Check your joint savings account right now, and then meet me at your yard at three. Come alone."

I opened the bank app. The joint savings was still there. Eighty-four thousand, three hundred dollars. Every dollar of it was there.

Then I read Gloria's text again, and I understood that the money being there right now wasn't the point. The point was "right now."

At five minutes to three, Gloria's little gray Corolla pulled through the gate of Woodard Marine.

The yard on a Sunday is quiet. No compressors, no grinders. Just the river slapping the bulkhead and the halyards pinging against the masts over at the marina next door. My foreman, Abel Cortez, was there anyway, because Abel is always there. He was sitting on an upside-down bucket by the travel lift, eating sunflower seeds and spitting the shells into a coffee can.

"Who's that?" he said.

"One of Corinne's friends."

"Uh-oh."

"Maybe not uh-oh."

"Boss, a woman from your wife's brunch club drives to your boatyard on a Sunday, it's uh-oh. I've been married twice. Trust me."

Gloria got out of the car holding her phone against her chest like it might go off.

"Can we talk inside?" she said.

I took her into the office, which is a double-wide trailer with a window unit, a coffee maker older than my son, and a wall calendar from a propeller company in Fort Lauderdale. She didn't sit down.

"I'm going to show you something," she said. "And then I'm going to need you to understand why I'm doing it, because tomorrow those five women are going to call me a snake."

"Okay."

"Corinne made a group chat in January. It's called the Anchor Girls. It's the six of us. Mostly it's recipes and who's getting Botox. But since March, it's been her."

She turned the phone around.

I don't know what I expected. I'd half expected pictures of the two of them, which would have been bad enough. What I got was worse, because it was organized.

Corinne, March 14: "Met someone on the sunset charter in Charleston. Preston. Yacht broker. Girls, I'm alive again."

Corinne, April 2: "Darius doesn't notice anything that doesn't have a fuel injector."

Corinne, May 20: "Okay, here's the plan. Don't judge."

Gloria scrolled slowly so I could read it.

"Step one. I tell him at brunch in front of you guys so he can't make a scene. Darius hates scenes. Step two. Monday morning I'm at the bank at nine when they open and I move the joint savings. 84K. It's half mine anyway, the rest is what I'm owed for 27 years of laundry. Step three. My lawyer says if I tell the court I'm scared of him, he'll be out of the house in a week. He's never touched me, but he's big and he's quiet and judges don't like quiet. Step four. Preston has a buyer in Jacksonville for the Grace. Cash. We split it. Darius can keep his greasy little yard."

There were little emojis after it. A sailboat. A money bag. A champagne bottle.

Under it, Tonya had written, "Girl. Iconic."

Marla had written, "Get it, Cor."

Ginny had written a crying-laughing face.

Gloria hadn't written anything.

I read it twice. Then I set the phone down on the desk very carefully, the way you set down a part you're afraid to drop.

"The Grace," I said.

"The boat. The one in her profile picture."

The Carolina Grace is a thirty-eight-foot sportfisher I bought as a wreck in 2009 for eleven thousand dollars and spent four years rebuilding. Twin Cummins diesels. I did every hose clamp myself. She sat in slip twelve at the marina next door, and Corinne used her for photos and for her girlfriends' birthday cruises, and I used her for the one week a year I took off.

"She thinks she can sell my boat?" I said.

"She thinks it's half hers. Darius, she posted a picture on that boat yesterday. With him. On the back deck. The caption was 'Living my best life.'" Gloria swallowed. "He's been out on it with her. When you were at that engine show in Raleigh in April. She had the keys."

Abel had come to the doorway and was leaning on the frame. He'd stopped chewing.

"Preston," I said. "How did she meet a yacht broker on a sunset charter in Charleston?"

"Her sister's birthday trip," Gloria said. "He was working the boat. He was charming. He asked a lot of questions about what her husband did." She looked at me. "He asked a lot of questions about the Grace."

"Why are you showing me this?"

She was quiet a second.

"Because six years ago my husband did this exact thing to me," she said. "Emptied the savings on a Monday morning and told the judge I threw a coffee mug at him. I didn't. Took me two years and thirty thousand dollars to climb out. And on Saturday night I sat in that chat and watched five women I've known since our kids were in Pop Warner send her champagne emojis about it." Her voice cracked a little and she pushed through it. "I'm not doing that again. Not even from the other side."

"Will you send me those screenshots?"

"I already took them. I'll send them now. And I'll sign whatever your lawyer needs, saying they're real and I'm in that chat."

"She'll know it was you."

"She'll figure it out by Wednesday," Gloria said. "Tonya can't keep a secret and neither can I, apparently."

My phone started buzzing on the desk as the screenshots came through. Twenty-three of them.

Abel let out a long low whistle from the doorway.

"Boss," he said, "I take it back. Not uh-oh. Whatever's bigger than uh-oh."

"Abel," I said, "go next door and look in slip twelve."

He went. I sat there scrolling through my wife's plan with my thumb, and the oddest thing happened. I wasn't shaking. I felt like I did the first time I opened up a seized engine that a customer had sworn up and down was "running fine last week." Once you see the scoring on the cylinder walls, you're not mad anymore. You just know what you're dealing with.

The phone rang. Abel.

"She's still here," he said. "The Grace is in her slip. But boss? Somebody topped off the water and there's a cooler on the back deck that ain't yours. And the fuel log in the dock office says she's on the list for the fuel dock tomorrow at noon. Full tanks."

"Under whose name?"

He paused.

"Gale," he said. "P. Gale."

At six o'clock that Sunday evening I was sitting in the kitchen of Paulette Kincaid, attorney at law, eating a slice of her pound cake and trying not to get crumbs on my wife's plan.

Paulette is sixty, short, gray, and has been one of my customers for fifteen years. She owns a 26-foot Grady-White that she runs too hard and brings to me every October like a kid bringing a report card home. She also has handled more divorces in New Hanover County than anybody alive.

She read all twenty-three screenshots with her reading glasses down on the end of her nose. She didn't say a word until she was done.

"Well," she said. "Your wife writes a very clear memo."

"Can she do it? Take the eighty-four thousand?"

"Is it a joint account?"

"Both our names."

"Then the bank will hand it to her with a smile at nine o'clock tomorrow morning," Paulette said, "unless somebody walks a piece of paper in front of a judge before that."

"What piece of paper?"

"In North Carolina you can't get divorced until you've been separated a year. That's the law. But you can file a lawsuit the day you separate for what we call equitable distribution, which is just a fancy name for dividing up the stuff fairly. And with that, I can ask the judge for an order freezing the marital accounts so nobody empties them. When I can show the judge she's planning to do it, in her own words, on a specific day, at a specific time…" She tapped the phone. "Judges love a specific time."

"So we file first."

"We file first. Eight-thirty tomorrow morning, when the clerk's office opens and Judge Vann starts signing things. And the boat. Whose name is the boat in?"

"The yard's," I said. "Woodard Marine LLC. I set up the company in 1994. Corinne and I got married in 1998. The yard bought the Grace in 2009 as a project boat. It's on the yard's books, insurance, everything."

Paulette took her glasses off.

"Does she know that?"

"She's never asked. She thinks it's our boat because she's in all the pictures."

"Darius," Paulette said, "a boat titled to a company you owned before the marriage is a whole different animal. I'm not going to promise you a court will call it all yours, because the yard's money and the marriage get tangled. But I can promise you this. She cannot sign it over to a man in Florida. She's not on the title. She's not an officer of the company. If that man sells it to anybody, he's selling something that isn't his to sell."

"He's got it on the fuel list for noon tomorrow."

"Then you have until noon tomorrow," she said, "to make sure it doesn't leave that dock." She stood up and got a legal pad. "And you're not going to touch him, or her. You're going to call the Sheriff's Office if that boat moves. Understand?"

"Understood."

She started writing, and then she stopped and looked over the pad at me.

"One more thing. That bit about telling the court she's scared of you." Paulette's mouth went flat. "That's the part that would've cost you the house, son. You'd have been out of there with a duffel bag before you knew what hit you. Now we have it in writing that she planned to say it, and that it isn't true. Her lawyer is going to want to see that."

"Should I send it to him?"

"Not yet," Paulette said. "First we let her walk into the bank."

A little before nine on Monday morning, Judge Harold Vann signed the order in his chambers with a ballpoint pen that skipped twice. Paulette walked it down to the clerk, got it stamped, and faxed it to the bank's legal department from the clerk's own machine, because she trusts fax machines more than email and she's usually right.

I was already parked across the street from the Wrightsville Avenue branch of Coastal Federal, with a cup of coffee going cold in the holder.

At two minutes after nine, Corinne's silver Acura pulled into the lot.

She went in alone. Preston's white Ram was not with her. I sat there and counted. One minute. Two. Through the front glass I could see her at the teller line in her sunglasses, sliding something across the counter. I could see the teller, a young guy with a buzz cut, look at his screen. Look at it again. Pick up the phone.

I got out of the truck and walked across the street.

When I came through the door, the branch manager was already standing next to the teller. She was a woman named Ellen Dupree whose husband's bass boat I'd repowered two summers ago, and she gave me a look that said she had read the fax and would very much like everybody to keep their voices down.

Corinne turned around and saw me.

"What did you do?" she said.

"Good morning to you too."

"Darius, what did you do? They're saying the account is frozen. It's my account."

"It's our account," I said. "That's sort of the problem."

"Mrs. Woodard," Ellen said gently, "there's a court order on all the joint accounts as of this morning. I can't release any funds. I'm so sorry. You'd need to talk to your attorney."

"A court order? For what? I haven't even filed anything."

"I did," I said.

She took off her sunglasses. Her eyes were red around the edges, but not from crying. From not sleeping.

"You filed for divorce. Over the weekend."

"North Carolina makes you wait a year for the divorce. I filed for the rest. The house, the money, the stuff. And an order so nobody touches the money until a judge says who gets what."

"Nobody was going to touch the money."

"You're standing at the teller window at nine-oh-two on Monday morning, Corinne."

"I was going to move my half. I'm allowed to move my half."

"Eighty-four thousand three hundred is your half?"

She opened her mouth and closed it. She hadn't said the number. I had.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

"Okay."

"I came to get some cash for the week. That's all. Because I don't know what you're going to do. You're the one who stood up at brunch and threw your ring at me."

"I set it down."

"In front of everybody!"

"You told me to get divorced while I still had my dignity," I said. "In front of everybody. I was just keeping some."

The teller with the buzz cut was looking at his keyboard so hard he might have been trying to set it on fire. An older man at the next window had stopped filling out his deposit slip.

Corinne leaned in close, and her voice went low and sweet, which is the voice she uses on contractors when the price is too high.

"Darius. Whatever you think you know, you don't. You've been in that yard for twenty-seven years. You don't know anything about me anymore. You don't know what I need."

"I know you need eighty-four thousand dollars by nine o'clock on a Monday," I said. "And I know somebody's got my boat on the fuel list at noon."

That one landed. I watched it land. For just a second she looked past me, out the front window, at the street, the way you look for the car that's supposed to pick you up.

Then she put her sunglasses back on.

"You'll hear from my lawyer," she said.

"I'm counting on it."

She walked out. The Acura left the lot faster than a person who's not in a hurry. And when I got back to my truck there were two texts waiting.

The first one was from Gloria. "She just posted in the chat. 'He froze the accounts. Who told him???' Five question marks from Tonya. Nobody's said my name yet."

The second one was from Abel.

"Boss. Your slip. The Grace is gone."

I drove the four miles back to the river doing the speed limit, because getting pulled over was not going to help anybody.

The Carolina Grace was not in slip twelve. She was over at the fuel dock, a long concrete pier where the marina sells diesel, tied up stern-in with the pump nozzle already in her port tank. It was twenty after eleven. Preston had moved her up from noon.

Preston Gale was standing on the back deck in the same fleece vest, holding a clipboard, talking to a heavyset kid in a marina polo who was running the pump. Corinne's Acura was parked in the lot by the bait shop. I couldn't see her.

Abel was waiting for me at the head of the pier with his arms folded.

"I asked the kid to stop pumping," he said. "Kid says the man's got a signed paper and a credit card and the boat's leaving. Kid's nineteen. Kid doesn't want trouble."

"I don't want trouble either."

"Boss, you got a face on you like trouble."

"I'm calling the Sheriff's Office," I said, and I did, right there, standing on the pier. Then I walked down the dock with the yard's title in a manila folder under my arm. I'd pulled it out of the office safe on the way. I'd also brought the boat's maintenance logbook, because that's the kind of man I am.

Preston saw me coming. He set his clipboard down on the gunwale.

"You must be Darius," he said. He had a voice like a radio ad for a car dealership. "Corinne said you might come down. Look, man, I get it. Nobody likes this. But she's entitled to her share, and this is the cleanest way."

"Shut off the pump," I said to the kid.

The kid looked at Preston.

"Keep pumping," Preston said. "I paid for the fuel."

"With whose card?" I said.

That stopped him for half a second. Then the smile came back.

"I've got a signed bill of sale from the owner," he said. "Buyer's waiting in Jacksonville. He's already put down a deposit. You want to fight about it, get a lawyer."

"I've got one. Who signed your bill of sale?"

"Your wife."

"My wife doesn't own this boat."

"Come on. Twenty-seven years. It's marital property, buddy."

"It's Woodard Marine's property," I said, and held up the folder. "Bought by the company in 2009. Title's in the company's name. The company's mine, since 1994, four years before I married her. She's not on the title and she's not an officer. You can't buy a boat from somebody who doesn't own it, and you sure can't sell one."

He looked at the folder and didn't take it. He looked at the dock behind me, at the bait shop, at the parking lot. His eyes moved the way Corinne's had at the bank.

"Unhook the line," he said to the kid.

The kid didn't move.

So Preston did it himself. He stepped onto the dock, reached for the stern line on the cleat, and I stepped in front of the cleat.

"Don't," I said.

He was two inches taller than me and maybe twenty pounds lighter, and I could see him decide that was enough. He put a hand flat on my chest and shoved.

I went back one step. I didn't fall. I've been standing on wet docks for thirty-six years.

"Last time," I said. "Leave the line alone."

He swung. It was a looping right hand, the kind a man throws when he's seen fights on television, and I saw it coming the whole way. I took it on my shoulder, caught his wrist on the way through, and turned him the way you turn a stuck prop shaft, slow and hard and all the way around, until he was face down on the concrete with his arm up behind his back and his cheek next to a puddle of somebody's spilled bait.

"Ow, ow, okay, man, okay!"

"I didn't hit you," I said. "I want everybody on this dock to notice I didn't hit you."

"I noticed," Abel said. He was filming on his phone. So was the kid. So, I found out later, was Corinne.

She'd come out of the dock office. I hadn't seen her. She was standing about twenty feet away with her phone held up in both hands.

"Get off him!" she said. "Darius, get off him, you're hurting him! I'm recording this! I'm recording this!"

"Good," I said. "Keep recording. Get the part where he swung first."

"You're an animal!"

"Corinne, he hit me."

"He was defending himself!"

"From a boat cleat?"

A New Hanover County Sheriff's cruiser came down the gravel road and parked behind the bait shop, and a deputy named Ray Fulcher walked down the pier with his thumbs in his belt like a man who'd seen every kind of thing a marina can produce on a Monday.

"Let him up, sir," he said.

I let Preston up. He stood there rubbing his wrist and telling the deputy I'd attacked him, and the deputy let him finish, and then he asked to see the kid's video, and the kid showed him, and then Abel's, and Abel showed him, and the deputy watched the part where Preston shoved me and then swung, and he watched it twice.

"Well," Deputy Fulcher said. "That's pretty clear."

"He grabbed me!" Preston said.

"After you swung on him. Who owns the boat?"

Preston pointed at Corinne. I held out the folder. The deputy took the folder.

He read the title for a long time. Then he read the bill of sale Preston handed him, a printed form from a boat brokerage in Jacksonville with Corinne's signature at the bottom and a line that said "Seller: owner of record."

"Ma'am," Deputy Fulcher said to Corinne. "Are you Woodard Marine LLC?"

"I'm his wife."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's half mine."

"Ma'am, I'm not a judge. But I can read a title, and your name isn't on this one. That boat's not leaving this dock today." He turned to Preston. "Sir, how much did your buyer put down?"

Preston didn't answer.

"Forty thousand," the kid from the marina said. Everybody looked at him. He went red. "He was on the phone about it at the pump. Loud."

"Forty thousand dollars," the deputy said, "on a boat the seller doesn't own." He wrote something in his notebook. "Mr. Woodard, do you want to press charges on the assault?"

I thought about it. I looked at Preston, rubbing his wrist, and at Corinne with her phone still up, still recording, though there was nothing to record now but a deputy writing in a notebook.

"Not today," I said. "I want my boat back in my slip."

"Then that's what we'll do."

Abel and I walked the Grace back to slip twelve. The deputy stood on the dock and watched us do it. When I came back up the pier, Corinne was standing by her car with Preston, and Preston was on his phone, saying, "No, no, it's handled, it's just a delay, I'll call you back in an hour," in that radio-ad voice, which wasn't working as well as it had at breakfast.

Corinne looked at me across the gravel lot.

"You just cost me forty thousand dollars," she said.

"I didn't sell my boat to anybody," I said. "You'd have to ask him whose forty thousand it is."

That night I sat at the kitchen table in a house that was suddenly very quiet, and I opened my laptop.

Paulette had sent me the name and email of Corinne's lawyer. It had come on a letter he'd emailed her at four that afternoon. Royce Delano, Delano and Price, Front Street. The letter said that his client "fears for her safety in the marital home" and requested that I "vacate the residence within seventy-two hours to avoid the need for emergency relief."

I read it twice. Then I read step three of Corinne's plan again, in her own words, with a little sailboat at the end.

I called Paulette.

"Now?" I said.

"Now," she said. "Professional and short. You don't argue. You don't call her names. You attach it and you say it's a courtesy."

So I wrote it the way I write an estimate.

"Mr. Delano. Attached please find twenty-three screenshots from a group text your client created in January and has posted in regularly since. A member of the group will sign a sworn statement that they are authentic. I'm sending them as a courtesy, so you know what you're working with before you file anything about fear. My attorney is Paulette Kincaid. Darius Woodard."

I hit send a little before ten that night.

Then I got up and checked the lock on the back door, which I'd never done once in twenty-seven years, and I went to bed in the guest room, because I didn't want to sleep where she'd slept.

I didn't hear back that night.

I heard from Corinne instead. Tuesday morning, a little after seven, she pulled into the gravel lot of Woodard Marine, and she wasn't alone. Her sister Delia was with her. And Delia was carrying a cardboard box with my father's brass compass sticking out of the top.

That compass had been screwed to the bulkhead in the Grace's cabin for eleven years. My father carried it on a shrimp boat out of Southport for thirty years before that. Nobody takes it off that wall but me.

I walked out of the trailer before they got to the door.

"Where did you get that?"

Delia set the box down on the hood of Corinne's Acura like it was hot. She's Corinne's younger sister, forty-eight, a school nurse in Leland, and the only person in that family who ever asked me how my day was.

"It was in Preston's truck," Delia said. "In the back seat. Your rods are in there too, and the radio, and that little brass clock. I saw it when he dropped Cor off at my house last night. I made him give it to me."

"He took it off my boat."

"He said he was getting her ready for the buyer," Delia said. "Darius, he had a screwdriver in his pocket. Like it was normal."

Corinne got out of the car. She'd done her makeup, the full war paint, and she had a folder of her own.

"I'm returning your things," she said. "Because I'm not a thief, no matter what you're telling people."

"I haven't told anybody anything."

"Then who told the bank? Who told you about the fuel dock? Who sent my lawyer a pile of my private messages at ten o'clock at night?"

"I sent your lawyer those."

"Where did you get them?"

"From the logbook," I said.

"What does that even mean?"

"It means you wrote it all down, Corinne."

Abel had come out of the shop with a rag in his hands and was pretending to wipe down a fuel filter that was already clean. Two of my mechanics had stopped working on the outboard on the stands. Delia had backed up to the fence.

Corinne opened her folder and took out a sheet of paper.

"You want to talk about writing things down? This is what Royce is filing today if you don't move out. 'Respondent is a large man with a history of intimidating silence.' That's you. Everybody at that brunch saw you throw that ring. Everybody at the marina saw you put a man on the ground."

"A man who swung at me."

"A man who was trying to help me get what I'm owed!"

"By selling my boat to a stranger in Florida."

"Our boat!"

"You keep saying that. The title doesn't."

She threw the paper at me. It didn't go anywhere. Paper never does. It sort of floated down and landed on the gravel between us, face up.

"Twenty-seven years," she said. "Twenty-seven years I sat in that house smelling diesel on your clothes. I raised Jordan basically by myself while you were out here till nine at night rebuilding some dentist's toy. You never took me anywhere that didn't have a fuel dock. You never once asked me what I wanted."

"What did you want?"

"Not this!"

"Then why'd you plan it?"

"I didn't plan anything! I fell in love! That's not a crime!"

"No," I said. "Telling a judge you're scared of me when you're not is a crime. You wrote that one down too."

She went quiet. For a second her chin trembled, and I thought maybe I'd see the woman I married, the one who used to sit on an overturned bucket in this yard in 1998 and hand me wrenches and laugh when I swore at a stuck bolt.

Then it was gone.

"You're going to look like a monster when this is over," she said. "I'm going to make sure everybody in this town knows what you are."

"Corinne," I said, "you told me I should get divorced while I still had my dignity. You were right. I'm keeping it. You might want to think about yours."

She got in the Acura. She left Delia standing by the fence with her arms wrapped around herself. The car spun a little gravel going out the gate.

Delia didn't move for a minute.

"I told her," she said finally. "In Charleston. On the charter. I told her he asked too many questions. About you. About the yard. About what the boat was worth." She looked at me. "She said I was jealous."

"Thank you for the compass."

"There's something else," Delia said. "When he dropped her at my house last night, he made a phone call in the driveway. I heard him. He said, 'She can't sell it, the title's in the company. I need the forty back before Tanner finds out.'"

"Who's Tanner?"

"The buyer, I think," she said. "The man in Jacksonville. Preston sounded scared."

I picked her sister's paper up off the gravel and folded it in half.

"Delia," I said, "would you tell that to a lawyer?"

She thought about it for a long time. Long enough that Abel quit wiping the filter.

"She's my sister," Delia said. "But I'm not going to lie for her. I'll tell it if somebody asks me."

Wednesday morning, at five minutes after eleven, my phone rang.

I was under the stern of a 40-foot Hatteras with a drop light, pulling a raw water pump, and I let it ring. The screen said "Delano & Price." It rang again two minutes later. Then a third time. By the fourth call I had the pump in a bucket and grease up to my elbows, and Abel stuck his head around the hull.

"Boss," he said. "That lawyer's called the shop line twice too. Your secretary says he sounds like a guy whose bilge pump just quit."

I wiped my hands on a rag and answered.

"Mr. Woodard." Royce Delano had a smooth voice that wasn't smooth this morning. He didn't say hello. "I need you to understand that I didn't have those messages when I sent that letter on Monday."

"I believe you."

"I've spent the last thirty-six hours reading them. I've spent the last twelve on the phone with my client. Mr. Woodard, I can't file what she wants me to file."

"Okay."

"I can't put her on a witness stand and let her say she's afraid of you when I've got her own words saying she isn't and she planned to say it anyway. I'd lose my license. I'd deserve to." He breathed out. "And now I've got an attorney in Jacksonville telling me his client, a Mr. Lamont Tanner, wired forty thousand dollars to a Preston Gale for a boat my client signed a bill of sale on. He's filed a police report down there for obtaining property by false pretenses. He's naming both of them."

"Both of them?"

"My client's signature is on that bill of sale," Royce said. "'Owner of record.' She signed it. She swears she didn't know about the title. I believe she believed it was half hers. But it doesn't matter what I believe."

I sat down on an upside-down bucket next to the Hatteras.

"What do you want, Mr. Delano?"

"I want to settle this before it gets any worse for anybody. I'd like to meet with your attorney today. I'd like my client to sign the consent order keeping the accounts frozen. I'd like your client, you, to not file anything about that dock video. And Mr. Woodard?" He hesitated. "I'm withdrawing the letter. All of it. Nobody's asking you to leave your house."

"Does she know you're calling me?"

"She knows I'm calling Paulette. She doesn't know I'm calling you." He paused. "She doesn't take my calls right now, frankly. She's with Mr. Gale. And she thinks one of her friends leaked those messages, and she's planning to find out which one at dinner tonight."

"At the Dockside."

"How did you know that?"

"Because it's the first Wednesday," I said. "The Anchor Girls have dinner at the Dockside the first Wednesday of every month. Twenty-seven years, Mr. Delano. I paid for most of them."

I called Paulette. Then I called Gloria.

"She's going to make you say it in front of everybody," I said.

"I know." Gloria's voice was steady. "I'm going anyway. I'm done sitting at the end of that table."

At seven o'clock that Wednesday night, I walked into the Dockside and sat at the bar, where I could see the big round table by the window. The same table. The same six chairs plus two. The same waitress, a girl named Kayla with a ponytail who'd gotten the forty dollars on Sunday, came over and set a sweet tea in front of me without asking.

"You sure about this, Mr. Woodard?" she said.

"I'm just having dinner."

"Uh-huh," Kayla said.

Corinne came in at ten after. Preston was with her, in a sport coat this time, with a fresh bandage around his right wrist where he'd landed on the concrete. They sat down with the other women. Gloria came in last and took her usual chair at the end.

I couldn't hear it all from the bar. I didn't have to. I heard Corinne's voice go up, the way it does, and I heard her say, "Somebody at this table went to my husband. Somebody sent him our messages. I want to know who."

Nobody said anything.

Tonya looked at Marla. Marla looked at Ginny. Ginny looked at the menu.

Then Gloria put her napkin on the table and stood up.

"I did," she said.

The whole restaurant heard it. A man at the next table actually turned around in his chair.

"You," Corinne said. "You? I helped you through your divorce."

"You did," Gloria said. "So I knew exactly what it looked like from the inside. You were going to tell a judge he scared you, Cor. He's never scared anybody in his life except a seized engine. You wrote it down and all of us sent you champagne."

"That was a private conversation!"

"It was a plan," Gloria said. "And I was in it. I'm not anymore."

Corinne turned and saw me at the bar. She stood up so fast her chair scraped, and she came across the dining room in four long steps, Preston behind her.

"You put her up to this."

"She came to me," I said. "Sunday, three o'clock, in my yard. Ask her."

"I'm asking you!"

"Corinne," I said, and I kept my voice down, because I didn't need the whole room. The whole room was already listening. "Your lawyer called me four times this morning. He's not filing your fear paper. A lawyer in Jacksonville filed a police report on the bill of sale you signed. And the man standing behind you told your sister on Monday night that he needed the forty thousand back before the buyer found out."

She turned around and looked at Preston.

Preston didn't look at her. He was looking at the front door, because the front door had just opened, and Deputy Ray Fulcher was standing in it with another deputy, and a heavyset man in a golf shirt I'd never seen before, holding a folded paper.

"That's him," the man in the golf shirt said, and pointed. "That's Gale."

"Mr. Gale," Deputy Fulcher said, walking over. "Mr. Tanner here drove up from Jacksonville this afternoon, and there's a warrant out of Duval County, Florida, for obtaining property by false pretenses. Forty thousand dollars. Turn around for me."

"This is a civil matter," Preston said. "This is a contract dispute, she's the one who signed the—"

"Turn around, sir."

He turned around. The cuffs went on with that flat little ratcheting sound, and Preston Gale said, very loudly, to the whole dining room, "She told me she owned the boat! It was her idea! She said he'd never fight for anything!"

Corinne made a sound I'd never heard her make in twenty-seven years. Not a word. Just air leaving.

"You said you loved me," she said to him.

"I said I'd get you out," Preston said. "Lady, I said a lot of things."

They walked him out past the hostess stand. Lamont Tanner followed them, and stopped long enough to look at Corinne.

"Mrs. Woodard," he said, "your signature's on my bill of sale. My lawyer will be in touch with yours."

Then he was gone, and it was just the dining room, and the six women at the round table, and Kayla with a pitcher of tea frozen halfway to a glass, and my wife standing in front of me at the bar.

She reached into her purse. She took out my wedding ring. She'd been carrying it since Sunday. She set it on the bar next to my sweet tea.

"Darius," she said. Her voice was very small now. "Please. Can we just go home and talk about this? Just you and me? I'll call off the lawyers. I'll do whatever you want. You can have the boat. I just need you to help me with the Florida thing. Please. I can't do that by myself."

I looked at the ring. Twenty-seven years. The little scratch on the inside from the time I caught it in a winch in 2003.

I picked it up and handed it to Kayla.

"Put that toward her table's check," I said. "Brunch is on her. Dinner is too."
