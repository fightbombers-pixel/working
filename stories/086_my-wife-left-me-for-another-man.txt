"I'm leaving you for Percy, and your mother and your sister are coming to the wedding."

My wife Latoya said that at my mother's Sunday dinner table, in front of 14 people, with a bowl of collard greens in one hand and a big serving spoon in the other, like she was announcing the potato salad was ready.

Nobody gasped. That was the part that got me. My mother kept buttering her roll. My sister Bernadette looked at her phone. My cousin's husband said, "Pass the hot sauce," and somebody passed it.

I looked down at my plate. Pot roast, rice, gravy. Thirty-one years of Sunday dinners at that table, and I'd never once noticed how loud a fork is when nobody's talking.

"Jamaica," Bernadette said finally, to nobody in particular. "Montego Bay. We fly out in the morning. Toya's paying."

"With whose money?" I said.

Nobody answered that.

So I got up, carried my plate to the sink, and washed it. I dried it with my mother's dish towel, the one with the rooster on it. Then I walked back to the table, set the clean plate down in front of Latoya, took off my wedding ring, and laid it right in the middle of that plate.

"Congratulations," I said.

Twelve days later, all four of them would be standing on the sidewalk in front of my house with their suitcases and their sunburns, looking at each other like people who'd just heard a smoke alarm go off in a house they were still standing in.

What none of them knew was what was waiting in my mailbox at home. A long white envelope from the fraud department of Wiregrass Federal Credit Union, which had come the day before, and which I hadn't opened yet.

My name is Marvin Gibbs. I'm 54, and I've been a mechanic at the city bus garage in Dothan, Alabama, for 26 years. You learn one thing working on buses. A bus will tell you exactly what's wrong with it if you listen to the noise it makes when it thinks nobody's paying attention.

Hold on to that.

I got my keys off the hook by my mother's door. Behind me, I heard my mother finally speak.

"Let him go, Toya. He'll sulk a week and then he'll be fine. He always is."

And then Bernadette, laughing a little.

"He's got all that granddaddy money sitting in the bank. He'll be fine."

I stopped with my hand on the screen door. I didn't turn around. I just stood there a second, because something about that sentence made a noise I didn't like. The kind of noise a bus makes on a cold morning right before something lets go.

Then I went home and opened the envelope.

It was one page. "Dear Member, our account review team has identified unusual activity on your savings account ending in 4471. Please contact your branch at your earliest convenience."

Account 4471 was my granddaddy's money. When Augustus Gibbs died in 1996, he left me his house on Cherokee Avenue and $61,000. I was 26 and single. I put the money in a savings account in my name only and promised myself I'd never touch it unless something was on fire. I never had. Latoya wasn't on that account. Nobody was.

I logged in on my phone at the kitchen table.

The balance said $23,112.

I sat there and looked at that number for a long time. Then I heard Latoya's car in the driveway.

She came in with two empty suitcases from the hall closet and walked right past me toward the stairs.

"Toya."

"I'm packing, Marvin. I don't have time for a whole thing."

"Where's thirty-eight thousand dollars?"

She stopped on the second step.

"What are you talking about?"

"Granddaddy's account. There's thirty-eight thousand dollars missing."

"I don't know anything about your granddaddy's account. I'm not even on it. You made sure of that."

"I know you're not on it."

"So how could I touch it?"

"That's what I'm asking."

"You probably moved it yourself and forgot. You forget everything that isn't a bus."

"I didn't forget."

"Then call the bank. Not my problem." She went up two more steps, then turned around. "And while we're talking about money, you should know something."

She came back down, set the suitcases by the door, and pulled a folded packet out of her purse. She dropped it on the kitchen table in front of me.

"Complaint for Divorce. Circuit Court of Houston County." She tapped it with one long nail. "Filed two weeks ago. My lawyer says this is the marital home. Twenty-two years I've lived here. I painted every room. I picked every curtain. I'm asking for the house, and I'm going to get it."

"This is my granddaddy's house."

"It's our house. Percy's moving in when we get back."

"Percy's moving into my granddaddy's house."

"He gave notice on his condo. Don't make that face. You'll have thirty days after it's final to get your things out. My lawyer says that's generous."

"You've been busy."

"Somebody in this marriage had to be."

She picked up her suitcases and went upstairs. I heard the closet doors bang, and then I heard her on the phone, laughing.

"No, he just sat there," she was saying. "Like a bump on a log. I told you he would."

I read the divorce papers twice. Then I put them in a drawer and looked at the number on my phone one more time.

$23,112.

At nine o'clock the next morning I was standing at the branch on Ross Clark Circle in my work clothes, and the branch manager was waiting for me at the door. Her name was Helen Crowder. She was about 60, wore a gold cross and a cardigan in August, and had been the manager there since before cell phones.

"Mr. Gibbs. Thank you for coming in so fast. Please. My office."

She closed the door. She didn't sit down right away.

"I want you to know first that the credit union is going to make you whole," she said. "Whatever the review finds, that money is coming back to you. That's federal law and it's our policy."

"Okay."

"The second thing is harder." She sat, and she slid a stack of printouts across the desk. "Over the last five months, there were seven cash withdrawals from your account. All at this branch. All at teller window three. None of them with you present."

"How do you take money out of an account when the owner's not there?"

"You don't. Not unless a teller overrides the signature requirement with her own employee code." She put one finger on a line on the printout. "Every one of these was approved with the same code."

I looked at the code. Then I looked at the employee name printed next to it, which I'd known since the day she was born.

Bernadette Gibbs.

"My sister," I said.

"I'm so sorry."

"Who got the cash?"

Helen turned the printout over. Stapled to the back were copies of withdrawal slips. On the "received by" line, the signature was a big looping L with a little heart over the i.

"Your sister handed it across the counter to your wife," Helen said. "Every time. The camera above window three confirms it."

I sat there and thought about my mother saying "he'll be fine," and my sister laughing about granddaddy's money, and Latoya saying "I'm not even on it, you made sure of that."

She was right. She wasn't on it. She didn't need to be.

"What happens now?" I said.

"Your sister was placed on administrative leave this morning. She doesn't know that yet, because she called in for vacation through the end of next week." Helen folded her hands. "When she comes back, she'll sit down with our compliance officer. After that, it's out of my hands. We have to make a referral. It's not a choice."

"She's in Jamaica," I said. "At my wife's wedding."

Helen Crowder had been in banking for 38 years. I saw her try very hard not to react, and fail just a little.

"Well," she said. "Bless her heart."

My lawyer's name was Harriet Mabry. My cousin swore by her. She was 58, wore her reading glasses on a beaded chain, and drank coffee out of a mug that said "I Object" in red letters. She read Latoya's divorce complaint in about four minutes and laughed out loud once, at page three.

"She's asking for the house," Harriet said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Whose name is on the deed?"

"Mine. My granddaddy left it to me in '96. I got married in 2003."

"Anybody ever refinance it? Put her name on it? Take a joint loan against it?"

"No, ma'am. It's been paid off since 1989. Granddaddy didn't believe in banks except to keep his money in."

Harriet set her glasses down.

"Mr. Gibbs, in Alabama, property you inherit is yours. It's called separate property. That means it's not part of what gets split, as long as you never mixed it in with the marriage. She can paint every wall in that house purple. It's still your granddaddy's house."

"So she doesn't get it."

"She doesn't get it. But here's the thing you need to hear. Alabama doesn't freeze everything automatically the minute somebody files. Until a judge tells you otherwise, that house is yours to do with what you want."

"To do with what I want."

"Live in it. Rent it. Sell it." She looked at me over the top of her mug. "Why? What are you thinking?"

I took out my phone and showed her the text that had come in at six that morning. A selfie at the Dothan airport. Latoya in big sunglasses. My mother in a church hat with a flower on it. Bernadette with a neck pillow. And Percy Quinn, a used car dealer I'd bought exactly one Camry from, grinning like he'd won the lottery and the lottery was me.

Under the picture, Latoya had written: "Wish you were here. Not."

"They land in Montego Bay at two," I said. "The wedding's Saturday. They fly back a week from Friday."

"Eleven days."

"Eleven days."

Harriet looked at the picture for a long time. Then she pulled a fresh legal pad off the stack.

"Can you sell a house in eleven days?"

"I don't know. Can you?"

"I can't," she said. "But I know who can."

As it turned out, so did Wendell.

Wendell Ames was my supervisor at the garage. He was 63, chewed sunflower seeds all day, and had told me the same joke about a Greyhound and a goat at least 300 times. When I told him the whole story Tuesday morning, leaning on the fender of bus 14, he spat a shell into a coffee can and didn't laugh at all.

"Your own momma," he said.

"My own momma."

"And Bernadette. Little Bernie. I bought Girl Scout cookies off that child."

"Wendell. Focus."

"I'm focused. I'm just mad." He wiped his hands on a rag. "My cousin Roscoe buys houses. Cash. Fixes them up and rents them. He's not going to give you top dollar, but he closes fast, and he doesn't ask a lot of questions about why you're in a hurry."

"How fast?"

"He did one in Enterprise in seven days."

"Can he do one in nine?"

Wendell spat another shell.

"Marvin, for your granddaddy's house on Cherokee Avenue, with the oak floors? Roscoe would do one in nine if you told him to do it standing on his head."

Roscoe Tate came over that evening with a tape measure and a clipboard. He was a big, quiet man who walked through every room and touched the walls like a doctor checking ribs. He stopped in the dining room and looked at Latoya's purple accent wall for a long time.

"That's coming down," he said.

"I figured."

"Two-forty," he said. "Cash. As is. You take what you want, leave what you don't, I'll haul it. Close a week from Thursday at the title office."

My granddaddy's house had been appraised at $275,000 two years ago. I thought about that. Then I thought about Percy Quinn hanging his coat on my granddaddy's hook.

"Two-fifty," I said.

"Two-forty-five."

"Done."

We shook on it on the front porch. Mrs. Odessa Pryor from across the street was watching from her rocking chair like it was a ball game. I waved. She waved back.

Wednesday afternoon I was under bus 22 when my phone started buzzing on the creeper beside me. It buzzed eleven times in four minutes before I rolled out and picked it up.

Latoya. Calling from a number with an 876 area code.

"Marvin! What did you do to the card?"

"Which card?"

"The Visa! The Visa! They declined it at the front desk in front of everybody! Percy had to put the room on his card!"

"That's the Visa in my name."

"It's our Visa!"

"It's my Visa. You were an authorized user. I called them Monday and took you off."

"You can't do that!"

"I just did."

"We're on vacation! Your mother is here! Your mother's room is on that card!"

"Then my mother should've thought about whose card it was before she got on the plane."

I could hear wind on the line, and steel drums somewhere, and Percy in the background saying, "Give me the phone, give me the phone."

"I'm putting you on speaker," Latoya said. "Percy wants to talk to you."

"I don't want to talk to Percy."

"Marvin, my man!" Percy's voice came in loud, the way men talk on speaker when they think they're a radio station. "Listen. I get it. You're hurt. I'd be hurt too. But taking the card off your own mother's hotel room? That's low, brother. That's real low."

"Who drove my mother to the airport, Percy?"

"What?"

"Who drove her? Who bought her ticket? Who told her she'd have a nice room by the ocean?"

"Toya did. Out of love."

"With thirty-eight thousand dollars of my granddaddy's money. That my sister handed her across a counter."

Silence on the line. The steel drums kept going.

Then Latoya came back on, and her voice was different. Lower.

"I don't know what you think you know," she said.

"I know what I know."

"You've always been jealous of what I have with your family. Your mother loves me more than she loves you, and you can't stand it. That's what this is."

"Maybe."

"You were never there for any of them. I was the one who took your mother to her doctor. I was the one who helped Bernie with her divorce. You were at the bus barn."

"I was at work."

"You were always at work!"

"Somebody had to pay for the Visa."

"Don't you dare get smart with me. I gave you twenty-two years."

"And I gave you a house. You just thought it came with the marriage."

"It does come with the marriage. My lawyer says—"

"Your lawyer should read a deed."

She hung up. Then she called back and hung up again before I could answer. Then a text came, all capital letters.

"I'M HAVING MY LAWYER STOP WHATEVER YOU'RE DOING."

I put the phone back on the creeper and rolled under bus 22 again. Wendell's boots appeared beside me.

"That the bride?"

"That was the bride."

"She sound happy?"

"She sounded like the starter on bus 9."

Wendell laughed so hard he had to sit down on a tire.

Thursday night it was my mother's turn.

She called at a quarter to ten, which is late for Dolores Gibbs, who has been in bed by nine since the Carter administration. I knew before I picked up that somebody had handed her the phone and told her what to say.

"Marvin Augustus Gibbs."

"Hey, Momma."

"Don't you 'hey, Momma' me. Do you know what it was like at that front desk? Do you know how it felt, standing there in front of all those people with my pocketbook in my hand, and the girl saying 'declined, declined'?"

"No, ma'am. I've never been to Jamaica."

"Toya had to cry before that man would even give us the rooms."

"I bet she did."

"I raised you better than this. Your daddy would be ashamed. Cutting off your own mother on vacation."

"Momma, who paid for your ticket?"

"Toya did. Out of the goodness of her heart."

"Did you ever ask her where she got it?"

There was a pause on the line. A long one. Long enough that I could hear the ocean, and somebody laughing in another room, and my mother breathing.

"That's not a question a lady asks," she said.

"It's the only question, Momma."

"You always were stingy. Just like Augustus. Sitting on money like a hen on an egg."

"Granddaddy left that egg to me."

"Well, it's not doing anybody any good sitting in a bank."

That one sat there between us for a while. I thought about Bernadette at the dinner table saying "all that granddaddy money," and how everybody had seemed to know exactly how much was sitting there except the man whose name was on it.

"Momma," I said, "I love you. Enjoy the ocean. You're going to want to remember it."

I hung up before she could answer. I sat on the floor of my granddaddy's empty living room with my back against a box and my phone in my hand, and for about ten minutes I didn't do anything at all. Then I got up and taped another box.

Saturday was the wedding. I knew it was the wedding because at a quarter past six in the evening my phone lit up with a picture, and I opened it standing in my granddaddy's kitchen, surrounded by cardboard boxes.

It was taken on a beach. Latoya in a white dress, barefoot, with flowers in her hair. Percy in a linen suit, sunglasses on, even though the sun was going down behind them. My mother standing next to them in her church hat, smiling with every tooth she had. Bernadette on the other side, holding a glass of something pink.

There was a man in a straw hat behind them reading out of a little book. It wasn't a real wedding, not legally. You can't marry somebody in Jamaica while you're still married to a man in Alabama. But nobody in that picture looked like they cared.

Under it was the text.

"Don't be home when we get back. When it's over, it's over for me."

I read it twice. Then I read it out loud, to the boxes.

"Don't be home when we get back."

I typed one word back.

"Understood."

Five minutes later my phone rang. A Dothan number this time. Percy, on his own phone, with the sound of a party behind him.

"Marvin. Hey. Just a courtesy call, man to man."

"Go ahead."

"My movers are bringing my stuff Thursday. My condo lease is up, so it's all coming to the house. Couch, bedroom set, my big TV, my safe. Toya says the garage code is your birthday. Hope that's still right."

"It was."

"Was?"

"Have a good wedding, Percy."

"You know what, man? You're taking this better than I thought. Real mature. I respect that."

"Thank you."

"No hard feelings?"

"Percy," I said, "there aren't going to be any feelings at all."

I hung up, picked up the tape gun, and finished taping the box marked "Granddaddy's tools."

The hearing on Latoya's emergency motion was Tuesday morning, by phone, because she was still in Jamaica and her lawyer had asked the judge for an order to stop me from "dissipating the marital residence." That's lawyer for selling a house.

Harriet put the call on speaker in her office. Latoya's lawyer, a man named Clement Royce, did most of the talking. He said "marital residence" eleven times. I counted.

When he was done, the judge, a woman with a voice like dry toast, asked one question.

"Mr. Royce, whose name is on the deed?"

"Your Honor, the house has been the family home for twenty-two years—"

"I asked whose name is on the deed."

"Mr. Gibbs's, Your Honor. But—"

"Is it inherited?"

"It... was originally, Your Honor, but—"

"Ms. Mabry?"

"Inherited from his grandfather in 1996, Your Honor, seven years before the marriage. Never refinanced, never retitled, no marital funds in it. The probate order is attached as exhibit A."

There was a short pause and the sound of pages.

"The emergency motion is denied," the judge said. "Mr. Royce, I'd suggest your client find somewhere to stay."

Harriet ended the call and took a long sip from her "I Object" mug.

"Well," she said. "That's one."

Thursday morning a moving truck from a company called Wiregrass Hauling pulled up to my curb at eight sharp. Two young men climbed down and walked up to the porch where I was sitting on an overturned bucket with a cup of coffee.

"Delivery for Percy Quinn," the taller one said, reading off a clipboard. "Says the garage code is—"

"House is sold," I said. "Closing's at two."

"Sir?"

"Mr. Quinn doesn't live here and never will. The new owner's name is Roscoe Tate, and he doesn't want a couch."

The young man looked at his clipboard. Then he looked at Mrs. Pryor across the street, who was already out on her porch with her own cup of coffee.

"What do we do with all his stuff?"

"Is the move paid for?"

"Half up front. Half on delivery."

"Then I'd call Mr. Quinn," I said. "But he's in Jamaica, so I'd let it ring a while."

They put it all in storage at their warehouse in Ozark at $140 a week, billed to Percy. I know because the taller one told me while they were turning around in the driveway, and he didn't seem too sorry about it.

At two o'clock I sat at a long table at Wiregrass Title and signed my name eleven times. Roscoe signed his name eleven times. The closing agent slid a cashier's check across the table to me.

$245,000, less fees.

Roscoe shook my hand and put the keys to my granddaddy's house in his shirt pocket.

"That purple wall's coming down Saturday," he said.

"Leave the oak floors."

"Wasn't going to touch them."

When I walked out to my truck, my phone buzzed. Helen Crowder at the credit union.

"Mr. Gibbs. I wanted you to know before the weekend. Your thirty-eight thousand has been credited back to your account this afternoon."

"Thank you, Mrs. Crowder."

"And the compliance interview with your sister is set for Monday at nine." She paused. "The referral has already gone to the district attorney's office. Their investigator asked me to tell you he'd like to speak with you, too. And with your wife."

I sat in the truck with the engine off for a minute. Then I drove to a pawn shop on Main Street and bought a pair of cheap binoculars, the kind bird watchers use, for $19.

They were landing at three the next day.

Friday afternoon I was sitting on Mrs. Odessa Pryor's front porch across the street from my granddaddy's house, in her second-best rocking chair, drinking her sweet tea. She was 81 and had lived on Cherokee Avenue since the Eisenhower administration. She'd asked to borrow the binoculars about four seconds after I sat down.

"You don't need binoculars, Miss Odessa. It's sixty feet."

"I'm not looking at sixty feet," she said. "I'm looking at their faces. I want to see every bit of it."

Roscoe's work truck was parked in the driveway. A dumpster sat at the curb, half full of carpet. In the front yard stood a little metal sign Roscoe had pushed into the grass that morning: "Tate Properties. Coming Soon For Rent."

At twenty to four, Bernadette's silver Nissan came around the corner from the airport.

It pulled in behind Roscoe's truck because there was nowhere else to go. All four doors opened at once.

Latoya got out of the passenger side in a sundress and a straw hat, with her phone already in her hand. Percy climbed out of the back with his sunglasses pushed up on his head and a sunburn the color of a fire hydrant. My mother came out slow, holding the door frame, still wearing a lei made of plastic flowers. Bernadette popped the trunk.

They were laughing. That was the first thing I saw. They were all laughing at something Percy had said, and my mother was swatting at his arm.

Then Latoya looked up at the house.

She saw the dumpster first. Then the sign. Then the front door, which was standing wide open, with somebody's work light shining inside where her purple dining room wall used to be.

The laughing stopped one person at a time, like lights going out down a hallway.

"What is this?" Latoya said. "What's that sign?"

Percy walked up the driveway with his suitcase still in his hand and punched the garage keypad. My birthday. The little light blinked red. He punched it again. Red.

"Toya, the code doesn't work."

"Try it again."

"I tried it twice."

Roscoe came out the front door with a crowbar in one hand and a piece of purple drywall in the other. He looked at the four of them on the driveway the way a man looks at a flat tire.

"Can I help y'all?"

"Who are you?" Latoya said. "What are you doing in my house?"

"Your house?" Roscoe set the drywall down very carefully on the porch rail. "Ma'am, I closed on this house yesterday at two o'clock. I'm the owner. Name's Roscoe Tate."

"That's impossible."

"Title office didn't think so."

"My husband can't sell this house! There's a divorce! My lawyer filed a motion!"

"Yes, ma'am," Roscoe said. "I heard the judge denied it."

Across the street, Miss Odessa let out a little sound, half laugh, half hiccup, and adjusted the binoculars.

That's when my mother turned around and saw me.

I don't know what she expected. Maybe she expected me sitting on my own porch with my head in my hands. Maybe she expected me not to be there at all. She looked across Cherokee Avenue at me, sitting in Miss Odessa's rocking chair with a glass of sweet tea, and her hand went up to the plastic flowers around her neck.

"Marvin," she said. "Marvin, what did you do?"

I set my glass down and walked across the street. I didn't hurry. I'd been waiting twelve days. I figured they could wait forty more seconds.

"Hey, Momma," I said. "How was Jamaica?"

"Where's all your things? Where's the furniture? Where's my good casserole dish I left at Christmas?"

"In a storage unit in Headland. Your dish is in a box with your name on it. I'll bring it by."

"You sold your granddaddy's house?"

"I did."

"Augustus's house? You sold Augustus's house?"

"He left it to me, Momma. Not to Toya. Not to Percy. To me."

Latoya pushed past her. Her face was red under the sunburn, and her hat had come off and she hadn't noticed.

"You sold my house while I was out of the country."

"You told me not to be home when you got back."

"I didn't mean sell the house!"

"You said, 'When it's over, it's over for me.' I took you at your word. It's over."

"Where am I supposed to sleep tonight?"

"Percy's condo?"

Percy cleared his throat. "Gave that up. Lease ran out Wednesday."

"Then Percy's storage unit," I said. "In Ozark. His couch is in there. His big TV. His safe. Hundred and forty dollars a week. The moving company said they'd call you."

Percy pulled out his phone and looked at it. His face changed. I guessed he'd had it in airplane mode for a week, because the screen just kept buzzing and buzzing in his hand, notification after notification stacking up.

"Eight voicemails from Wiregrass Hauling," he said quietly.

"Probably about the second half of the bill."

"You can't do this!" Latoya was shouting now. Up and down the street, screen doors were opening. Mr. Tolbert from two houses down came out in his undershirt. The Hollins boys stopped their basketball game. "Twenty-two years! I am your wife!"

"You married Percy on Saturday. I saw the picture."

"That wasn't real! That was a beach ceremony! You know it wasn't legal!"

"Then you should've told Percy," I said. "He looked real happy about it."

Percy looked at her.

"What do you mean it wasn't real?"

"Not now, Percy."

"You said the divorce would be final when we got back."

"It will be. Soon. Thirty days. Don't start with me."

"You told me the house was coming in the divorce. You said your lawyer had it handled. I gave up my lease, Toya."

"I said not now!"

Bernadette hadn't said a word. She'd been standing by the open trunk of the Nissan this whole time with her phone in her hand. She'd turned it on when they landed, same as Percy, and while everybody else was shouting, she'd been listening to a voicemail with one finger pressed against her other ear.

Now she lowered the phone. She looked at me. I'd known that face for 48 years, since the day my parents brought her home from Flowers Hospital wrapped in a yellow blanket. I'd never once seen it look like that.

"Marvin," she said. "Marvin, that was Mrs. Crowder."

"I figured."

"She said I'm on administrative leave. She said I have a meeting Monday at nine with compliance. She said..." Her voice cracked. "She said there's an investigator from the district attorney's office who wants to talk to me."

Everything on that driveway stopped.

Latoya turned around very slowly and looked at Bernadette. Bernadette looked at Latoya. Then both of them, at the same time, looked at my mother.

And my mother looked at the ground.

Miss Odessa told me later she'd never seen anything like it through a pair of binoculars, and she'd watched a whole lot of things on Cherokee Avenue since 1958. Three women standing in a triangle on a driveway, still in their vacation clothes, with plastic flowers around their necks, all smiling a minute ago and now not one of them able to look the others in the eye.

"What's she talking about?" Percy said. "Toya? What investigator?"

"Nothing. It's a bank thing. It's Bernie's job."

"It's my granddaddy's money," I said.

Percy turned to me. "What money?"

"Thirty-eight thousand dollars. Taken out of a savings account in my name only. Seven times in five months. Teller window three. My sister's employee code on every override, and Toya's signature on every slip. There's a camera over that window, Percy. It's got all seven."

"That's a lie," Latoya said.

"It's on video, Toya."

"Your sister gave it to me! She said you said it was okay!"

Bernadette spun around. "I never said that! You told me he knew! You stood at my window and told me Marvin said it was fine, he just didn't want to come in!"

"You knew he didn't know! You said yourself he'd never check!"

"Because Momma said he never looks at his statements!"

And there it was. Out loud, on the driveway, in front of half of Cherokee Avenue.

My mother finally lifted her head.

"Marvin, baby," she said. "Baby, listen to me. It was for the trip. It was for family. Toya said she'd pay it back as soon as the house came through. She said you'd never even miss it. It was just sitting there."

"It was Granddaddy's, Momma."

"Augustus would've wanted us to have a nice time. He would've wanted the family to be together."

"Granddaddy wanted it there if something was on fire."

"Well, something's on fire now!" She grabbed my arm with both hands. "Bernie can't go to jail. Look at her. She can't. You have to call the bank and tell them you said it was okay. Tell them you forgot. You can do that for your sister."

"Momma."

"You can do that for me."

"Momma, the bank already gave me my money back. Yesterday. All thirty-eight thousand." I put my hand over hers, gently, and took it off my arm. "It's not my money anymore that's missing. It's theirs. And they've got a camera, and a code, and seven slips. They don't need me to say anything at all."

"Then what good are you?" she said.

I'd thought a lot about what I'd say to her when this moment came. I'd lain awake in my granddaddy's empty bedroom with the boxes all around me, rehearsing it. All of that went right out of my head.

"I was good for thirty-eight thousand dollars and a hotel room by the ocean," I said. "That's what I was good for, Momma. Y'all just didn't ask first."

She let go and stepped back like I'd slapped her. I hadn't. I never would. But I'd said it, and it was true, and everybody on that driveway knew it was true.

Percy picked up his suitcase. He looked at Latoya for a long second, the way you look at a car on your lot after you find out the odometer was rolled back.

"You paid for my wedding," he said, "with stolen money."

"Our wedding."

"You paid for my plane ticket with stolen money. You put me in a hotel with stolen money. My name's on that resort bill, Toya, because your card got declined. I paid for half that trip on my own card, and now there's an investigator."

"Percy, it's not like that—"

"I sell cars," he said. "I know exactly what it's like."

He started walking. Down the driveway, past the dumpster, past Roscoe's sign, out onto Cherokee Avenue, pulling his rolling suitcase behind him. The wheels made a long rattling noise on the sidewalk.

"Where are you going?" Latoya yelled after him.

"Ozark," he said, without turning around. "To get my couch."

"How are you even going to get there?"

He didn't answer. He just kept walking toward Main Street with his phone to his ear, and I heard him say, "Yeah, I need a ride. No, just one. Just me."

Latoya stood there in the middle of my granddaddy's driveway, with her hat in the grass behind her and her phone in her hand, and looked at me.

All the shouting had gone out of her. She looked smaller than I'd ever seen her. Twenty-two years, and I'd never once seen her look like she didn't know what to do next.

"Marvin," she said. Very quiet. "Marvin, please."

"Please what?"

"Please, I don't have anywhere to go. I have two suitcases and a sundress. Where am I supposed to go?"

"Bernie's?"

Bernadette shook her head without looking up. "Not my house."

"Momma's?"

My mother didn't say anything at all. She just stood there holding her plastic lei.

Latoya took one step toward me.

"Let me come with you," she said. "Wherever you're staying. Just for tonight. Just until we figure this out. We can figure this out, Marvin. We always figure it out. Twenty-two years. Please. Don't leave me standing out here with all these people watching."

Up and down the street, the screen doors were still open. Mr. Tolbert in his undershirt. The Hollins boys holding their basketball. Miss Odessa with her binoculars.

I took my truck keys out of my pocket.

"Toya," I said, "you told me not to be home when you got back."

I walked to my truck, climbed in, and started the engine.

"I'm not."
