"Should I even marry him? Y'all vote. Be honest."

My fiancée said it laughing into her phone on a Friday night, eight days before our wedding, live-streaming her bachelorette party from a rented cabin in Gatlinburg to 212 people. I know it was 212 because the little number was right there in the corner of my screen, next to the little hearts floating up. Eight women in matching pink T-shirts were squeezed onto a couch behind her, and every one of them screamed.

I was sitting at my kitchen table in Ashland, Kentucky, in my reading glasses, with a cup of decaf and the $9,500 receipt for the wedding venue still stuck to the refrigerator with a hardware store magnet. Two years engaged. Eight days out. And she was taking a poll.

I pulled up the little comment box at the bottom of the screen and typed two words with one finger, the way I type everything, and hit send.

"Don't bother."

Forty seconds later, the woman who'd asked the question was reading my answer out loud to the whole cabin, with her mouth hanging open and the phone shaking in her hand.

What she didn't know was who'd sent me the link to that stream in the first place.

My name is Bernard Tully. I'm 54, I've managed Cox Hardware and Supply on Winchester Avenue in Ashland for 19 years, and I've been a widower since my first wife, Marlene, passed in 2018. In a hardware store, you learn to tell a man who's come in to fix something from a man who's come in to look like he's fixing something. The first one asks you questions. The second one only asks you for the price. Remember that.

I didn't turn the stream off. I wanted to see it.

Evelyn Marsh is 49, and she's pretty in a way that makes waitresses refill her coffee without asking, and on that screen she looked exactly like a woman who'd just stepped on a rake. Behind her, the pink T-shirts had gone quiet. Somebody's hand was over somebody's mouth.

"Bernard?" she said into the phone, like I could hear her. "Bernie? Is that you?"

The comments started scrolling so fast I couldn't read them. Little crying-laughing faces. Somebody named BigSandyGirl typed "OMG HE'S WATCHING." Somebody else typed "GIRL." My own daughter's name went by once, and I didn't catch what she said.

"It's a joke," Evelyn said to the camera. Her voice was going up. "It's a joke, everybody, it's a bachelorette party, we're doing polls, it's what you do! Bernie, honey, it's a bit! Tell them it's a bit!"

I didn't type anything else. I closed the stream. I picked up my phone, scrolled down to "Lou Ann Hatch, Hatch Farm Venue," and sent a text.

"This is Bernard Tully. Please cancel the Tully-Marsh wedding on the 18th. Keep the deposit. I'm sorry for the trouble."

I'd just set the phone down on the table when it rang.

It wasn't Evelyn. It was the maid of honor. Her sister, Joanie Kemper.

"Bernard," Joanie said, and she was whispering, and I could hear a bathroom fan going over her voice. "Bernard, please, please tell me you didn't just cancel the venue."

"I just canceled the venue."

"Oh, God. Oh my God." I heard something hit the sink. "Bernard, listen to me. I'm locked in the bathroom. She's out there screaming at everybody asking who sent you the link. She's going through people's phones. Please. You can't tell her it was me."

"I wasn't going to."

"And Bernard..." Joanie's breath hitched. "You don't understand. She closed on her house today. This afternoon. Before we drove down. She sold it. She signed it all over at the title company at two o'clock and she's got 30 days to be out, because she was moving in with you after the wedding. She doesn't have anywhere to go." A pause. "Except me. She's going to move in with me, Bernard. Me and my husband and our two teenagers in a three-bedroom ranch in Flatwoods."

I sat there with my decaf going cold.

"Joanie," I said. "Why'd you send me that link?"

There was a long quiet on the line. The bathroom fan hummed.

"Because she's been saying it for weeks," Joanie said, very low. "Not as a joke. And because I watched her do this exact thing to a man named Gary Hensley in 2009, eight days before that wedding too, and I didn't say a word. And I've had to look at that man in the Kroger for seventeen years." She swallowed. "I couldn't do it twice."

Somebody pounded on the bathroom door behind her. I heard it through the phone, and I heard Evelyn's voice, high and shrill.

"Joanie! Joanie, open this door! Did you send it? Did you send him that link? Open this door right now!"

"I have to go," Joanie whispered. And she hung up.

I had seven missed calls by midnight and 23 by the time I gave up on sleeping and got dressed at five Saturday morning.

I opened the store at seven like I've done every Saturday for 19 years. Saturdays at a hardware store start early. Contractors need their screws, old men need their coffee, and the old men in Ashland have been drinking their free coffee at the little round table by the key machine since before I ever worked there.

Three of them were already sitting there when I unlocked the door. Lonnie Chaffin, retired from the refinery. Dub Estep, retired from the railroad. And Vernon Cox, who owns the store and is 81 years old and still comes in every single morning to sit by the key machine and tell me what I'm doing wrong.

They all looked up at me at the same time.

"Morning," I said.

"Morning, Bernard," said Vernon. He took a long sip of coffee. "Saw your fiancée on the Facebook last night."

"Did you."

"My granddaughter showed me. She said it was going around." He set the cup down. "She said Evelyn asked 212 people if she ought to marry you."

"She did."

"And you said 'Don't bother.'"

"I did."

Lonnie Chaffin snorted into his coffee and had to wipe his chin. Dub Estep slapped the table.

"Two words," Dub said. "Two words, and that's the whole sermon."

"Now Dub," Vernon said, but he was grinning. He looked back at me over his glasses, and the grin went away. "You all right, son?"

I thought about it. I'd thought about it all night, lying in the dark listening to the phone buzz on the nightstand.

"I'm all right," I said. "I'm not happy. But I'm all right."

"You canceled it? The wedding?"

"Texted the venue last night."

"Before or after she started calling?"

"Before."

Vernon nodded slowly, like I'd just given him the right answer on a quiz.

"Marlene would have liked that," he said. "She never did have any patience for people asking questions they already knew the answer to."

I went behind the counter and turned on the register. My phone buzzed in my shirt pocket. I took it out and looked.

"Thirty-one missed calls," I said.

"Answer it," Lonnie said.

"No."

"Then read us one."

I scrolled up to the newest text and read it out loud, because I figured these three had earned it.

"'Bernie, I'm driving home right now. I'll be in Ashland by noon. We need to talk face to face. I'm coming to the store.'"

The three of them looked at each other.

"Well," Vernon Cox said, and settled back in his chair with his coffee. "I believe I'll stay for lunch."

She came in at ten after twelve.

Saturday noon at Cox Hardware is the busiest hour of the week. There were maybe fifteen customers in the store. A contractor named Hobie Carver was at the counter buying a box of deck screws. A young couple was in the paint aisle arguing about gray. Two of the Little League coaches were looking at chalk liners. And the three old men were still at the round table by the key machine, on their third pot of free coffee, pretending to read the Daily Independent.

The bell over the door rang, and Evelyn walked in still wearing the pink T-shirt.

It said "BRIDE" across the front in silver glitter. I don't think she realized she still had it on. She'd driven four hours straight from Gatlinburg, and her mascara had run and dried and run again, and her hair was up in a clip with pieces coming down.

She walked straight down the center aisle to the counter, past the paint, past the chalk liners, past Hobie Carver with his deck screws, and she put both hands flat on the counter in front of me.

"How could you," she said.

"Afternoon, Evelyn."

"Don't 'afternoon' me. How could you do that to me? In front of everybody?"

"You asked a question."

"It was a joke! It was a bachelorette party! Everybody does polls! It's a thing! You don't understand because you're 54 and you've never been on TikTok in your life!"

"You asked 212 people if you should marry me," I said. "I answered."

Behind her, Hobie Carver very carefully set his box of deck screws down on the counter and backed up two steps.

"You humiliated me," Evelyn said. "Do you understand that? Do you understand what you did? I was live. My whole church saw it. My boss saw it. My cousins in Huntington saw it. People are making videos of it, Bernard. Somebody put it to music."

"What music?"

"I don't know! Some song! It doesn't matter!"

"It's that Tammy Wynette song," Lonnie Chaffin offered from the round table. "'D-I-V-O-R-C-E.' My granddaughter showed me."

Evelyn spun around and looked at the old men like she'd just noticed they were there. Lonnie raised his coffee cup to her, polite as a deacon.

She turned back to me and lowered her voice, which for Evelyn meant it was only loud enough for the first two aisles.

"Bernie. Honey. Listen to me. I'm sorry, okay? I'm sorry. I had too much wine. The girls were egging me on. Joanie started it. It was Joanie's idea, the poll. I didn't mean it."

"You've been saying it for weeks."

Her face went still.

"Who told you that?"

"Does it matter?"

"Who told you that, Bernard?"

I didn't answer. She looked at me a long time. I watched her try to figure it out, and I watched her land on her sister, because who else would it be, and I watched her decide not to say so out loud in a hardware store full of people.

"You canceled the venue," she said instead. "Lou Ann called me at eight this morning to ask if I knew. Eight in the morning. On my bachelorette weekend."

"I canceled the venue."

"You can't just cancel a wedding over a joke!"

"You can't just ask if you should marry somebody, eight days before, as a joke."

"Everybody laughed!"

"I didn't."

"Well, you're no fun, Bernard! That's the whole problem! You've never been any fun! You stand behind this counter selling bolts to old men and you go home and watch the weather and you're asleep by nine-thirty!"

"Then you won't miss me."

She stopped. Somebody in the paint aisle dropped a stir stick.

"That's not what I..." She took a breath. She changed gears. I watched her change them, the way you watch a man try three keys on a ring before he finds the right one. "Bernie. I sold my house. Yesterday. I closed on it. I did that for us. For you. So we could start our life in your house on Bath Avenue. And now I've got 30 days to get out and nowhere to go. Do you understand? You're making me homeless. Over a joke."

"I didn't ask you to sell your house before the wedding."

"It was the smart thing to do! The market's good!"

"Then you've got the money from it."

"That's not the point!"

"What is the point, Evelyn?"

"The point is you owe me!"

The whole store was quiet now. The young couple in the paint aisle had stopped arguing about gray. The Little League coaches had put down the chalk liners. Hobie Carver was looking at the ceiling like there was something interesting up there.

"I don't owe you anything," I said. "I gave you a ring. I gave you two years. I put $9,500 down on a barn because you said you'd always wanted a barn wedding. And last night you asked 212 strangers whether I was worth showing up for." I picked up Hobie's box of deck screws and rang them up, $34.18, and handed him his change. "Here you go, Hobie."

"Thank you, Bernard," Hobie said, very softly, and left without his receipt.

Evelyn stared at me.

"That's it?" she said. "You're just going to stand there and ring up screws?"

"It's Saturday. I've got customers."

"I'm your customer!"

"No, ma'am," I said. "You came in to look like you were fixing something. You haven't asked me one question yet. Just the price."

She didn't understand what I meant. I didn't expect her to. But Vernon Cox, at the round table by the key machine, set his coffee cup down very gently in its saucer, and I knew he did.

Evelyn reached down and pulled the engagement ring off her finger. It was a half-carat solitaire. I'd bought it at Cooper's Jewelers on Thirteenth Street two years ago for $3,800 and paid it off in eleven months.

She threw it on the counter. It bounced once and rolled into the bin of loose washers by the register.

"Fine," she said. "Keep it. Keep everything. You'll be sorry. You're going to be alone in that big house for the rest of your life with your weather channel and your bolts. Nobody else is going to want a boring old widower who smells like WD-40."

"Maybe not," I said.

She turned and walked out. The bell rang over the door. Through the front window I watched her get into her Equinox and sit there with both hands on the wheel, not starting it, for almost a full minute.

Then she started it and pulled out onto Winchester Avenue without looking, and a city bus had to stand on its brakes.

Nobody in the store said anything for a while. Then Dub Estep got up from the round table with his cane, walked over to the counter, reached into the bin of loose washers, fished out the ring and set it down in front of me.

"Two words last night," Dub said. "One sentence today. You're getting long-winded, Bernard."

Joanie Kemper came to my house that night.

She didn't come alone. Her husband, Rusty, came with her, a big quiet man who drives a coal truck and stood on my porch steps with his hands in his jacket pockets like he wasn't sure he was allowed to be there.

"She wanted to come by herself," Rusty said. "I didn't think that was a good idea."

"Come in. Both of you."

I made coffee. Real coffee, not decaf. I had a feeling we'd need it.

Joanie sat at my kitchen table right where I'd been sitting the night before, and she looked at the hardware store magnet on the refrigerator holding up the $9,500 receipt from Hatch Farm.

"She knows it was me," Joanie said. "She figured it out at the cabin. She took my phone while I was in the shower this morning and saw the text I sent you. Then she made Paige drive her home, and she left me there." Joanie laughed, a short ugly laugh. "Rusty had to drive four hours down to Gatlinburg to come get me."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. I'd do it again." She wrapped both hands around her coffee mug. "Bernard, I need to tell you some things. And then I'll never bring any of it up again, I swear."

"Go ahead."

"It wasn't a joke. The poll. She's been saying it since August. 'I don't even know if I should marry him.' 'I don't even know if I want to.' At my house. At Mom's. At the beauty shop. And every time somebody asked her why she was doing it then, she said the same thing." Joanie looked up at me. "She said, 'Because he's got that paid-off house on Bath Avenue and Vernon Cox's pension plan, and I'm tired of working.'"

I didn't say anything. Rusty shifted his weight on the back step where he was standing, half in and half out of the kitchen.

"And she said you'd never cancel," Joanie said. "She said it last night, in the cabin, before the stream. She said, 'Bernie's the nicest man in Boyd County. I could stand up at the altar and say I'd rather marry the preacher and he'd still say I do.' And everybody laughed. And I went in the bathroom and sent you the link."

"Why last night? Why not in August?"

Joanie was quiet a long time.

"Because in August I still thought she'd change her mind and just leave you, like a decent person," she said. "And last night I realized she was going to marry you instead. And that was worse."

"Tell me about Gary Hensley," I said.

Joanie closed her eyes.

"Gary was a lineman for the power company. Sweet man. Big ears. He had a little farm out toward Grayson that his grandparents left him." She opened her eyes. "Evelyn was going to marry him in October of 2009 at the First Baptist in Catlettsburg. Eight days before, at her shower, she stood up in front of forty women and said, 'I don't even know why I'm doing this, except for the farm.' Somebody told Gary. Gary showed up at Mom's house that night and asked her straight out if it was true."

"What did she say?"

"She said it was a joke." Joanie smiled, without anything in it. "And Gary married her anyway, Bernard. Did you know that? He married her. And three years later she left him for a car dealer in Huntington and got half the farm in the divorce. Gary sold the other half to pay her off. He works at the Kroger deli now. I see him every Tuesday. He always asks how she's doing."

Rusty cleared his throat from the back step.

"Tell him the other thing," he said.

"Rusty."

"Tell him, Joanie. He ought to know."

Joanie set her mug down.

"She already has a man in Huntington picked out," she said. "For after. If you'd gone through with it. She showed us his picture in the cabin. He sells boats. She said she'd give it two years with you and then she'd get the Bath Avenue house in the divorce, same as Gary's farm, and go." She looked at me, and her eyes were full. "That's what I couldn't stand, Bernard. That she'd already planned the ending. Before the wedding."

I sat there and thought about Marlene. Marlene, who'd taken nineteen years of my bad jokes and my early bedtimes and called me a boring old man every single morning and meant it as the nicest thing in the world. Marlene, who'd died in the hospice in Ashland with her hand in mine and said, "Don't you let anybody take advantage of you after I'm gone, Bernie. You're too nice. Somebody's going to try." I'd laughed at her. I'd told her nobody'd want an old hardware man.

"Thank you, Joanie," I said.

"You're not mad?"

"At you? You're the only one who asked me a question."

She didn't understand that either. But Rusty did. He nodded once from the back step.

"She's moving in with us Monday," Joanie said, standing up. "For thirty days. Then I'm done. I've told her."

"Good luck."

"Bernard." She stopped at the door. "She's going to come at you. She's going to come at you every way she knows. Please don't let her in."

"I won't."

"Everybody says that."

"Joanie," I said. "I texted a venue at eleven o'clock at night to cancel a wedding I paid for. I think I'm past everybody."

Lou Ann Hatch called me after church on Sunday. She's run the venue on her family's old tobacco farm outside Catlettsburg for eleven years, and she sounded like a woman who'd seen a lot of weddings and a few funerals and couldn't tell anymore which was worse.

"Bernard, I got your text. I'm sorry, hon. I saw the video. My niece showed me."

"Everybody's niece showed everybody."

"Well, here's what I can do. The deposit's nonrefundable, that's in the contract, and I'd keep it if I had to. But I've got nothing else booked on the 18th, and the barn's set up, and the tables are rented. If you want to use the date for something else, anything, it's yours. Family reunion. Church supper. I don't care. Just don't let that barn sit empty on a Saturday in October."

I stood in my kitchen with the phone against my ear and looked at the receipt on the refrigerator.

"Lou Ann," I said, "how many people does that barn hold?"

"Two hundred, seated."

"Cox Hardware turns 75 years old this month," I said. "Vernon Cox's daddy opened it in 1951. Vernon's 81. Every year I've told him we ought to throw a customer picnic, and every year he says it costs too much." I took a breath. "Can you do pulled pork for two hundred?"

Lou Ann laughed out loud.

"Hon," she said, "I can do pulled pork for two thousand."

My daughter drove down from Lexington that evening without calling first, which is how I knew she was worried.

Callie is 29, a nurse at UK Medical Center, and she has her mother's habit of walking into a kitchen and opening the refrigerator before she says hello. She stood there with the door open looking at a half a ham and a jar of pickles.

"Daddy, you don't have any food."

"I've got ham."

"You've got ham from the Fourth of July." She shut the door and turned around and looked at me. "I saw it. The stream. Kayla from high school sent it to me. I was on shift. I watched it in the supply closet."

"I figured."

"You typed 'Don't bother.'"

"I did."

"Daddy." She sat down across from me. "With one finger. I could tell. It took you like forty seconds."

"It's a small keyboard."

She laughed, and then her face crumpled up the way it used to when she was nine and fell off her bike, and she reached across the table and grabbed my hand.

"I never liked her," she said. "I'm sorry. I tried. I tried for two years because you seemed happy. But she called me 'the stepdaughter' at Easter like it was a job title. And she asked me at Thanksgiving if Mom left you anything in her will." Callie wiped her eyes. "I didn't tell you because I didn't want to be that kid. The one who ruins her dad's second chance."

"You weren't that kid."

"Somebody sent you the link, though, didn't they?" She looked at me. "It wasn't me. I'd never have had the nerve."

"It doesn't matter who sent it."

"It matters to me. Whoever it was, I want to send them a fruit basket."

"Send it to your Aunt Joanie," I said, before I could stop myself.

Callie's mouth fell open. Then she started to laugh, and she laughed so hard she had to put her forehead down on my kitchen table, and after a minute I laughed too, the first real laugh I'd had since Friday night, the two of us sitting there in the kitchen Marlene painted yellow in 2004, laughing like idiots over a jar of pickles and a ham from July.

"Okay," Callie said, when she could talk. "Okay. Here's what's going to happen. I'm going to Kroger. I'm buying you real food. And you're not going to answer the phone tonight, no matter how many times it rings."

"I wasn't going to."

"Daddy. Promise."

"I promise."

She grabbed her keys and stopped at the door.

"Mom would have typed it faster," she said. "But she'd have typed the same thing."

The calls ran 44 on Sunday, 29 on Monday and 17 on Tuesday. I know because Vernon Cox made me read the number out loud every morning at the round table like a stock report, and Dub kept a tally on the back of a paint chip.

"Seventeen," Dub said Tuesday. "She's slowing down."

"She's thinking," Lonnie said. "That's worse."

Tuesday evening her mother came to my front porch.

Ida Marsh is 76. She taught third grade at Poage Elementary for 38 years and still talks like there's a class in front of her. She came up my porch steps slow, holding the rail, and she wouldn't sit down when I offered her the swing.

"Bernard," she said. "Evelyn asked me to come over here and tell you that she's sorry and that she loves you and that you're breaking her heart."

"Yes, ma'am."

"I told her I'd come." Ida looked at me over her glasses. "I didn't tell her what I'd say when I got here."

"What are you going to say, Mrs. Marsh?"

"I'm going to say I've raised two daughters. One of them sent you a link. The other one sold her house the day before she asked 212 people if she should marry you." She pressed her lips together. "I'm 76 years old, Bernard, and I'm too tired to keep lying for my own child. Gary Hensley came to my door in 2009 and asked me if it was true, and I told him no. I've had to look at that man at the Kroger deli for seventeen years, the same as Joanie."

"Yes, ma'am."

"So I came to tell you no. That's all. Don't take her back." She turned around and took hold of the porch rail. "She'll tell you I said she loves you. I didn't. Have a good evening, Bernard."

Wednesday morning a woman I barely knew came into the store and stood in the plumbing aisle for ten minutes holding a wax ring she didn't need.

I recognized her from the stream. She'd been on the end of the couch in a pink T-shirt, laughing the loudest. Her name was Paige Riddle, and she worked with Evelyn at the insurance agency on Greenup Avenue, and she was the one who'd driven Evelyn home from Gatlinburg and left Joanie in the cabin.

She finally came up to the counter and set the wax ring down.

"That's $4.29," I said.

"I don't need it. I don't even know what it is." She didn't look at me. "Mr. Tully, I came to say I'm sorry."

"For what?"

"For laughing. On the video. I was the loud one." She twisted the strap of her purse around her finger. "I've watched it about thirty times since Friday. Everybody at the office has. And every time, the part I can't stand isn't her asking. It's me. It's me laughing, like it was the funniest thing in the world, a man's wedding."

"You'd had some wine."

"I'd had some wine," she agreed. "But I'd heard her say it sober plenty of times. At lunch. At the copier. 'I don't even know if I want to marry him.' And I'd laugh then too." She finally looked up. "She told us on the drive home that she'd get you back. She said you'd cave by the 18th. She said you always cave."

"Do I?"

"I don't know you, Mr. Tully." Paige picked up the wax ring and put it back down. "But I don't think you're going to."

"No, ma'am."

"Good." She took a breath. "And I wanted you to know Joanie's not the only one who's done lying for her. I told our boss yesterday that I wasn't going to cover for her long lunches anymore. She's been meeting some man from Huntington on company time since summer."

I rang up the wax ring anyway, because she'd held it so long it seemed wrong not to, and she paid for it in quarters out of the bottom of her purse, and left.

Wednesday night, a little after one in the morning, my phone rang, and it was Evelyn, crying.

"Bernie, my car broke down. I'm on 23 by the Bellefonte exit. It's dark and there's trucks going by and I'm scared. Please come get me. Please."

I sat up in bed. My heart was going. I won't pretend it wasn't.

"Are you safe? Are you off the road?"

"I'm on the shoulder. Please, Bernie. You're ten minutes away."

"Stay in the car with the doors locked and your flashers on," I said. "I'm calling Tater."

"Who?"

"Tater Combs. Combs Towing. He's five minutes from the Bellefonte exit and he's got a light bar on his truck you can see from Ohio."

"Bernie, I don't want a tow truck! I want you!"

"You'll be safer with Tater."

I hung up and called Tater Combs, who has towed every car in Boyd County at least once and owes me for about forty years of discount hitch pins. He called me back at a quarter to two.

"Bernard," Tater said. "Your girl's Equinox is sitting on the shoulder by Bellefonte. Flashers on. Doors locked. Engine runs fine. I started it myself. Gas tank's three-quarters full. There's not one thing wrong with that car."

"Is she okay?"

"She's fine. She's mad. She said a word to me I ain't heard since Korea." Tater paused. "You want me to tow it anyway? I could. Just for the principle of the thing."

"No. Tell her goodnight."

"She already left," Tater said. "Drove off while I was talking to you. Bernard, she did seventy-five getting back on the interstate."

The last rung came Thursday at the store, and it came on paper.

Evelyn walked in at closing, a little before six, in a nice blouse and her hair done, calm as Sunday, and she laid a sheet of paper on the counter in front of me. It was a lease application for a two-bedroom apartment at the Sutton Place complex in Russell. Under "Co-Signer," the line was blank.

"They won't approve me by myself," she said. "My credit isn't good enough without the house. I just need your signature, Bernie. That's all. Just a signature. Then you never have to see me again."

I looked at the paper. I looked at her.

"No," I said.

"It's one signature!"

"No."

"After two years? You won't even do that? You'd let me live with my sister and her two teenagers and her coal-truck husband because you're too proud to sign one piece of paper?"

"It's not pride, Evelyn. A co-signer is a man who promises to pay if you don't. I've watched you not pay for two years."

She picked up the lease application, folded it in half, then in half again, very carefully.

"You think you're so smart," she said. "You think the whole town's on your side. You think those three old fossils by the key machine think you're some kind of hero. Well, Saturday was supposed to be my wedding day, Bernard. And I'm going to be at Hatch Farm on Saturday whether you like it or not, because I paid for half of that barn."

"You didn't pay for any of it."

"I paid with two years of my life!"

She walked out. The bell rang over the door.

Vernon Cox, who was the only one still sitting at the round table, set his coffee down.

"Bernard," he said, "is she talking about the picnic?"

"She's talking about the picnic."

The night before the 18th I drove out to Hatch Farm after closing with Vernon, Dub and Lonnie in the cab of my truck, all four of us squeezed in like sardines, because Lou Ann needed help setting up two hundred folding chairs and the three of them refused to be left out of it. They didn't set up a single chair. They sat on hay bales and told me where every chair should go.

"Bernard," Dub said, around nine o'clock, while I was unfolding chair number one hundred and something. "What're you going to do if she shows up tomorrow?"

"She'll show up."

"I know she'll show up. I asked what you're going to do."

I set the chair down in its row and straightened it.

"Same thing I did last Friday night," I said. "Same two words."

Vernon Cox nodded from his hay bale like a judge.

"Two words," he said. "Don't you dare make it three."

Saturday the 18th came up clear and cool, the kind of October day in eastern Kentucky when the hills across the Big Sandy go orange and red all at once and every pickup in the county has its windows down.

Hatch Farm sits on a ridge above the river, and the barn is a big red tobacco barn with the sides opened up and string lights hung from the rafters and long tables covered in white paper. Lou Ann had kept every one of the string lights. She'd taken down the wedding arch and put up a banner instead that one of the Little League coaches painted for us. It said "COX HARDWARE AND SUPPLY, 75 YEARS, THANK YOU ASHLAND" in red letters about two feet high.

By three in the afternoon there were 180 people under that roof. Contractors and their wives. Retired men from the refinery and the railroad. Little League teams in their jerseys. The young couple from the paint aisle, who'd picked a gray after all. Hobie Carver and his whole crew. Tater Combs, who'd parked his tow truck at the end of the gravel drive with the light bar flashing, just for show. Joanie and Rusty Kemper came, and stood at the back, and Joanie cried a little when she saw the string lights. Ida Marsh came and sat with the three old men at a table by the pulled pork, and she and Vernon Cox argued about the Cincinnati Reds for an hour.

At four o'clock I climbed up on a hay bale with a microphone Lou Ann had found in her office, and everybody quieted down, and I said a few words about Vernon's daddy opening the store with one room and a nail barrel in 1951, and about how a hardware store is the only place left in America where a man can walk in, say "I need a thing that goes on the other thing," and walk out with it. Everybody laughed. Then I asked Vernon Cox to stand up, and 180 people gave an 81-year-old man in a Cox Hardware ball cap a standing ovation, and he stood there with his cane and his mouth working and couldn't say a word.

That was when the Equinox came up the gravel drive.

It came fast. It parked crooked, half on the grass, right beside Tater's tow truck. And Evelyn got out wearing a white dress.

It wasn't her wedding dress. I'd never seen her wedding dress. But it was white, and it was long, and she'd had her hair done up with flowers in it, and she walked across the gravel and through the big open barn doors and down the center aisle between the long tables, between 180 people with pulled pork sandwiches, like it was exactly what she'd planned to do on the 18th of October all along.

The barn went quiet, one table at a time, the way water goes still.

She stopped at the foot of the hay bale I was standing on. She looked up at me. Her eyes were wet and her chin was up.

"Bernard Tully," she said, loud, for everybody. "This was supposed to be our wedding day."

I didn't say anything. I still had the microphone in my hand.

"I made a mistake," she said. "I'm saying it in front of everybody. I said a stupid thing at a stupid party, and I'm sorry, and I'm standing here in front of this whole town in a white dress asking you to forgive me. Two years, Bernie. Two years. Doesn't that count for anything?"

Somebody's baby fussed. Somebody hushed it. Up by the pulled pork, Ida Marsh had put her hand over her eyes.

"Evelyn," I said, and I set the microphone down on the hay bale, because I didn't need it. "You asked 212 people if you should marry me."

"It was a joke!"

"Gary Hensley got told it was a joke too."

Her face went red, then white, then red again. Out of the corner of my eye I saw Joanie at the back of the barn take hold of Rusty's arm.

"Who told you about Gary?"

"Does it matter?"

"Who told you, Bernard?"

"Everybody who loved you enough to stop lying for you," I said.

She looked around the barn then. She looked at the long tables and the string lights and the banner. She looked at her mother sitting with the old men. She looked at her sister at the back. She looked at 180 people from Ashland, Kentucky, who'd seen the video, every last one of them, and not one face looking back at her was the face she wanted.

"Fine," she said, and her voice cracked straight down the middle. "Then I'll ask you one more time, right here, in front of all of them. And you'd better think real hard before you answer, Bernard Tully, because I'm not going to ask again." She lifted her chin. "Should I even marry you?"

I climbed down off the hay bale, so I was looking her straight in the eye, the way Marlene would have wanted me to.

"Don't bother," I said.
