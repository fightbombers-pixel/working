"Now that I'm in charge, you're out!"

My wife said it from the doorway of my office at Castille Marine Supply in Mobile, Alabama, at eight in the morning on a Wednesday, two days after we buried her father. She said it loud on purpose. Behind her in the hallway stood the HR manager, two girls from accounts payable, our CFO and the yard foreman, who happened to be our son.

On my desk, centered on the blotter like a place setting, was a single sheet of company letterhead. It was a resignation letter. My name was typed at the bottom, with a blank line over it for my signature.

I'd worked for that company for twenty-six years. I'd run the yard, the warehouse and the trucks since 1999. Her father had hired me the summer before I married his daughter, and the last thing he ever said to me, in the ICU at Springhill Medical Center, was "Don't let her sell it, son."

I picked up the letter. I read it once, all the way through, the way I read a bill of lading.

"Perfect," I said.

Annette blinked. That wasn't the word she'd rehearsed for.

I set the letter back on the blotter, unsigned, and leaned back in my chair. Out in the hallway, Wyatt's mouth fell open.

Then her phone buzzed in her hand. She looked down at it, and I watched the color go out of her face like somebody had pulled a plug.

Eleven days later, she'd be sitting at the far end of her father's long boardroom table, reading her father's handwriting out loud, and she'd have to stop twice to get through it.

What she didn't know that Wednesday morning was that there was a second envelope in her father's lawyer's safe, sealed with red wax like something out of a movie, and on the front, in her daddy's big square printing, it said To Be Read Aloud At The First Shareholders' Meeting.

My name is Cliff Abbott. I'm fifty-eight. I'm vice president of operations at a marine supply company on the Theodore Industrial Canal that sells everything from shackles to anchor chain to the shrimp boats and supply vessels of the Gulf Coast. A man who runs a yard learns one thing early: whoever signs the delivery ticket doesn't own the cargo. It's the paper behind the paper that tells you that. Hold on to that.

"What is it?" Chet Rawlins said from the hallway. He's our CFO, forty-seven, with a pair of tortoiseshell glasses and a quarter-zip pullover he wears in August. He moved up next to Annette's shoulder to see her phone.

She turned it so he couldn't. That was interesting.

"It's nothing," she said. "It's Lionel. Daddy's lawyer. Estate stuff." She looked at me, and her voice came back, but thinner. "You have until noon to sign that, Cliff. Then security walks you out."

"I'll be here," I said.

She turned and went down the hall fast, her heels clicking, with her phone held up close to her face, reading. Chet hurried after her. The HR manager, Mrs. Trahan, looked at me with something like pity and followed them.

Wyatt stayed. He came in and shut my door. He's twenty-six, six-foot-three, and runs the outside crew. He's got my build and his mother's temper, and it was coming up off him like heat off a parking lot.

"Dad," he said. "What the hell was that?"

"Your mother fired me."

"She can't fire you. Granddaddy's been dead three days."

"Your grandfather's board voted her president yesterday afternoon. Her and Chet and Mr. Gaudet. Three to nothing. I wasn't invited."

"So you're just going to sit there?"

"Son, did you see her face when that email came in?"

He thought about it.

"She looked like she saw a ghost," he said.

"She did," I said. "Your granddaddy's."

Lionel Ardoin's office is on Government Street in a white house with green shutters that used to belong to a cotton broker. Lionel is sixty-eight, wears seersucker from Easter to Labor Day, and has been Ray Castille's lawyer since they were both young and broke. At eleven-fifteen that morning, I was sitting in a wingback chair in his front room when Annette came through the door without knocking, with Chet Rawlins right behind her.

She saw me and stopped dead.

"What is he doing here?"

"I asked him to come," Lionel said mildly, from behind his desk. "Sit down, Annette. You too, Mr. Rawlins, if you're staying."

"Chet's my CFO," Annette said. "He stays."

"Then he sits."

They sat on the leather sofa. Annette held her phone in her lap like a hot potato.

"Lionel," she said. "What is this email? Voting trust? What voting trust? Daddy never said a word to me about any voting trust."

"No," Lionel said. "He didn't. He asked me not to, either."

He took a document out of a folder and laid it on the corner of his desk where she could reach it. She didn't reach for it.

"In March of 2022," Lionel said, "after his first heart scare, your father placed his sixty-two percent of Castille Marine Supply into a voting trust. That's a legal arrangement. It's perfectly common in family companies. It separates two things that usually go together. The money, and the vote."

"I know what a voting trust is," Annette said.

"Good. Then you'll understand it quickly. Upon your father's death, you inherit the economic interest in his shares. All the dividends, every dollar the company pays out, goes to you. That part of your inheritance is untouched." He folded his hands. "But the right to vote those shares, for the board, for officers, for a sale of the company, belongs to the voting trustee for five years after his death."

"And who's the trustee?"

Lionel looked at me. Annette turned her head and looked at me too, slowly, the way you look at a dog you just realized is not your dog.

"No," she said.

"Cliff Abbott," Lionel said. "Sole voting trustee. Your father signed it in this room, in front of two witnesses and a notary. Cliff signed it as trustee the same afternoon."

"You knew," she said to me. "You knew about this? For three years? And you never told me?"

"Your father asked me not to."

"He was my father!"

"He was my friend," I said. "And he asked me a favor."

Chet leaned forward. "Mr. Ardoin, with respect, the board voted Annette president yesterday. That vote stands. The board appoints the officers, not the shareholders."

"That's correct, Mr. Rawlins," Lionel said pleasantly. "And the shareholders elect the board. And sixty-two percent of the shareholder vote belongs to Mr. Abbott. So the board you're on is the board Mr. Abbott allows to stay on." He slid another page across the desk. "That's the notice of a special shareholders' meeting, which the trustee has asked me to call. Eleven days from today. Ten in the morning. In the company boardroom. All shareholders and directors are invited."

Annette picked up the notice now. Her hand was shaking.

"Daddy wasn't in his right mind in 2022," she said. "He'd just had a heart attack. He was on all kinds of medication. Cliff got to him. Cliff got into his head and talked him into this."

"Your father anticipated you'd say that," Lionel said. "Almost word for word, actually."

"What does that mean?"

"It means you should talk to a lawyer of your own, Annette. Today." He stood up. "And it means you should be very careful about anything you sign on behalf of that company in the next eleven days. As president. Because the president answers to the board, and the board answers to the trustee."

Chet put his hand on Annette's arm. He leaned over and whispered something in her ear. He thought he was whispering quietly. But Lionel's front room has a twelve-foot ceiling and hardwood floors, and I've spent twenty-six years listening for things over the noise of a forklift.

"The LOI," Chet whispered. "We need it signed before the meeting."

Annette nodded once, very small.

Then she stood up, put her phone in her purse and walked out of Lionel's office without saying goodbye.

Earline Batson came to my house that night at a little after nine. I opened the door in my sock feet and found her on the porch holding a banker's box against her chest, like she'd carried it from the car with her whole heart.

Earline was Ray's secretary for thirty-one years. She's seventy, with hair the color of a nickel and a voice that could stop a forklift at fifty yards. She'd been crying at the funeral harder than Annette had.

"Mr. Ray told me if anything happened to him," she said, "I was to give you this box and nobody else. Not Annette. Not Lionel. You." She pushed it into my arms. "I'd have come sooner, but she's had me cleaning out his office since Monday. She wanted it empty by Friday. She's putting Chet in there."

"In Ray's office?"

"In Mr. Ray's office." Earline's lip curled. "With his framed picture of the Crimson Tide on the wall. Chet went to Auburn."

I set the box on the kitchen table and opened it. Inside were Ray's desk calendars, five years of them, the old paper kind he insisted on. A brown accordion file. And a white envelope with my name on it in Ray's square printing.

I opened the envelope first. One sheet of paper.

"Cliff. If you're reading this, I'm gone and she's already made her move. Earline will give you the rest. Read the calendars for Mardi Gras 2022 and every Pensacola trip after. Then look in the accordion file. I'm sorry, son. I should have told you myself. I was a coward about my girl. Don't let her sell it. Ray."

I sat down. Earline sat down across from me without being asked and folded her hands on my kitchen table.

I opened the calendar for 2022 to February. Mardi Gras week. In Ray's square printing on the Thursday, it said: "A. & C.R. — Battle House — saw them. God help me."

The Battle House is the fanciest hotel in downtown Mobile.

I turned the pages. March: "Pensacola, A. says girls' trip. C.R. took vacation same days." April: "Pensacola." June: "Pensacola again?" August. October. Every month or two, all the way through that year and the next and the next, in my father-in-law's handwriting. Pensacola. C.R. Pensacola.

"He knew," I said.

"He knew since Mardi Gras three years ago," Earline said. "He walked into the Battle House for a Rotary lunch and saw them coming out of the elevator holding hands. He came back to the office white as a sheet and sat with the door closed all afternoon. The next week, he went to see Lionel about the voting trust."

"Why didn't he tell me?"

"Because she's his only child," Earline said. "And because he thought if he told you, you'd leave, and then there'd be nobody standing between her and that man and the company." She tapped the accordion file. "Look at that. That's why he was really scared."

I opened the accordion file. It was mostly printouts. Emails, from Chet's company account, to a man at a private equity firm in Atlanta called Bayline Capital. Talking about an acquisition. Valuations. A target price of thirty-eight million dollars for Castille Marine Supply. And the last page, stapled, was a two-page document on Bayline letterhead.

Consulting Agreement. Between Bayline Capital Partners and C. Rawlins Advisory LLC. Upon closing of the acquisition of Castille Marine Supply, Bayline will pay C. Rawlins Advisory a consulting fee of one point two million dollars for "transaction facilitation services."

"Where did Ray get this?" I asked.

"Off the office printer," Earline said. "Chet printed it to the wrong machine one Saturday last fall. The one in my office. He didn't know Mr. Ray came in on Saturdays to read the mail. Mr. Ray found it in my tray Monday morning." She sniffed. "He made a copy and put the original right back. Chet came in at nine and grabbed it like it was on fire."

One point two million dollars, under the table, to the company's own CFO, for selling the company out from under the family. And he was sleeping with the family.

"Earline," I said, "who else knows about this?"

"Me. You. And Mr. Ray's in heaven, so I guess God." She stood up and buttoned her coat. "Mr. Cliff, he loved her. You know that. He spoiled her rotten and he knew it, and he loved her anyway. But he loved that company too. Forty-four years he built it out of one truck and a rented shed." She looked at me. "Don't you let her sell it."

"I don't intend to."

"Good." She picked up her purse. "Because you should know. She's changing your badge code tomorrow morning. I heard her tell Mrs. Trahan. Six a.m."

At six-fifteen Thursday morning I swiped my badge at the yard gate, the same gate I'd opened every working morning for twenty-six years, and the little light on the reader went red.

I swiped it again. Red.

Leon Mims, the gate guard, came out of his shack in his reflective vest. He's sixty-four, a Vietnam vet's son, and has worked that gate eighteen years.

"Morning, Mr. Cliff," he said, looking miserable. "They told me last night. I'm supposed to not let you in."

"Who told you?"

"Mrs. Abbott. Well. Mrs. President Abbott." He shook his head. "I'm sorry. I got a paper says if I let you through I'm fired."

"Then don't let me through, Leon. I'll wait here."

"For what?"

"For the shift to come in."

At six-thirty the day shift started arriving. Forty-some men and women in steel toes and hard hats, coming in from Theodore and Tillman's Corner and Grand Bay in pickup trucks with dirty tailgates. Every one of them had to drive past me standing at my own gate in my work shirt with my lunch cooler. Every one of them slowed down.

By six-forty-five, half of them were standing around me in the gravel, and the other half had parked and walked back out.

"Mr. Cliff, what's going on?"

"Is it true you quit?"

"Wyatt says they fired you."

At seven, a white Tahoe pulled up inside the gate. Annette got out of the passenger side. Chet Rawlins got out of the driver's side. They walked to the gate together and stood on the inside of it, with the chain-link between us.

"You need to leave, Cliff," Annette said. "You're trespassing."

"I'm standing on a public road, Annette."

"You're making a scene."

"I'm waiting for my men to go to work. They don't seem to want to."

Chet stepped up to the fence. He had his quarter-zip on. It was eighty-one degrees.

"Folks," he said loudly, to the crowd, "Mr. Abbott has resigned. As of today, I'm acting chief operating officer. Nothing changes for any of you. The company is in great hands. So let's all go to work, okay?"

Nobody moved.

Then Wyatt pushed through the crowd from the back. He'd been inside since five-thirty, loading a truck for a run to Bayou La Batre. He walked right up to the fence next to me and looked at his mother through the chain-link.

"Dad didn't resign," he said. "I was standing in the hall. You put a letter on his desk and he didn't sign it."

"Wyatt, go back to work," Annette said.

"Not till you tell them the truth."

Chet sighed. "Look, son—"

"I'm not your son," Wyatt said. "And you've been in this company four years. My dad's been here twenty-six. He knows every man here by name. You don't even know where the forklift keys are."

Somebody in the crowd laughed. A big laugh, the kind that spreads.

Chet's ears went red. He turned to me. "Mr. Abbott, I'd advise you to think carefully about inciting the workforce. There could be legal consequences."

"Chet," I said, "you want to talk about legal consequences in front of all these people? Right here, right now?"

He didn't say anything.

"Because I'd be glad to," I said. "We could talk about Pensacola. We could talk about Bayline Capital in Atlanta and a thirty-eight-million-dollar letter of intent. We could talk about a little company called C. Rawlins Advisory and the one point two million dollars it gets paid the day this company gets sold. We can talk about all of that, out loud, to forty people who'd like to know if they still have jobs next year."

Chet went still. Annette turned and looked at him.

"What's he talking about?" she said quietly. "What one point two million?"

"He's bluffing," Chet said. "Annette, he's making things up. Let's go inside."

"Chet."

"Inside," he said, and took her by the elbow, harder than he needed to, and walked her back to the Tahoe.

I stood at the gate and watched them go.

Then Leon cleared his throat next to me.

"Mr. Cliff," he said, "I'm going to go get a cup of coffee. I'll be gone about ten minutes. Gate sticks sometimes. Might be open when I get back."

He went into the shack and came back out with a coffee cup, and walked off toward the vending machines. The gate was open.

I didn't go in.

"Go to work, y'all," I said to the crowd. "I'll be back Tuesday after next. Ten o'clock. The boardroom."

Annette moved out of our house on Saturday. I came back from the hardware store and her side of the closet was empty and there was a note on the kitchen counter in her round handwriting.

"I'm staying at Daddy's. Don't call. Talk to my lawyer."

Daddy's was Ray's big brick house in Spring Hill, with the magnolia in the front yard and the room in the back where he kept his fishing rods. Annette was an only child, and Ray had left it to her outright, along with his money market accounts and his bass boat. The house wasn't part of any trust. It was hers, free and clear, and she was welcome to it.

On Monday, her lawyer went to court.

He was a man named Dwight Collier from a big firm in Birmingham, and he filed a lawsuit in Mobile County Circuit Court on behalf of Annette Castille Abbott, individually and as president of Castille Marine Supply, against Clifford Abbott, individually and as voting trustee. He asked the court to throw out the voting trust. He said Ray Castille had been "a grieving, medicated and cognitively impaired elderly man" when he signed it, and that I'd used my "position of trust and daily proximity" to manipulate him into handing me control of a thirty-eight-million-dollar company.

And he asked for an emergency order freezing me as trustee, so I couldn't vote the shares at the special meeting until the court decided.

A judge signed it that afternoon. Temporary, ten days, until a hearing.

Lionel called me at four-thirty.

"She got the TRO," he said. "You can't vote those shares until the hearing. Which is two days after the meeting."

"So the meeting's dead."

"The meeting goes forward, but you can't vote at it. And while you're frozen, she's the president of a company with nobody watching her." He was quiet for a second. "Cliff, if Earline's right about Atlanta, this is exactly the window Chet wanted. She can sign that letter of intent as president and nobody can stop her."

"How binding is a letter of intent?"

"Depends on the letter. If Bayline's lawyers wrote it, there'll be a no-shop clause and a breakup fee. If she signs it and then the company backs out, the company owes Bayline money. A lot of money. Maybe a million." He sighed. "It's a trap, Cliff. It forces the sale."

That was the first time since the funeral I felt the floor move under me. I'd been calm because I had the paper. Now somebody had found a way around the paper, and I was a man standing outside a gate again.

I went to bed that night and didn't sleep. At two in the morning I got up and went out to the garage and sat on a bucket and looked at Ray's desk calendars, which I'd stacked on my workbench.

I opened the one from 2022 again. I'd been reading it for the Pensacola trips. I hadn't been reading it for anything else.

March 9, 2022, a week after the Battle House. In Ray's square printing: "Dr. Fortier, 2 pm. Memory testing. Lionel says do it BEFORE signing. Annette will say I'm senile. Beat her to it."

I sat on that bucket and laughed out loud in my dark garage at two in the morning, like a crazy man.

Ray had known his daughter so well he'd gone and gotten his own brain tested a week before signing, just so she couldn't say what she'd just said in a lawsuit.

Dr. Paul Fortier had a neurology practice off Airport Boulevard. I called his office at eight a.m. sharp. It took Lionel two days and a signed release from Ray's estate, which Lionel, as executor, could give, but on Thursday afternoon, a two-page letter came over Lionel's fax machine.

"I evaluated Raymond Castille on March 9, 2022, at his own request, for the stated purpose of documenting his mental capacity prior to executing certain business documents. Mr. Castille scored 29 out of 30 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment. He was alert, oriented, and fully capable of understanding the nature and consequences of his decisions. He told me, and I quote from my notes, 'My daughter is going to say I was out of my mind. I want a doctor to say I wasn't.'"

Lionel read it twice. Then he took his glasses off and rubbed his eyes.

"Twenty-nine out of thirty," he said. "Cliff, I'm sixty-eight and I'd be lucky to get a twenty-six."

"Is it enough?"

"For the judge? It's more than enough. I'll file an emergency motion to dissolve the TRO in the morning, with this attached. Judge Rainey's a fair man. He hates being lied to in an emergency filing more than anything in the world." Lionel put the letter in a folder. "But Cliff, the hearing's still Monday at the earliest. The meeting is Tuesday."

"And the letter of intent?"

That's when my phone buzzed. A text from a number I'd only had since the night of the banker's box.

"Mr. Cliff. It's Earline. Chet's flying to Atlanta Saturday morning on the first Delta. Annette's going with him. I heard him on the phone booking it in Mr. Ray's office. He told the man at Bayline she'd sign it in person, Saturday at noon, at their office on Peachtree."

Saturday at noon. Three days before the meeting. While I was frozen.

I showed it to Lionel.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he picked up his phone.

"Who are you calling?" I said.

"The judge's clerk," Lionel said. "Tell her it's an emergency. Then I'm going to call a man I went to law school with, who happens to be general counsel at Bayline Capital in Atlanta." He started dialing. "Private equity firms are a lot of things, Cliff. But they hate one thing more than losing a deal."

"What's that?"

"Getting sued over one."

Chet Rawlins came to my house that night.

He parked his Audi at the curb a little after nine and walked up my driveway in the porch light holding a bottle of bourbon by the neck, like we were old friends and this was a social call. I met him at the bottom of the porch steps. I didn't invite him up.

"Cliff," he said. "Can we talk like businessmen?"

"You're on my lawn, Chet. Talk."

He held out the bourbon. I didn't take it. He set it on the porch rail.

"Look," he said. "I know you've got your feelings about me and Annette. I get it. I'd have them too. But this isn't about feelings. This is about thirty-eight million dollars, and a company that's going to get eaten alive in five years by the big distributors out of Houston if somebody doesn't sell it while it's still worth something."

"Ray didn't think so."

"Ray was seventy-nine and still did his calendar in pencil." Chet smiled like that was a joke we both got. "I'm offering you a way out where everybody wins. You stop fighting the sale. The TRO expires, you vote yes, or you just don't show up. Bayline closes. Annette gets her money. And you." He reached into his jacket and took out a folded piece of paper. "There's a consulting arrangement for you, too. Five hundred thousand. Paid at closing. Nobody ever has to know."

"Half of yours," I said.

His smile slipped half an inch.

"I don't know what you think you know," he said.

"I think you printed something to the wrong printer last fall."

He stood there in my porch light for a second. I watched him run it back, every Saturday, every printer, every tray in that office. Then he put the paper back in his jacket.

"Here's the other way it goes," he said, and his voice went flat. "You keep pushing. I tell Judge Rainey's courtroom that you manipulated a sick old man for three years. I tell every customer on the Gulf Coast that the company's in chaos and the new president's been locked out by her own husband. Your yard guys start hearing rumors about layoffs. Your son starts hearing rumors about his daddy. By the time you get to vote those shares, Cliff, there won't be a company left worth voting."

"You'd do that to forty families?"

"I'd do it to four hundred," Chet said. "It's just business."

"Then you'd better get off my lawn before I start talking business too."

He picked up the bourbon off my porch rail. At the bottom of the driveway he turned around.

"You're a yard man, Cliff," he said. "You load trucks. You're out of your depth."

"Maybe," I said. "But I always know what's on the truck."

He drove off. I went inside and wrote down every word he'd said on a legal pad, with the date and the time, and put it in Ray's banker's box with the calendars.

Judge Rainey dissolved the TRO at four-forty on Friday afternoon, from the bench, after reading Dr. Fortier's letter and Ray's calendar entry from March 9. He said something from the bench that Lionel wrote down and read to me over the phone afterward, word for word.

"The court notes that the plaintiff has represented to this court, in an emergency filing, that her father was cognitively impaired. The court further notes that her father apparently disagreed, in writing, with a neurologist. The TRO is dissolved. Counsel for the plaintiff should consider very carefully what he files next."

Annette didn't fly to Atlanta. Chet did.

The special shareholders' meeting of Castille Marine Supply was held on Tuesday, eleven days after the morning my wife put a resignation letter on my desk, at ten o'clock, in the boardroom on the second floor of the office over the yard. Ray had built that boardroom himself in 1991, out of cypress he bought from a sawmill in Lucedale. The table is twenty-two feet long. His chair is at the head of it, a big leather thing with the stuffing coming out of one arm, and nobody had sat in it since the funeral.

I didn't sit in it either. I sat where I always sat, halfway down the left side.

Annette sat at the far end, near the door, next to Dwight Collier from Birmingham, who looked like a man who'd been told by his managing partner not to say anything he couldn't take back. Chet Rawlins sat beside her, in a suit this time, with a leather folder. He'd come back from Atlanta Sunday night. He looked like he hadn't slept since.

The other shareholders were there too. Old Mr. Gaudet, eighty-four, who'd owned eight percent since 1986 and sat on the board mostly to get the free lunch. Ray's cousin Delphine, who owned five percent and lived in Daphne and came to the meeting in a church hat. The rest was owned by a handful of long-time employees, and three of them had driven in from the yard in their work shirts. Wyatt sat by the window. Earline sat by the coffee service, with a steno pad, the way she had at every meeting for thirty-one years.

And at the end of the table, at the corner nearest Ray's empty chair, sat a woman in a navy suit I'd never seen before. She had a Bayline Capital lanyard around her neck and a laptop open in front of her.

Before Lionel called the meeting to order, Annette leaned down the table toward me and spoke low, so Dwight Collier couldn't hear.

"Chet says you went to Atlanta's lawyers behind my back," she said. "Is that true?"

"Lionel called a man he went to school with."

"You're trying to embarrass me in front of Daddy's people."

"Annette, you put a resignation letter on my desk in front of our son. I'm just showing up to a meeting."

"You could have told me about the trust. Three years, Cliff. Three years of Sunday dinners at Daddy's, and you never said one word."

"Your daddy asked me not to. And you never asked me where you went in Pensacola, either."

She sat back in her chair like I'd pushed her. Across from her, Chet was studying the grain of the cypress like it was going to be on a test.

Lionel Ardoin stood at the head of the table, next to the empty chair.

"This special meeting of the shareholders of Castille Marine Supply is called to order," he said. "Before we take up any business, I am required by the terms of the will of Raymond Castille to read one document aloud." He held up a long white envelope with a red wax seal, and on the front, in big square printing, it said To Be Read Aloud At The First Shareholders' Meeting.

Annette went very still.

"Actually," Lionel said, "Ray's will says it is to be read aloud by his daughter."

He walked down the twenty-two feet of cypress and laid it in front of Annette.

She looked at it a long time. Then she broke the seal with her thumbnail and unfolded two sheets of yellow legal paper, and she started to read her father's handwriting out loud.

"Annie. If you're reading this to the company, I'm gone, and you've already tried to sell it. Don't be mad at Lionel. I made him promise."

Her voice broke on the second line. She stopped. She started again.

"I know about Chet. I've known since Mardi Gras of '22. I saw you at the Battle House and I went home and sat in my truck in the driveway for an hour. I'm not writing to shame you. You're a grown woman and you'll answer to God and Cliff, not to me. I'm writing because I also know about Atlanta. I know about Bayline. And I know about a company called C. Rawlins Advisory, and a consulting fee of one point two million dollars that gets paid the day my company gets sold. Ask him about it, Annie. Ask him right now, in front of everybody. Ask him if he ever told you."

Annette stopped reading. She turned her head and looked at Chet.

Chet had his hands flat on his leather folder. His face had gone the color of skim milk.

"That's a private consulting arrangement," he said. "It's completely standard in M and A transactions. It has nothing to do with—"

"Did you ever tell me?" Annette said.

"Annette, now isn't—"

"Did you ever tell me, Chet?"

He didn't answer. That was the answer.

The woman in the navy suit at the end of the table cleared her throat.

"For the record," she said, "my name is Meredith Shaw, deputy general counsel for Bayline Capital. I'm here at the request of Mr. Ardoin and with the approval of our managing partner." She folded her hands. "Bayline was not aware that Mr. Rawlins was an officer of the target company at the time he negotiated that consulting agreement. He represented himself to us as an independent adviser. He also represented to us, on Saturday in our offices, that he had authority to bind the company and that the company's majority shareholder had approved the sale." She looked at me. "We now understand that was not true. Bayline is withdrawing its letter of intent effective immediately, and we're reviewing what other representations Mr. Rawlins made to our firm."

Chet stood up. "Meredith. Please. We can still—"

"Sit down, Mr. Rawlins," she said. "You're done talking to my firm."

Lionel turned to me. "Mr. Abbott. As voting trustee for the shares of Raymond Castille, sixty-two percent, how do you vote on the first item of business, the removal of the current board of directors?"

"For," I said.

"Mr. Gaudet?"

The old man leaned forward and put his hand to his ear. "What are we voting on?"

"Throwing them out, Mr. Gaudet," Earline said loudly from the coffee service.

"Oh. For."

"Ms. Delphine?"

Ray's cousin adjusted her church hat. "For, honey. Lord, for."

It went down the table. Every hand that had a vote went up. Lionel wrote it down. Then we elected a new board of three: me, Mr. Gaudet and Earline Batson, who said "Well, I'll be" and turned pink to the roots of her hair.

The new board met for four minutes, right there at the same table, with the same coffee. We removed Chet Rawlins as chief financial officer, for cause, effective immediately, and asked Lionel to refer the Bayline matter to the company's outside auditors. We removed Annette Abbott as president. Mr. Gaudet moved to name me president until the board could find somebody better. Earline seconded it before he'd finished his sentence.

Chet picked up his leather folder and walked out of the boardroom without looking at Annette. At the door he stopped and said, to nobody in particular, "This company would've been worth forty million to somebody who knew what to do with it."

"It was worth a lot more than that to the man who built it," Wyatt said from the window. "Leon's waiting at the gate for your badge."

The door closed.

Annette sat at the end of the long cypress table with her father's letter in her lap. She hadn't finished reading it. There was a second page. She looked at it now, and then she read the last part to herself, and then she read it out loud, very softly, because I think she had to.

"Annie, the dividends are yours. Every penny. You'll never want for anything. But the company goes to the man who stayed. If you want it back, you can have it the way I got it. Start in the yard. Love, Daddy."

She folded the letter. Her hands were shaking so bad it took her three tries.

"Cliff," she said. Her voice was small. "Cliff, I didn't know about the money. I swear to God I didn't know about the one point two million. He told me Daddy wanted to sell. He told me you'd be taken care of. He said—" She stopped. "I put that letter on your desk. In front of everybody. In front of our son."

"I know."

"Can we just— Can we go home and talk? Please. Can we try?"

I reached into the inside pocket of my sport coat. I'd brought one thing with me that morning. A single sheet of company letterhead, folded in thirds, that had been sitting on my desk blotter for eleven days.

The resignation letter. My name typed at the bottom, the signature line still blank.

I took a pen out of my pocket, crossed out my name, and in its place, very neatly, the way I fill out a bill of lading, I wrote hers.

Then I unfolded it and set it down in front of her on Ray's cypress table, with the pen on top.

"Now that I'm in charge," I said, "you're out. Sign it at the bottom, Annette. Then go see Wyatt at the gate. Your daddy said start in the yard, and the yard starts at six."

She stared at the letter. She stared at our son by the window. Then she picked up the pen and signed her own name on the line she'd typed for me.

"Perfect," I said.
