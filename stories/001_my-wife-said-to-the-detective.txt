"He followed me to the hotel. I was terrified!"

My wife said it to a police detective in the lobby of the Plainsman Hotel in downtown Amarillo, on a Friday night, with about forty wedding guests spilling out of the ballroom behind her in rented tuxedos and bridesmaid dresses the color of Pepto-Bismol. Somebody's phone was already up and recording. A flower girl was eating a mint off the front desk and staring at me.

Next to Rhonda stood Kyle Dorsey, forty-one years old, with his sunglasses pushed up on his head at nine o'clock at night, and he was nodding so hard I thought his neck might come loose.

"He did, he did, I saw the whole thing," Kyle said. "He came in right behind her. Right behind her, Detective."

Twenty-nine years I'd been married to that woman. Twenty-nine years, and the most she'd ever been scared of me was the time I backed the boat trailer into her mailbox.

I didn't say a word. I took my phone out of my jacket, pressed one name on the screen, set the phone face up on the marble front desk with the speaker on, and folded my hands in front of me.

The detective looked at the phone. Then she looked at me.

"Mr. Pace? Do you want to tell me your side?"

I shook my head, once.

Fourteen minutes later, that same detective was sitting in a little room downtown, and she couldn't stop staring at a laptop screen with her hand over her mouth.

What Rhonda didn't know was what was on the thumb drive in the outside pocket of my lawyer's briefcase.

My name is Gordon Pace. I'm fifty-six. I've driven a truck out of Amarillo, Texas for thirty-one years, with a million miles on my record and not one claim. The first thing they teach you in this job, before you ever turn a key, is that you never back up without getting out and looking. You get out, you walk all the way around, and you see what's really behind you. Hold on to that.

The phone on the desk picked up on the second ring.

"Gordon," my lawyer said, loud enough for the flower girl to hear. "Are you saying anything?"

"No, ma'am."

"Keep it that way. Detective, this is Marisa Calderon. I represent Mr. Pace. He'll come with you voluntarily, and I'll meet you at the station in twenty minutes. He won't be answering questions without me."

The detective had the kind of face that had heard every lie in the Texas Panhandle and graded them on a curve. She was in her forties, short dark hair, a gold shield on her belt, and a notebook she wrote in without looking down.

"Detective Lorena Salazar," she said to the phone. "Your client was observed entering the hotel directly behind the complainant, Ms. Calderon. We have a complainant, a witness, and..." She flipped a page. "A third party. An independent witness."

Kyle stepped aside, and a man I'd never seen before stepped forward. He was soft around the middle, in a polo shirt with a little bank logo stitched on it, and he kept wiping his palms on his khakis.

"Dale Akins," the detective read off her notebook. "Says he was having a drink at the bar. Doesn't know either party. Disinterested witness."

"I don't know him," Dale Akins said quickly. "I mean, I don't know anybody here. I just saw what I saw."

Kyle leaned close to me while the detective was talking to the phone. His breath smelled like the hotel bar's well bourbon.

"Told you, trucker," he whispered. "You're done."

I looked at Dale Akins. I looked at the little bank logo on his chest. And for the first time that night, I had to work very hard not to smile.

To understand why I walked into that hotel at all, you have to go back three days, to a Tuesday afternoon, and a phone call from a man I'd met exactly once.

"Mr. Pace? Royce Tatum. I own Iron Prairie Fitness out on Soncy Road. I believe your wife's a member."

"She's a member," I said. I was in my truck at the Love's in Vega, eating sunflower seeds. "She's been a member for about two years. She practically lives there."

"Yeah." There was a pause, the kind of pause a man takes when he's about to say something he can't take back. "Mr. Pace, I'm a Marine. I don't do this kind of thing over the phone. Can you come by my office? Today?"

I was at the gym by four. Royce Tatum was sixty-eight, built like a mailbox, with a gray flattop and forearms full of faded ink. His office was in the back, behind the smoothie bar, a little room with a metal desk, a picture of a bass boat, and a sign taped to the door at eye level that said, in red letters, THIS OFFICE IS MONITORED BY VIDEO AND AUDIO RECORDING.

"I put that camera in eight months ago," he said, tapping the sign. "Register's been coming up short. Five hundred here, five hundred there. Never a big number, always a round one. I figured it was one of the college kids on the night shift."

"Was it?"

"Sit down, Mr. Pace."

I sat. He turned his laptop toward me. The time stamp in the corner said Monday, a little after ten at night. The gym was closed. In the frame, sitting on Royce's own desk with her legs crossed, was my wife. Next to her, leaning against the filing cabinet, was Kyle Dorsey, Royce's manager.

"Kyle runs the place at night," Royce said quietly. "I've been grooming him to buy me out. I'm too old. I want to fish."

On the screen, Rhonda was laughing.

"He'll come," she said. "If I text him and say I want to talk like adults, he'll come. He always comes. He's a golden retriever."

"Plainsman, Friday at eight," Kyle said. "You get there at eight. He gets there at eight-oh-five. You walk in through the lobby, you go to the bar, you wait till he walks in, and you get up and run to the front desk and call 911."

"And then what?"

"Then I'm there, and I saw him following you. And Dale's there, and he saw it too. Dale from the bank. Dale's in. Dale owes me."

"And the cops believe us?"

"Two witnesses and a crying wife? Babe, it's not even close." Kyle grinned at the camera without knowing it was there. "Monday morning, you go to the courthouse and get an emergency protective order. Judge kicks him out of the house. He's living in his truck, which, let's be honest, is where he lives anyway."

"And the house?"

"Leave the bank to me."

Royce hit pause. He didn't look at me for a while. He looked at the picture of the bass boat.

"That's the first twenty minutes," he said. "The recording runs four hours. I couldn't stomach the rest."

I sat there. I was aware of my own hands on my knees, and of the smoothie blender going behind the wall, and of a woman on a treadmill somewhere singing along badly to ZZ Top.

"A golden retriever," I said.

"I'm sorry, son."

"Don't be. I've been called worse by dispatchers." I stood up. "Mr. Tatum, can I have a copy of that?"

"Already made it." He held up a little blue thumb drive. "Whole night. Four hours. I'd have gone to the police myself, but I'm a gym owner with a recording of two adults talking trash in my office. I figured the man they're talking about ought to decide what to do with it."

"Why are you doing this?"

Royce put the drive in my palm and closed my fingers over it with a hand like a catcher's mitt.

"Because I was about to sell my life's work to that boy," he said. "And a man who'd do that to a husband would do it to an old man in a heartbeat."

I called Marisa Calderon from the parking lot. Marisa did my late father's estate, she smokes exactly one cigarette a day at noon, and she talks like she's billing by the word, which she is.

"Say that again," she said, after I told her. "Slowly."

I said it again, slowly. She was quiet for a long time.

"Gordon, I want you to think about this like a trucker."

"I don't know how to think any other way."

"Right now, that drive is two people talking ugly in a gym office. That's not a crime. People talk ugly in gym offices every day. If they never do it, nobody gets charged, and you've got a nasty video and a divorce. But if they actually do it..." She stopped.

"If they lie to a cop," I said.

"If they make a false report to a police officer, on the record, in front of witnesses, then it's not talk anymore. In Texas that's a crime. And if they bring a paid witness, that's another one."

"So you want me to walk into it."

"I want you to walk into it with your mouth shut," she said. "That's the only thing I'll ask. You don't argue, you don't explain, you don't say one single word to anybody. You let them tell their story all the way to the end. Can you do that?"

"Say nothing."

"Say nothing. I'll do the talking. I'm better at it, and I charge for it."

I was still sitting in my truck in the gym parking lot when my phone buzzed. It was a text from Rhonda, the first one in three weeks that wasn't about the electric bill.

"Can we meet Friday? Plainsman, 8pm. Just us. Let's be adults about this."

I looked at it for a long time.

Then I typed back, "See you there."

So that's how I came to be standing in the Plainsman lobby at five after eight, three days later, with my hands folded and a flower girl staring at me.

Rhonda had played it beautifully, I'll give her that. She'd gotten there at eight, sat at the bar with a white wine, and the second I walked through the lobby doors she'd jumped off her stool, run past me to the front desk, and grabbed the clerk's phone with both hands. She cried real tears. I don't know where she found them. I hadn't seen her cry since her mother's funeral, and she'd been dry-eyed at that.

The station was on Southeast Seventh. Detective Salazar put me in a little room with a table, three chairs and a box of tissues, and Marisa came through the door eighteen minutes after me in a gray suit with her briefcase under her arm like a football.

"Did he say anything?" was the first thing she asked.

"Not one word," Salazar said. "Which, I'll be honest, is a first for a man in his situation. Usually they can't shut up."

"He's a trucker. He's used to talking to nobody for nine hours." Marisa sat down. "Detective, before you take a statement from my client, I'd like you to look at something."

"Counselor, I've got three sworn statements on my desk. The complainant, Rhonda Pace. Her friend, Kyle Dorsey. And a disinterested witness."

"Dale Akins."

Salazar's eyes narrowed just a little.

"You know Mr. Akins?"

"I've never met him," Marisa said, and opened her briefcase. "But I've watched him. Do you have a laptop, Detective? Mine's old, and I'd hate for it to die halfway through the good part."

Salazar looked at her for a long moment. Then she stepped out and came back with a department laptop and set it on the table. Marisa plugged in the little blue thumb drive.

"This came from the owner of Iron Prairie Fitness, a Mr. Royce Tatum," Marisa said. "Office camera, video and audio. There's a recording notice on the door at eye level, and I have a photo of it. Mr. Tatum will give you a statement tomorrow, and he's a retired Marine gunnery sergeant, so he'll probably give it to you standing at attention. The date is this Monday."

She pressed play.

I'd seen it already. I watched Salazar instead.

She watched Rhonda laugh and call me a golden retriever. She watched Kyle lay out the whole night like a football play: the text, the bar, eight o'clock, eight-oh-five, the 911 call. Her face didn't move. Her pen did. She was writing without looking down.

"And then I'm there, and I saw him following you," Kyle said on the screen. "And Dale's there, and he saw it too. Dale from the bank. Dale's in. Dale owes me."

Salazar stopped writing.

"Pause it," she said.

Marisa paused it.

"Play that again."

Marisa played it again. Dale from the bank. Dale's in.

Salazar sat back in her chair. She looked at me for the first time since we'd sat down, really looked, the way you look at a man you'd already decided about and now have to decide about all over again.

"Mr. Pace," she said. "Did you follow your wife to the Plainsman Hotel tonight?"

Marisa nodded at me. I could talk now.

"No, ma'am. She invited me." I slid my phone across the table with the text open. "Friday, eight p.m. Just us. Let's be adults about this."

Salazar read it. She read it twice. Then she took a picture of it with her own phone.

"There's more," Marisa said. "Detective, the full recording is about four hours long. Mr. Tatum couldn't bring himself to watch past the first twenty minutes. I could." She leaned forward and dragged the little bar on the screen. "Two hours and fifty-one minutes in. Rhonda Pace has already gone home. Kyle Dorsey is still in the office. Somebody knocks."

She pressed play.

On the screen, the office door opened, and in came a soft man in a polo shirt with a little bank logo on his chest. He shut the door behind him and wiped his palms on his khakis.

"You got it?" Dale Akins said.

"Relax, Dale." Kyle walked to the corner of the frame, where there was a gray metal cash box on a shelf. He opened it with a key from his own keyring. He counted out bills, one at a time, licking his thumb like a bank teller. "Four hundred. Four-fifty. Five hundred. There. Same as always."

"I'm not doing anything illegal, right?"

"You're having a drink at a bar, Dale. That's legal in Texas." Kyle stuffed the bills into Dale's shirt pocket and patted it. "You were in the lobby. You saw him come in right behind her. You don't know her, you don't know me, you're just a concerned citizen. You got that?"

"I saw him come in behind her."

"Right behind her."

"Right behind her," Dale said. "And the other thing? The bank thing?"

"The bank thing is next week. One problem at a time."

Marisa stopped the video. Nobody in the little room said anything for a while. Somewhere down the hall, a phone was ringing and nobody was picking it up.

"Round number," I said. "Royce told me the register's been short five hundred at a time for eight months."

Salazar didn't answer me. She was holding her own notebook, open to a page, and I could see Dale Akins's name on it in her neat handwriting, with the words "disinterested witness" underlined twice.

"I took that statement myself," she said, very quietly. "Forty minutes ago. He looked me in the eye."

"He's a loan processor," Marisa said. "Looking people in the eye is his whole job."

Salazar closed the notebook. She stood up, and she was suddenly a different woman, all edges.

"Ms. Calderon, I need a copy of this drive tonight. Mr. Pace, please don't go anywhere for about ten minutes."

"Where would I go?"

"Mr. Pace," she said from the door, "I've been a detective for twelve years, and in twelve years nobody has ever paid a witness in front of a camera with a sign on the door. Please let me enjoy this."

She left. Marisa went to the little window in the door and looked out, and after a minute she crooked her finger at me.

From the window, you could see down the hallway into the front lobby. Rhonda and Kyle were sitting on a bench by the vending machine. Rhonda had her makeup fixed already. Kyle had one arm around her and was scrolling his phone with the other hand. Dale Akins was standing a little apart, by the water fountain, like a man waiting for a bus that wasn't coming.

Salazar walked into the lobby with two uniformed officers.

I couldn't hear what she said. I didn't need to. I watched Kyle stand up, smiling, ready to shake her hand. I watched the smile stop. I watched one of the officers take him by the arm and turn him around and put the cuffs on him right there in front of the vending machine, and I watched Kyle say something with a lot of teeth in it, and I watched the officer not care at all.

Dale Akins started crying before anybody even touched him.

Rhonda stood up. Salazar said something to her, and pointed to the bench, and Rhonda sat back down like her knees had given out. Then my wife turned her head and looked straight down the hallway, through the little window in the door, right at me.

I didn't wave. I didn't do anything. I just looked back.

She was up off that bench before the officer could stop her, and she got about ten feet down the hall before he caught her elbow, and she was screaming over his shoulder at the door.

"Gordon! Gordon, tell them! It was his idea! It was all his idea, Gordon, tell them!"

Marisa let the little curtain fall back over the window.

"Twenty-nine years," she said, "and she still thinks you'll fix it for her."

"I always did."

"Not tonight, you don't." Her phone buzzed. She looked at it, and her face changed, and she held it out so I could read it. It was from her paralegal, who checks the county records every morning for Marisa's estate clients and apparently checks them on Friday nights too, because she has no life either.

"Panhandle Commerce Bank. Home equity line of credit on 4417 Bell Ranch Road. Application complete, both owners' signatures on file. $150,000. Scheduled to fund Monday at 10 a.m."

"Both owners," I said. "I never signed anything."

"I know you didn't," Marisa said. "So who did?"

Salazar didn't charge me with anything that night, and she didn't charge Rhonda either, not yet. She told Rhonda, in front of me, that she was under investigation for making a false report to a peace officer, that she should get a lawyer, and that she should not, under any circumstances, contact Mr. Kyle Dorsey or Mr. Dale Akins. Kyle went to a holding cell. Dale went home after three hours with a ticket to come back Monday and the expression of a man who'd finally learned what the word disinterested means.

I slept at the Big Texan Motel on I-40, under a giant cowboy sign, because three weeks earlier my wife had moved into our guest room and told me the house felt "like a truck stop" when I was in it. I'd been sleeping in my cab in the driveway when I was home. That should have told me something. Get out and look.

Saturday morning she found me.

I don't know how she knew where I was, except that I've stayed at the Big Texan every time we ever had a fight, going back to 1998, and Rhonda keeps track of things like that. She keeps track of everything. She keeps score.

She was standing by my truck when I came out of the room with my coffee, arms crossed, sunglasses on, in the same blouse she'd worn the night before.

"You set me up," she said.

"Morning, Rhonda."

"You set me up, Gordon. You knew. You knew the whole time and you walked in there and let me..."

"Let you what?"

"Let me make a fool of myself."

"You texted me. Friday, eight o'clock, let's be adults. I came."

"You came with a lawyer on speakerphone!"

"I came with my mouth shut. You did all the talking. You and Kyle and Dale from the bank."

She flinched at Dale's name. She took her sunglasses off, and her eyes were red, and for a second she looked like the girl in the yellow dress at Cadillac Ranch who spray-painted our initials on a car half buried in the dirt. Then she put the sunglasses back on.

"Kyle said it would just be a scare," she said. "Just enough to get a protective order. Nobody was going to jail. You weren't going to jail. You'd just... be out. You'd be out of the house. You're never there anyway!"

"So that makes it okay."

"You're never THERE, Gordon! Do you know what that's like? Being married to a voice on the phone? You're in Flagstaff, you're in Denver, you're in a Love's parking lot in Oklahoma!"

"For thirty-one years. That voice on the phone paid for your Camry and your mother's hip and both of Tess's degrees. You've got a list of everything I ever did wrong, Rhonda. You want to see mine? It's a receipt."

"Oh, here we go. Mr. Martyr."

"You're the one who keeps the list, honey. I just pay it."

"Kyle loves me."

"Kyle paid a bank man five hundred dollars out of his boss's cash box to lie to a detective about me. That's what Kyle loves. Kyle loves a plan."

"That was... that's not..." She stopped. "I didn't know about the money."

"Okay."

"I didn't!"

"Then tell me about the other thing," I said. "The bank thing. A hundred and fifty thousand dollars on our house, funding Monday at ten. Both owners' signatures on file. Did you sign it?"

She went still. I've seen deer do that on Highway 287 in the headlights, right before they make the wrong decision.

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Rhonda. Did you sign it?"

"Kyle handled the bank. I just... Kyle handled it."

"That's not what I asked."

"I'm not talking to you without a lawyer," she said, which is the first smart thing she'd said since Tuesday, and she turned around and walked to her Camry and drove off without looking back.

My phone rang before her taillights were out of the lot. It was Tess, my daughter, twenty-seven, an ER nurse in Lubbock, calling between shifts.

"Dad? Are you okay? Mom called me at two in the morning."

"I'm fine, honey. What did she want?"

Tess was quiet for a second, the way she gets right before she says the thing that matters.

"She wanted me to tell a lawyer that you scare her. That you've always scared her. She said if I loved her I'd say it." Her voice cracked. "Dad, you've never scared anybody in your life. You apologize to the dishwasher when you bump it."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her no. I told her I'm a nurse and I don't lie on paper. And she hung up on me." Tess sniffed. "Dad, who's Dale?"

"Long story. I'll tell you over enchiladas."

I'd barely set the phone down on the hood when it rang again. This time it was Royce Tatum, and he didn't bother with hello.

"Mr. Pace. Kyle's out."

"Out? He paid a witness in front of a camera. They arrested him last night."

"His brother put up five thousand dollars bond about an hour ago. He came straight to the gym to clean out his locker. I told him he's fired and to get off my property, and he laughed in my face." Royce took a breath. "Gordon, he wasn't asking about his locker. He was asking everybody in the building one thing. Whether Rhonda had talked to the police yet."

I called Salazar and told her. She said thank you, she said Kyle's bond conditions included no contact with Rhonda Pace, and she said if he broke them she'd be glad to explain the conditions to him personally. Then she said something I didn't expect.

"Mr. Pace, Panhandle Commerce Bank opens at nine on Monday. I'll be there at nine. I'd appreciate it if you and your lawyer were there at nine too."

"You know about the loan?"

"Your lawyer called me at seven this morning. Does she ever sleep?"

"Not that I've seen."

Monday at five to nine, Marisa and I sat in two plastic chairs in the lobby of Panhandle Commerce Bank on Georgia Street, under a poster of a smiling family in front of a house that looked nothing like mine. Marisa had a folder on her knees. I had my hat in my lap. At nine on the dot, a tall woman from the bank's fraud department came out and walked us into a glass conference room. She was the kind of banker who doesn't smile until the audit's done, and the audit was never done.

She laid the loan file on the table and turned it around so I could read it.

"Mr. Pace, this is a home equity line of credit on 4417 Bell Ranch Road. A hundred and fifty thousand dollars. It's scheduled to fund at ten o'clock this morning to a checking account in your wife's name. Is this your signature?"

I looked at it. It was my name, written in blue ink, in a nice, careful, slanted hand, with a little loop on the G.

"No, ma'am."

"You're certain?"

I took a pen out of my shirt pocket, wrote my name on the back of an envelope, and slid it across. My signature looks like a seismograph during an earthquake. I've signed a hundred thousand bills of lading in a truck cab with a clipboard on my knee. Nobody has ever accused it of having a loop.

The fraud officer looked at the envelope. She looked at the loan file. She made a short, dry note.

"That's what I thought," she said. "The signature was notarized here, in this branch, last Thursday. You'll see the notary's stamp."

She tapped the page. Under my fake signature was a purple notary seal, and under the seal was a name.

Dale Akins.

"He signed as notary that you personally appeared before him," the fraud officer said, "and showed him your Texas driver's license."

"Thursday I was in Albuquerque," I said. "Picking up forty thousand pounds of dog food. I've got a bill of lading and a fuel receipt with the time on it."

"I'm sure you do." She turned one more page. "There's one more signature on this file, Mr. Pace. The co-owner."

I looked down. Right next to my fake signature, in the box marked Co-Borrower, was a name I'd watched being written on birthday cards and Christmas cards and permission slips for twenty-nine years. Rhonda Lynn Pace. The R was big and round, the way she always made it, and the tail of the e ran off the line, the way it always did.

That one was real.

Marisa didn't say anything. She just put her hand flat on the table next to mine, which is about as close to a hug as Marisa Calderon gets.

"Kyle handled the bank," I said.

"Kyle handled your half," Marisa said. "She handled hers."

"The line won't fund," the fraud officer said, and closed the file. "As of this morning, it's frozen. And the bank will be cooperating fully with the Amarillo Police Department." She looked through the glass wall. "Who I believe are here."

Salazar walked across the bank lobby with one uniformed officer behind her. She didn't come into our conference room. She walked right past it, to a little cubicle by the drive-through window, where a soft man in a polo shirt with a bank logo on it was sitting at his desk eating a breakfast taco like it was any other Monday.

We watched through the glass. Dale Akins looked up and saw her and dropped the taco on his keyboard.

"Mr. Akins," Salazar said, loud enough to carry. "Stand up, please."

"I already gave a statement! I came back like you said! I told you Friday I didn't know..."

"Forgery of a financial instrument, Mr. Akins. And tampering with a witness, which is you. You're the witness. Turn around."

Dale turned around. He was shaking. Half the branch was watching, tellers frozen with stacks of twenties in their hands, a man in a cowboy hat at the deposit counter with his checkbook open. The cuffs clicked.

"Kyle said the husband signed it at home!" Dale said, to nobody, to everybody. "Kyle said I was just stamping it, he said the husband signed at home and it was fine, he said it was a formality!"

"Did the husband show you his license?" Salazar asked.

Dale opened his mouth. Then he closed it.

"Kyle said he'd lost it," he said quietly.

Salazar walked him out past our glass room. As he went by, Dale looked through the glass right at me, at my hat in my lap, and I don't think he even knew who I was. He'd sworn on Friday night that he saw me walk into a hotel. He'd sworn on Thursday that I stood in front of his desk. He'd never seen my face in his life until this minute.

"Get out and look," I said, mostly to myself.

"What?" Marisa said.

"Nothing. Trucker thing."

That night, a little before eleven, I was lying on the motel bed watching a fishing show with the sound off when my phone lit up with Rhonda's name. I almost let it go. Then I saw it was the third time in a row, and I picked up.

She was whispering.

"Gordon. He's here. Kyle's here. He's on the porch."

I sat up.

"Call 911."

"I'm on the landline, my cell's in the car, Gordon, he's banging on the door, he says I signed it too and if I talk to the police he's going to tell them I planned the whole..."

There was a crash on her end, the storm door slamming against the house, and Kyle's voice, muffled, shouting her name.

"Rhonda, lock yourself in the bathroom. I'm coming. Hang up and dial 911, right now."

I was in my truck in thirty seconds. Not the Peterbilt, my pickup. I called 911 myself while I drove, with the phone on the seat on speaker, and gave the dispatcher the address and told her there was a man on the porch violating a bond condition, and she told me to stay in my vehicle when I got there, and I said yes ma'am, and I meant it, mostly.

Bell Ranch Road is eleven minutes from the Big Texan. I made it in eight.

Kyle's black Tahoe was parked across my driveway with the door hanging open and the dome light on. Kyle was on my front porch, under the porch light I'd replaced in April, kicking the front door. My front door. Solid oak, that door. My father hung it in 1974.

I got out of the truck. I know what the dispatcher said.

"Kyle," I said, from the bottom of the porch steps.

He turned around. His eyes were wet and wild and his sunglasses were gone, and without them he looked younger and a lot dumber.

"You," he said. "You did this. You and that old jarhead."

"Police are on the way, Kyle. Go sit in your car."

"She signed it! She signed that loan, Gordon, she signed it with her own hand, and if I go down she goes down, you hear me? You hear me, Rhonda?" He turned and pounded on the door with his fist. "You go down with me!"

"Kyle. Get off the porch."

"Or what?"

He came down the steps at me. He came fast, and he swung, a big, loopy right hand, all shoulder and no feet, a gym punch. I've been in exactly two fights in my life, both of them at a truck stop in Oklahoma in 1993, and I lost one of them. But you don't drive a million miles without learning how to get out of the way of something big coming at you.

I stepped inside the punch. His fist went past my ear and his arm went over my shoulder, and I turned my hip the way my old man taught me in this exact front yard when I was twelve, and Kyle Dorsey went over and landed flat on his back on the lawn I'd mowed on Saturday morning, and all the air came out of him at once like a blown tire.

I stepped back with my hands open and held them out to my sides.

He lay there wheezing. Then the red and blue lights came around the corner, two patrol cars and an unmarked one behind them, and the unmarked one had Detective Lorena Salazar in it. She'd heard the call go out on the radio. She told me later she'd been waiting all weekend to hear that address.

She walked across my lawn and looked down at Kyle, and then at me, and then at my open hands.

"Did he swing first, Mr. Pace?"

"He did."

"I saw it." It was my neighbor from across the street, a retired schoolteacher, in her bathrobe and curlers, holding up her phone. "I got it all. I've got it on the Ring and on my phone. That boy came at him like a bull."

Salazar nodded at the officers. They rolled Kyle over onto his stomach on my grass and cuffed him for the second time in four days.

"Mr. Dorsey," Salazar said, "your bond conditions said no contact with Rhonda Pace. You're on her porch. It's eleven at night. Your bond is revoked, and I'm adding assault. Would you like to explain anything to me?"

Kyle lifted his face off the grass.

"She signed it too!" he shouted, at the house. "Tell them, Rhonda! Tell them you signed the loan! Tell them whose idea the hotel was! Tell them!"

The front door opened.

Rhonda stood in the doorway in her bathrobe with the landline phone still in her hand, its long curly cord stretched all the way from the kitchen wall. She looked down at Kyle on the lawn, and at the cops, and at me standing there with my hands still open.

Then she looked at Salazar.

"I'd like to talk to a lawyer," she said. "And then I'd like to give a statement."

Ten days later, we sat across from each other in Marisa's conference room on Polk Street, at a long table that used to be a bowling lane. Marisa's office was a bowling alley in the sixties. She says she kept the lane because it reminds clients that everything eventually ends up in the gutter.

Rhonda had a lawyer now, a tired young man named on his business card as a family law associate, who kept clicking his pen. Rhonda had a folder too. I could tell from the way she held it that she thought it was a weapon.

"My client," the young man said, "is prepared to sign the agreed divorce decree, provided that the residence at 4417 Bell Ranch Road is awarded to her."

"No," Marisa said.

"Ms. Calderon, my client has been through a traumatic..."

"No."

Rhonda opened her folder.

"I'll say he hit Kyle," she said. "I was there. I saw it from the door. I'll say Gordon went after him first. He's always had a temper, he's always..."

"Rhonda," I said.

She stopped. It was the first time I'd spoken since we sat down.

"The neighbor's doorbell camera has the whole thing. So does her phone. Detective Salazar has both. He swung first. You know he swung first. You were standing in the door."

"Nobody'll believe an old woman in curlers."

"Then they'll believe the camera."

Marisa slid a single sheet of paper down the bowling lane. It stopped in front of Rhonda like a strike.

"That's a copy of the co-borrower page from the Panhandle Commerce file," Marisa said. "Your client's signature, Counselor. It's real. Next to a forged one, notarized by a man who's now cooperating with the Potter County District Attorney. A hundred and fifty thousand dollars against a house where one of the two owners never signed anything."

The young lawyer stopped clicking his pen.

"Detective Salazar tells me," Marisa went on, "that the district attorney views Mrs. Pace as a witness against Mr. Dorsey and Mr. Akins. A witness, Rhonda. Not a defendant. That's because you gave a full statement the night Kyle was on your lawn, and it matched the gym recording and Mr. Akins's statement. That's a very good place to be. I'd hate for you to talk yourself out of it by making a second false accusation against the same man in the same month."

Rhonda looked at the page. At her own big round R. At the tail of the e running off the line.

"Kyle said it was just a formality," she said.

"Dale said the same thing," I said. "Dale's in the county jail."

"Kyle said once the house was in my name, we'd be done talking."

"We are done talking."

"Gordon..." Her voice broke for the first time. "Gordon, I don't have anywhere to go. Kyle's in jail. My sister won't answer the phone. Tess won't answer the phone. Where am I supposed to go?"

"You keep the Camry. You keep half the savings. Your half. Not a dime less." I looked at her across a bowling lane where somebody bowled a perfect game in 1963, according to the little brass plaque. "You told a detective I followed you to a hotel. I didn't follow you, Rhonda. I walked in the front door because you asked me to. That's all I ever did for twenty-nine years. I came when you called."

"I know."

"I'm not coming anymore."

Her lawyer leaned over and whispered something in her ear. She closed her eyes. Then she opened them, and pulled the decree toward her, and signed it. The R was big and round. The tail of the e ran off the line.

Marisa turned the decree around and slid it down the lane to me, with a pen on top.

I picked it up. I read every single page first, all eleven of them, slowly, while Rhonda watched me and her lawyer looked at his watch, because you never back up without getting out and looking.

Then I signed my name at the bottom, in a hand that looks like a seismograph during an earthquake, with no loop on the G.

"That," I said, and slid it back down the lane, "is what my signature looks like."
