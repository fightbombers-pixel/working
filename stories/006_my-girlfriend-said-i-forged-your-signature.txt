"I forged your signature to co-sign a car for my brother. You can afford it."

My girlfriend said it across her mother's dinner table on a Sunday afternoon, in front of nine people and a pot roast, six days after the fact and twenty days before her brother's wedding. She said it the way you'd say you borrowed a man's lawnmower. Then she passed the green beans.

Her brother Rusty grinned at me from the other end of the table and jingled a key fob. It had a ram's head on it. The truck was a brand-new Ram 1500, sitting in June Dunbar's driveway with a temporary tag in the back window, and according to the paperwork Loretta pulled out of her purse and waved at me like a party favor, I was on the hook for every one of its fifty-eight thousand, four hundred dollars.

I set my fork down on the edge of my plate.

"I see," I said.

That was all. Two words. Then I stood up, pushed my chair in the way my mother taught me, and walked out the front door to the driveway. I took out my phone and photographed the VIN through the windshield, then the temporary tag, then the dealer sticker that said Summit City Dodge. Behind me, through the screen door, I could hear Loretta laughing and telling her mother I'd gone out for some air.

Three hours later, Rusty was standing on the Summit City Dodge lot calling me, asking why the finance manager wouldn't look him in the eye.

What Loretta didn't know was the text sitting on my phone from a Tuesday afternoon in September, the one I'd read twice, saved, and never said a word about.

My name is Herbert Moss. I'm fifty-eight years old, and I've been a floor supervisor at Three Rivers Axle in Fort Wayne, Indiana, for thirty years. On a factory floor, every single thing gets logged. Who badged in, who badged out, who signed off on what machine at what minute. People think the log is paperwork. The log is the truth with a timestamp on it. Hold on to that.

I sat in my truck in June's driveway for a minute with the engine off. Through the front window I could see them all at the table. June at the head. Loretta next to her. Rusty with the key fob. His fiancée, Shayla Morrow, who looked like she wanted to crawl under the tablecloth. Two aunts, an uncle, and a cousin who'd been on his phone the whole meal.

Then the screen door banged, and Rusty came jogging across the lawn in his socks.

"Herb. Hey, Herb, hold up."

I rolled the window down.

Rusty is thirty-three, works the parts counter at a farm supply store in New Haven, and is about as smart as a bag of hammers but not a bad kid. He leaned on my door.

"Man, that was weird in there," he said. "You okay?"

"Rusty, did you know?"

"Know what?"

"That she signed my name."

He blinked at me. "She said you were cool with it. She said you said, quote, 'Whatever helps the kid.' She said you were just too busy to drive over and sign it yourself."

"I never said that."

"Well, she said you did." He scratched the back of his neck. "I mean, you're cool with it now, right? 'Cause the dealership already ran it and everything."

"Rusty," I said. "Go back inside and finish your pot roast."

"But you're cool?"

I started the engine. "I said I see."

He stood there in his socks on the wet grass watching me back out, and I could tell from his face that he didn't know what that meant. Nobody in that house knew what that meant.

Here's what it meant.

On September ninth, a Tuesday, at a little after two in the afternoon, my phone buzzed on the floor at work with a credit alert. A hard inquiry. Summit City Dodge. I looked at it, frowned, and figured it was a mistake. Then I didn't figure that anymore, because I hadn't been to a dealership in four years, and the only other person who knew where I kept my tax folder, with my Social Security card and my last three pay stubs in it, was the woman I'd been living with for seven years.

I didn't say anything. I wanted to see if she'd tell me.

She told me six days later. Over pot roast. With an audience.

My phone rang before I got home. It was Rusty again.

"Herb, so the finance guy here, Marty, he's acting real strange. I came over to grab the floor mats I forgot, and I said my brother-in-law says hi, and Marty went kind of gray and said he needed to make a call. What's going on?"

"I'm not your brother-in-law, Rusty."

"Well, you know. Basically."

"Not basically. Not legally. Not on paper." I pulled into my driveway. "And on paper is where all of this is going to get decided."

I hung up before he could ask me what that meant too.

I didn't sleep much. Loretta came home a little after nine, singing along to the radio in her car. She came into the living room, kissed the top of my head like nothing had happened, and said, "You were rude to leave like that. Mom made a pie."

"I'm sure it was a good pie."

"It was cherry." She sat down on the arm of my recliner. "You're not actually mad, are you? About the truck?"

"I'm thinking about it."

"Herbert, you make good money. You've got no kids, no debts, the house is almost paid off. Rusty's got nobody. He needed a truck for the farm-store deliveries and nobody would give him a loan with his credit. You can afford it."

There it was again. You can afford it.

"How'd you get my Social Security number?" I said.

She laughed. "Honey, I've done your taxes paperwork for five years. It's in the blue folder."

"And the signature?"

"I've watched you sign birthday cards for seven years. It's not hard. It's a big H and a squiggle." She kissed my head again. "Don't be dramatic. It's just a truck."

She went to bed. I sat in the dark for another hour with the TV off, and then I got up and went to the blue folder in my desk. My Social Security card was there. My pay stubs were there. The one from August was missing.

At eight sharp Monday morning, I took a personal day for the first time in two years and walked into the Three Rivers Federal Credit Union on Coldwater Road, because that's who the Summit City paperwork said was carrying the loan.

The teller sent me to a little glass office in the back, where a woman about my age with short gray hair and reading glasses pushed up on her head introduced herself as Ramona Fitch, fraud investigations.

"Mr. Moss," she said, looking at the photos on my phone. "You're telling me you never signed this."

"Never saw it before yesterday."

"Never went to the dealership?"

"Not in four years."

She pulled the loan file up on her screen and turned it toward me. There it was. Herbert J. Moss, co-borrower. A big H and a squiggle.

"That's not my signature," I said.

"It's close."

"It's close because she's watched me sign birthday cards for seven years."

Ramona's mouth twitched like she wanted to laugh and knew she shouldn't. "Who's she?"

"My girlfriend. Loretta Dunbar. It's her brother's truck."

She wrote that down. Then she scrolled. "The dealer's notes say the co-borrower's spouse brought the documents home for him to sign, because he was at work. Spouse."

"We're not married."

"Says here, 'Mrs. Moss.'"

"There is no Mrs. Moss," I said. "There's never been a Mrs. Moss. My mother was the last one, and she's been gone since 2011."

Ramona took her glasses off her head and set them on the desk.

"Mr. Moss, here's what happens," she said. "You file a police report, today if you can. You fill out an identity theft affidavit with us. I open a fraud investigation. If we find the signature isn't yours, you come off the loan entirely. It's like you were never on it."

"And the truck?"

"The truck has a primary borrower. Russell Dunbar. If he can't qualify for the loan alone, and from what I'm looking at, he can't, then the loan was approved on a fraud and it gets called due. He'll have a short window to pay it off or refinance somewhere else." She paused. "If he can't do that, we take the collateral back."

"The truck."

"The truck."

"How short is the window?"

"Usually ten business days after the investigation closes." She looked at me. "Mr. Moss, I have to ask. You understand that if you file this, there's a police report with your girlfriend's name on it. Forgery on a loan document is a felony in Indiana."

I sat there for a second and thought about that. Seven years. Christmas mornings. Her laugh in the kitchen.

Then I thought about that Tuesday in September, the phone buzzing on the floor while I was walking corporate auditors through a lockout procedure, and how she'd known for six days and smiled at me every one of them.

"I understand," I said. "Where's the police station?"

The Fort Wayne Police Department's front desk took my report in about forty minutes. The officer was a heavyset guy named Tony Cardella, maybe forty, with a coffee mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST DAD.

"So she just told you," he said. "At dinner."

"At dinner. In front of nine people."

"And you said?"

"I said 'I see.'"

He looked up from his keyboard. "That's it?"

"That's it."

"Man, my wife would've had me in the ground." He typed for a while. "You got anything showing you didn't sign it? Besides your word?"

I held up my phone and showed him the credit alert. September ninth, a few minutes after two in the afternoon.

"I was at work when this came in," I said. "And I can prove exactly where I was standing."

Officer Cardella wrote down the time. He wrote it down twice.

"This'll go to a detective," he said. "Probably Detective Albanese, she gets the fraud stuff. She'll call you." He printed the report and handed me a copy with a case number on top. "Mr. Moss, for what it's worth, most guys who walk in here with a story like this are screaming. You're the calmest one I've seen all year."

"On my floor," I said, "the guy who yells is the guy who didn't check the machine."

I got back in my truck. Before I could start it, my phone buzzed. A text from Loretta.

"Rusty says the dealership called him. They want him to come in. What did you do?"

I looked at it for a long time. Then I typed two words, the same two words, and I sent them.

"I see."

The next text came in under ten seconds.

"Herbert. Answer me. WHAT DID YOU DO."

I put the phone face down on the passenger seat and drove to Fort Wayne's Famous Coney Island downtown, because a man needs to eat, and because I knew my oldest friend would be there at noon like he's been there every Monday since 1997.

Clyde Ambrose was in the back booth with two coneys, extra onions, and a copy of the News-Sentinel folded to the obituaries. Clyde reads the obituaries the way other men read box scores. He says it's to see who he outlasted.

Clyde and I grew up three houses apart on Spy Run Avenue. He worked the line next to me for eight years before he hurt his back, and then he bought a used wrecker and started Ambrose Towing and Recovery. Twenty-two years later he's got four trucks, a contract with half the lenders in Allen County, and a sense of humor that only shows up when things are really bad.

"You look like a man who took a personal day," he said.

"I took a personal day."

"Herbert Moss took a personal day. Somebody check if the river's running backward." He pushed the second coney at me. "Eat. Then talk."

I ate. Then I talked. When I got to the part where she said "You can afford it," Clyde put his coffee down very carefully, the way you set down something you're afraid you might throw.

"She forged your name," he said.

"Big H and a squiggle."

"On fifty-eight grand."

"Fifty-eight four."

"For Rusty." He shook his head. "Rusty Dunbar once got his arm stuck in a vending machine at the Allen County fair trying to steal a Snickers he'd already paid for."

"I remember."

"Took two firemen."

"I remember, Clyde."

He leaned back. "Who's carrying the loan?"

"Three Rivers Federal."

Clyde went very still. Then he started to grin, slow, like a man who's just been dealt a card he likes.

"Herb," he said. "Who do you think Three Rivers Federal calls when they need a vehicle recovered?"

"I didn't come here for that."

"I know you didn't. I'm just telling you. It's a small town, wearing a big town's pants." He pointed his coney at me. "Whatever happens with that truck, it won't be me that makes it happen. It'll be the bank. I just drive the hook."

"Clyde."

"I'm only saying, if it happens, I'd want you to hear it from me first."

When I got home at four, Loretta was waiting at the kitchen table. She had her purse in front of her and her car keys in her hand, like she couldn't decide whether to leave or fight. She decided to fight.

"Rusty went to the dealership," she said. "The finance manager told him there's a fraud hold on the loan. A fraud hold, Herbert. They told my brother he might be committing fraud."

"He's not. You are."

"Excuse me?"

"He didn't sign my name. You did."

She stood up. "You went to the police."

"I went to the bank. Then I went to the police."

"Over a truck!"

"Over my name," I said. "My name's on fifty-eight thousand dollars I never agreed to. If Rusty stops paying, they come after me. My credit, my house, my pension. That's not a truck, Loretta. That's my whole life with a big H and a squiggle on it."

"Rusty's not going to stop paying!"

"Rusty got his arm stuck in a vending machine."

"That was twelve years ago!"

"Took two firemen."

She threw her keys on the table. They slid off the other side and hit the floor. Neither of us picked them up.

"You said you'd help him," she said. "Last Christmas. You said, 'If the kid ever needs a hand, let me know.' Those were your words."

"A hand. Not my Social Security number."

"It's the same thing!"

"It's not even in the same zip code."

She came around the table. Her voice dropped, went soft, the voice she uses on her mother. "Honey. Herbert. You can afford it. That's all I was thinking. You're the most responsible man I know. You'd never let it fall apart. I knew Rusty would be safe because you'd be standing behind him."

"Without asking me."

"If I'd asked, you'd have said no."

"That's why you ask, Loretta. So people can say no."

She stared at me. And then something in her face shut like a door.

"Fine," she said. "You want to play it like this? Here's how it goes. You call the bank, and the police, and whoever else you called, and you tell them you remembered. You signed it and forgot. You're getting older, you forget things, everybody knows it."

"I'm fifty-eight."

"You put your reading glasses in the freezer last month."

"That was one time."

"It's your word against mine, Herbert. And I'm the one who's been in this house seven years. I've paid toward the mortgage. I've paid the light bill. If you don't take it back, I'll go to a lawyer, and I'll take half this house." She picked her keys up off the floor. "You've got until the wedding."

She went into the guest room and shut the door. Not slammed. Shut. Which was worse.

I called Nadine Fulton at home that night, which you're not supposed to do, but Nadine did my mother's will and told me then to call her anytime, and I'd never once taken her up on it.

"Herbert Moss," she said. "It's nine-thirty at night."

"I know. I'm sorry. Can she take half my house?"

Nadine laughed, a short dry bark. "Are you married to her?"

"No."

"Then no. Indiana hasn't recognized common-law marriage since 1958. Whose name is on the deed?"

"Mine. Bought it in 2009. Two years before I met her."

"Then it's your house. If she paid toward bills, she paid rent, basically. She might file something claiming she's owed money back. A judge will look at it, and a judge will see that she lived there seven years for a share of the light bill. Good luck to her." I heard her take a sip of something. "Herbert, is this the same woman who forged your name on a car loan?"

"How do you know about that?"

"I'm on the credit union board. Ramona Fitch called me this afternoon to ask if I knew you. Small town."

"Big town's pants," I said.

"What?"

"Nothing. Something Clyde says."

"Here's what you do," Nadine said. "Tomorrow, you give her a written thirty-day notice to vacate. Polite. One paragraph. You keep a copy. You don't argue with her, you don't threaten her, and you don't say one word about the police report. Let the detective do her job."

"She says she'll tell them I signed it and forgot."

"Did you?"

"No."

"Then let her say it," Nadine said. "Under oath, if it comes to that. People who lie the loudest usually lie in writing too. And writing is where they get caught."

I typed up the notice that night on the old computer in the den. One paragraph. Thirty days. I left it on the kitchen table under the salt shaker, where she'd see it with her coffee.

I was gone by six. I didn't see her read it. But a little before seven my phone started buzzing, and it didn't really stop for the next four days.

Wednesday, it was her mother. June Dunbar called me at the plant, at my desk line, which Loretta must have given her.

"Herbert, I'm calling as the closest thing you've got to a mother-in-law," June said.

"June, I'm at work."

"Then I'll be quick. You are humiliating my daughter. You are humiliating my son. Two weeks before his wedding. Do you know what Shayla's family is going to think?"

"I imagine they'll think whatever's true."

"Be a man, Herbert. Sign whatever you need to sign, call off the police, and help the family. That's what men do."

"June," I said. "A man doesn't let somebody else sign for him. That's sort of the whole point of being one."

She hung up on me. I wrote down the time, out of habit.

Thursday, it was Rusty, at the plant gate at the end of my shift. He was standing next to the Ram, which still had the temporary tag and now had a little fuzzy pair of dice hanging from the mirror.

"Herb, the bank called me," he said. "They say if the investigation goes the way it's going, I got ten business days to pay off the whole thing or get my own loan."

"I know."

"Nobody's gonna give me a fifty-eight-thousand-dollar loan, Herb."

"I know that too."

"So you gotta tell 'em you signed."

"I didn't sign, Rusty."

"But Loretta said—"

"Loretta said a lot of things." I unlocked my truck. "Rusty, can I ask you something? Did you ever see me sign anything? Did you ever talk to me about this truck? Even once?"

He opened his mouth. Then he closed it. He looked at the Ram, and then at me, and I could see the slow gears turning behind his eyes, the way you watch an old conveyor start up in the morning.

"No," he said finally. "She handled it. She said you were too busy."

"I'm a floor supervisor, Rusty. I'm busy. I'm not too busy to sign my own name."

He didn't say anything else. He got in the Ram and drove off, and he didn't spin the tires, which for Rusty was a big deal.

Friday morning, Gail Patterson from HR stuck her head into my office. Gail is sixty-one, has worked at Three Rivers for twenty-six years, and knows everything about everybody in the building, including things they don't know themselves.

"Herbert, honey, close the door," she said, and closed it herself. "A detective called me yesterday. A woman named Albanese. Asking about you."

"I figured she might."

"She wanted to know about an employment verification." Gail sat down across from me and opened a manila folder. "Summit City Dodge called on September ninth, a little before two, to verify your employment and your salary for a car loan. The caller who set it up was a woman who said she was your wife."

"Mrs. Moss."

"Mrs. Moss," Gail said. "I was the one who took the call. I remember thinking, since when does Herbert have a Mrs.? I even put a note in the log."

She slid the log across my desk. In Gail's neat handwriting, next to the date and time: Caller ID'd self as wife. Herbert not married? Check.

"I never checked," she said, sounding guilty. "I got busy. I'm sorry."

"Gail, you just did me the biggest favor of my life."

"There's more," she said. "Albanese asked where you were at two in the afternoon that day. So I pulled it."

She put a second sheet on top of the first. It was a printout from the badge system and the corporate safety audit log. On September ninth, from half past one until after four in the afternoon, Herbert J. Moss was on Line Four, walking two auditors from corporate through a lockout-tagout inspection. Both auditors had signed the log next to my name. Every station had a timestamp.

The dealership paperwork said I'd signed the loan a little after two, at Summit City Dodge, eleven miles across town.

"You couldn't have signed it," Gail said. "Unless you've got a twin."

"Just a girlfriend," I said, "with a good eye for squiggles."

My phone buzzed on the desk. A number I didn't know, a 260 area code. I picked up.

"Mr. Moss? This is Detective Teresa Albanese, Fort Wayne PD." She had a voice like a woman who'd heard every excuse in the world and wasn't impressed by any of them. "I've got the finance manager from Summit City Dodge sitting in my office. He just changed his statement. Come down and give me yours, and I'll walk you through where this stands."

The finance manager was coming out of the detective's office just as I walked in. Marty Lomax is about forty-five, with a spray tan the color of a basketball and a tie that cost more than it should. He saw me and stopped in the hallway like a deer in a parking lot.

"Mr. Moss," he said. "I, uh. I want to say I'm sorry."

"For what?"

He looked back at the office door, then at me. "We're supposed to have the co-borrower sign in person. With ID. It's the rule. But she was so nice, and she had your pay stub and your Social, and she said you were on a double shift and couldn't get away, and the month was ending and I needed the deal." He swallowed. "I let her take the papers out to her car. She was back in ten minutes."

"Ten minutes," I said.

"I thought you were in the parking lot. I swear to God. I thought she walked them out to you."

"I was eleven miles away, walking two auditors through a lockout inspection."

He went a shade lighter under the tan. "Yeah. The detective showed me."

Detective Teresa Albanese was about fifty, with a gray streak in her black hair and an office that smelled like burnt coffee and printer toner. She had my badge log, Gail's call log, Ramona's fraud file, and Marty's new statement laid out on her desk like a hand of cards.

"Mr. Moss," she said. "Sit down."

I sat.

"Here's where we are," she said. "The dealership now admits the documents left the building unsigned and came back signed. Your plant has you on camera and on a signed audit log across town at that exact time. The employment verification was set up by a woman calling herself your wife. And you're not married."

"No, ma'am."

"So I've got a pretty clear picture." She folded her hands. "I'm sending it to the prosecutor's office. They decide on charges, not me and not you. I want you to understand that."

"I understand."

"Some people in your chair ask me to drop it. Because it's family."

"She's not family," I said. "She made sure of that on September ninth."

Detective Albanese looked at me for a long moment, and then she nodded once, like a box had been checked.

"The credit union already has my report," she said. "Ramona Fitch tells me the investigation closes Monday. After that, Mr. Dunbar has ten business days."

I counted it in my head. Ten business days from Monday was the Friday before the wedding.

"The wedding's that Saturday," I said.

"I'm aware," the detective said dryly. "Your girlfriend called me twice this morning to tell me so. She also told me you signed the loan and forgot because you're getting senile."

"What'd you tell her?"

"I told her to come in and make that statement under oath." She smiled for the first time. "She hasn't come in."

That weekend, the phone went to pieces. I'd look at it in the morning while Clyde made eggs at his place, where I'd started eating breakfast so I didn't have to sit across from the guest-room door. Thirty-one texts from Loretta on Saturday. Forty on Sunday. Clyde started keeping count on a paper napkin.

"Forty," he said Sunday night. "That's a personal best."

"She sent one that just says 'HERBERT' eleven times."

"In a row?"

"In a row."

"That's not a text. That's a chant."

Sunday night, the guest-room door finally opened. I was at the kitchen table with a bowl of cereal, and Loretta came out in her robe and set a single typed sheet of paper next to my bowl, with a pen on top of it.

"Just read it," she said. "That's all I'm asking."

I read it. It said that I, Herbert J. Moss, had authorized Loretta Dunbar to sign the co-borrower documents on my behalf on September ninth, and that I had simply forgotten doing so. There was a line at the bottom for my signature and a line for a notary.

"Who wrote this?" I said.

"I did. I looked it up online. If you sign it, the bank has to drop it. The police have to drop it. Everybody goes back to normal, and nobody has to know anything."

"The detective has my badge log."

"Logs are wrong all the time."

"Not mine," I said. "Thirty years, Loretta. Not once."

She pushed the pen an inch closer to my hand. "Please, Herbert. Just sign it. You can afford to be the bigger man."

I picked up the pen. Her eyes went to it, and I watched the hope come up in her face like a kid on Christmas morning. Then I clicked it closed and set it back on top of the paper, and I slid the whole thing back across the table to her.

"Okay," I said. "I read it."

She stood there in her robe for a long second. Then she picked up the paper and tore it in half, and then in half again, and let the pieces fall on the table next to my cereal.

"You'll fold," she said. "By the wedding. You always fold."

She went back into the guest room. I finished my cereal. I put the torn pieces in a plastic sandwich bag and put the bag in the blue folder, because on my floor, you keep everything.

The texts went from angry to sweet and back again, like a furnace with a bad thermostat. "You're destroying my family." "I made your coffee every morning for seven years." "Rusty is going to lose everything." "Remember Gatlinburg?" "I'll tell everyone what kind of man you really are." "Please just call me." I read every one. I didn't answer any of them.

Monday, Ramona Fitch called. The investigation was closed. The signature wasn't mine. I was off the loan, like I'd never been on it. Rusty had ten business days.

On Wednesday, Rusty called and asked me to meet him at the Coney Island. Just me.

He was already in the back booth when I got there, in his farm-store polo, not eating. Rusty not eating is like a fire not burning.

"Herb," he said. "I gotta tell you some stuff."

"Go ahead."

"I went to three banks. Nobody'll touch me. One guy laughed. Like, out loud." He turned his coffee cup around on the table. "I told Loretta I'd just give the truck back. Drive it to the dealer and hand 'em the keys. Be done with it."

"That's the smartest thing anybody in your family's said in two weeks."

"She said no."

I waited.

"She said I can't give it back before the wedding," Rusty said. "Because she already told Shayla's whole family it was a wedding present. From you."

I sat back.

"From me."

"Uncle Herb's wedding present. She told Shayla's dad. She told Shayla's grandma. Shayla's dad shook my hand about it, Herb. He said, 'That's a man who takes care of his people.'" Rusty's face crumpled up. "And it's not even true. None of it's true. You never even knew."

"No," I said. "I didn't."

"Shayla's dad is gonna be at the wedding. Walt Morrow. He's a deacon. He runs the biggest dairy co-op in the county. He already doesn't like me much." He pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. "We're supposed to drive the truck to Gatlinburg after the reception. For the honeymoon. Loretta says she'll 'handle it.' She keeps saying that. 'I'll handle it.'"

"How's she going to handle it?"

"She says you'll fold. She says you always fold. She says by Saturday, you'll show up at the wedding and tell everybody you signed, and it'll all go away."

My phone buzzed on the table. Loretta. I turned it so Rusty could read it with me.

"Come to the wedding Saturday. Stand up in front of my family and tell them you signed it. Or I swear to God I'll take half your house and tell everyone in this town you're a senile old man who throws his family away."

Rusty read it twice. Then he looked up at me.

"Are you gonna come?"

I thought about it. I thought about thirty years on a line where you don't walk away from a problem, you walk up to it, you look at it, and you lock it out in front of everybody so nobody gets hurt by it later.

"Yeah, Rusty," I said. "I'm going to come."

The wedding was at the VFW hall on Lake Avenue, the big back room with the wood paneling and the flags. A hundred and forty guests. Pink and white streamers. A DJ in the corner playing "Night Moves" during dinner, which Shayla's grandmother loudly said was inappropriate.

I came at the reception, not the ceremony. I wore my good gray suit and brought one envelope in my inside pocket.

Loretta saw me the second I walked in. She was at the family table in a lavender dress, and her whole face lit up with relief, the way it would if she'd been holding her breath for two weeks. She got up and crossed the room fast and grabbed my arm.

"I knew you'd come," she said. "I knew it. I told Mom you'd come."

"I said I'd come."

"Okay. Okay, good. So after the cake, you stand up, and you say a few nice words, and you say the truck's from you, and—"

"Loretta."

"—and then we can all just go back to normal. I'll forgive you for the police thing. I already told Mom I'd forgive you."

I took the first envelope out and handed it to her.

"What's this?"

"It's a copy of the notice. You've got thirteen days left, and then I'm going to need my house key back."

She stared at it.

"You came to my brother's wedding," she said slowly, "to evict me."

"I came because you told me to come and say who signed that loan," I said. "I'm going to do that. I'm just going to tell the truth while I'm at it."

Her face changed. "You wouldn't."

"You can afford it," I said. "That's what you told me."

The DJ was calling everybody out for the first dance when a set of amber lights started flashing through the front windows of the VFW. Heads turned. People drifted toward the windows with their drinks the way people do at a fire.

I didn't have to look. I knew those lights. I'd known them since 1999.

At eight o'clock that morning, Clyde had called me.

"Herb," he'd said. "I got a recovery order from Three Rivers Federal on a white Ram 1500. Ten business days ran out yesterday at close. And the bank's been told the vehicle's leaving the state tonight for Tennessee. They want it before it crosses the line."

"Who told them that?"

"The borrower," Clyde said. "Rusty called Ramona himself yesterday afternoon. Told her he couldn't pay, told her where the truck would be, told her he was sorry. The kid did the decent thing, Herb. First time in his life."

So now, in front of a hundred and forty wedding guests, Clyde Ambrose was backing his flatbed up to a white Ram 1500 that had JUST MARRIED written across the back window in shoe polish and a string of tin cans tied to the hitch.

Loretta went past me out the front door like a shot. I followed. So did about half the wedding.

"Stop!" she yelled. "Stop right now! That's my brother's truck!"

Clyde climbed down from the cab, slow, with his clipboard. He touched his cap.

"Evening, ma'am."

"You can't take that. This is a wedding."

"Yes, ma'am. I can see that. Congratulations to the couple." He held up the clipboard. "This is a recovery order from Three Rivers Federal Credit Union. The loan was obtained by fraud and called due. Borrower couldn't pay. Vehicle's being recovered."

"Fraud?" Somebody behind me said it. Then a couple of people said it. Fraud, fraud, like it was going around the crowd hand to hand.

Loretta spun around and found me.

"Herbert. Tell him. Tell him you signed. Tell him right now, and he'll leave."

"I didn't sign it, Loretta."

"Tell him!"

"I was on Line Four with two auditors. It's in the log. Signed and timestamped."

"Nobody cares about your stupid log!"

"The detective cared," I said. "The bank cared. The dealership cared enough to change its story."

A big man in a dark suit pushed through to the front. Sixty-something, weathered face, hands like shovels. Walt Morrow, the bride's father.

"What's going on here?" he said. "Loretta, you told me this truck was a wedding gift from this man."

Loretta opened her mouth.

"It was a gift," she said. "From Herbert. He's just confused. He's been forgetting things, he—"

"Mr. Morrow," I said. "I never knew about this truck until three weeks ago, when she told me at her mother's dinner table that she forged my name on the loan. Her words were, 'I forged your signature to co-sign a car for my brother. You can afford it.'"

Walt Morrow turned and looked at Loretta. Not angry. Worse. The way a deacon looks at somebody in the back pew who just lied in church.

"Is that true?"

"It's—he's twisting it—"

Behind her, June Dunbar was standing in the VFW doorway with her hand over her mouth. Shayla was next to her, holding her train up off the gravel. Nobody was saying anything to help Loretta. Not one person.

Then Rusty came out through the crowd, still in his tux, his bow tie hanging loose. He walked right past his sister, up to Clyde, and held out the key fob with the ram's head on it.

"It's true, Mr. Morrow," Rusty said. "She signed it. Herb never knew. I didn't know he didn't know until last week." He put the fob in Clyde's hand. "It was never mine. I called the bank myself."

"Rusty!" Loretta said. "What are you doing?"

"What you should've done, Lo." He turned to Shayla. "I'm sorry, babe. We'll take your car to Tennessee. It's got a cassette player. It'll be fun."

Shayla laughed through her tears and grabbed his hand. Somebody in the crowd started clapping. Then a few more.

Loretta stood in the gravel in her lavender dress, with the house key envelope in one hand, watching Clyde winch the tin cans and the JUST MARRIED and the fifty-eight thousand, four hundred dollars up onto the flatbed.

She turned to me one last time. Her voice was shaking.

"You could've just paid it, Herbert," she said. "You can afford it."

"I can," I said. "That's why I'm keeping it."

Clyde finished chaining the truck down and climbed back into his cab. As he pulled out of the VFW lot, the tin cans dragged and rattled across the gravel behind him.

Walt Morrow watched the truck go. Then he put his big hand on my shoulder, looked over at Loretta, and said it loud enough for the whole front lot to hear.

"Ma'am, around here, when you sign a man's name, you'd better be standing next to him while he watches."
