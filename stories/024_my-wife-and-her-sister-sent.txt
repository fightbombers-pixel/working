"Elliot, it's me and Fran. You're out of the family business. Effective today. Don't come in Monday. Expect a call from our lawyer."

That was my wife's voice, coming out of my phone on speaker at the Lansing City Market on a Saturday morning in October, in front of fourteen people standing in line for cannoli. I had flour up to my elbows, so I'd hit speaker with my knuckle the way I always do, thinking it was our bookkeeper about the egg order.

Then her sister's voice came on, sharper. "And leave your keys with Marco. Don't make this ugly."

The beep. The line went quiet.

So did the line of customers. A woman in a Michigan State sweatshirt holding a box of sfogliatelle just stood there with her mouth open. Behind the counter, my two weekend girls froze like somebody had pulled a fire alarm.

Twenty-two years I'd run that bakery. I'd owned forty-five percent of it for seventeen. And my wife and her sister had fired me by voicemail, on speaker, at the farmers market.

I wiped my hands on my apron, picked up the phone and typed back one word.

"Understood."

Then I put the phone face down on the counter and handed the woman in the Spartans sweatshirt her box.

"That'll be fourteen dollars, ma'am," I said. "The cannoli's on me."

Twenty days later, my phone would show twenty-nine missed calls in forty-eight hours from two women who'd never in twenty-two years called me twice in one day.

What they didn't know that Saturday was what their own father had typed into section 8.2 of the bakery's operating agreement in 2008, on his old IBM Selectric, in a document both of them had signed without reading.

My name is Elliot Burgess. I'm fifty-three. I've run Dellucci Bakery on Michigan Avenue in Lansing since 2003, the year my father-in-law had his first heart attack and asked me to come in "for a few weeks" to keep the ovens going. I was a network administrator for the State of Michigan back then. I never went back. And what twenty-two years of baking taught me is this: you can't rush a dough. Every step has a time on it, and if you skip one, it doesn't matter how hot your oven is. Remember that.

"Mr. B?" said Tasha, the older of my two weekend girls, very quietly. "Are you okay?"

"I'm fine."

"Did they just fire you? On speaker?"

"They did."

"Can they do that?"

"They just did," I said. "Now, can you get the next lady a dozen pizzelle? She's been very patient."

Wendy was at the kitchen island when I got home that afternoon, with a glass of pinot grigio and her laptop open. She didn't look up.

"You got the message," she said.

"Me and fourteen people buying cannoli."

She winced. Just a little. "You had it on speaker?"

"My hands were in flour, Wendy. I always have it on speaker. You know that."

"Well." She took a sip. "Now everybody knows. It's probably better that way."

I set my keys on the counter. "Want to tell me why?"

She closed the laptop. My wife is fifty-one, dark-haired like all the Delluccis, with her father's nose and her mother's way of deciding something and then explaining it to you like you were slow.

"Fran and I have talked about it for a long time," she said. "The bakery's been stuck. Same products, same customers, same way Daddy did everything. We want to grow. We want to modernize. And frankly, Elliot, you're part of the old way."

"I'm the guy who put in the new ovens."

"In 2014."

"They're still the newest ovens on Michigan Avenue."

"We're bringing in a general manager. Somebody with real restaurant experience." She paused like she was deciding how much to say. "Garth Nolan."

I knew the name. Everybody at Lansing Eastern High School class of 1992 knew the name. Garth Nolan had been her boyfriend for three years before he went off to Michigan State and broke her heart at a homecoming dance. He'd come back to town last spring, divorced, after the Applebee's franchise he managed in Grand Rapids closed.

"Garth Nolan," I said. "Your prom date."

"He managed a restaurant with forty employees."

"Into the ground."

"That's not fair."

"Wendy, are you sleeping with Garth Nolan?"

She looked at me for a long time over the wine glass. Then she set it down.

"I want a divorce too," she said. "Since we're getting everything out. Barry Sutcliffe's drawing it up. He'll call you Monday about the bakery and the house, both."

"Barry Sutcliffe."

"He's very good."

"He's your father's old poker buddy. He's eighty."

"He's seventy-six, and he knows the bakery's paperwork better than anybody." She opened her laptop again, like the conversation was a phone call she was ready to hang up. "Barry says when a member is terminated from the company, the other members can buy him out at book value. Section nine-something. Book value is about three-eighty. That's fair, Elliot. Three hundred eighty thousand dollars for your share. You'll be fine."

"The bakery's worth four million, Wendy."

"On paper."

"On Meijer's paper. They tried to buy it from your dad in 2019."

She didn't look up from the screen, but her fingers stopped typing for half a second. Then they started again.

"I'll sleep at Fran's tonight," she said. "Barry will call you."

I went out to the garage and found the operating agreement in a fireproof box under my workbench, where I'd kept it since 2008. Thirty-one pages. My father-in-law, Joe Dellucci, had typed the first draft himself on his IBM Selectric, two fingers at a time, and then paid a lawyer to fix the grammar.

I read it all the way through, twice. Then I sat down on the step to the kitchen and laughed until my eyes watered.

Nora Fiske's office is above a florist on Grand River Avenue in Old Town. She's in her forties, wears cardigans with the sleeves pushed up and has represented half the small businesses in Ingham County that have ever had a family fight. I sat across from her Monday morning while she read the agreement with a yellow highlighter in her teeth.

"Okay," she said finally, taking the highlighter out. "First thing. Section nine-four. The book value buyout." She tapped it. "Your wife's lawyer isn't wrong that it exists. If a member is removed, the company can buy his interest at book value. But read the second sentence."

I'd read it. I let her say it anyway.

"The company has thirty days to elect to buy, and the purchase price must be paid in full, in cash, at closing." She looked up. "Elliot, does that bakery have three hundred eighty thousand dollars in cash sitting around?"

"It has about forty thousand in operating. Payroll's every two weeks."

"Then they can't do it unless they borrow. And look at section six-three." She flipped back. "Unanimous consent of the members required for any borrowing over fifty thousand dollars, any sale of the company or its assets, any amendment to this agreement, and the hiring of any manager or officer at a salary over seventy-five thousand." She raised an eyebrow. "Unanimous. Your father-in-law didn't trust anybody, did he."

"He didn't trust his daughters," I said. "Not with the business. He used to say they'd sell it for a cruise."

"So they can't borrow without your vote. They can't hire their GM without your vote. And they can't sell to Meijer without your vote." Nora sat back. "They've fired you as an employee. Fine. They can do that, you're at-will. But they can't fire you as a forty-five-percent owner. You're still there, every time they want to do anything bigger than a bread order."

"What about eight-two?"

Nora smiled. It was a good smile.

"Section eight-two," she said. "Transfer of interests. Any member may sell his interest to a third party, provided he first offers it to the other members at the same price and on the same terms, by written notice to the company. The other members have ten days to match. If they don't, he's free to sell to whoever he wants." She closed the agreement. "Your father-in-law wrote a right of first refusal with a ten-day fuse. Most of these give sixty. He gave ten."

"He was impatient."

"He was a baker. Ten days is a long time for bread." She leaned forward. "Elliot. Do you have a buyer?"

"I have somebody I'd like to call."

"Then here's how it goes. You get an offer, in writing. You send written notice to the company, by certified mail, return receipt, at the company's address, offering your forty-five percent to your wife and her sister at that exact price. If they match within ten days, they buy you out at a real price instead of book value, and you walk away rich. If they don't—"

"They won't. They don't have the money."

"Then on day eleven," Nora said, "you can sell your share to anybody you want. And whoever buys it gets every right you have. The vote. The veto. Section six-three." She paused. "And section eleven, which I'm guessing your wife hasn't read either. Every member has the right to inspect and copy all books and records of the company, on five days' notice."

"Every member," I said.

"Every member," Nora said. "Who are you going to call, Elliot?"

I didn't answer right away.

That evening our bookkeeper, Marco Abate, called me at home. He's sixty-one, has kept Dellucci's books since 1994, and wears a cardigan over a short-sleeved shirt every single day of the year.

"Elliot," he said, quietly, like he was calling from a closet. "Wendy came in today. She said anything that comes to the bakery addressed to you, I'm supposed to put in the trash. She said you don't work here anymore and your mail isn't the company's problem."

"Did she say anything about mail addressed to the company?"

"Well, no. That's my job. I sign for all the company mail. Always have."

"Marco," I said, "something's going to come by certified mail in a few days. It's going to be addressed to Dellucci Bakery LLC, attention: the members. Not to me. To the company."

"Okay."

"Sign for it. Put it on Wendy's desk. Don't throw it away."

He was quiet for a second.

"Elliot," he said. "What did you do?"

"Nothing yet," I said. "Just sign for the mail, Marco. Like always."

Romano's Italian Bakery sits on the east side of Lansing, three miles from Dellucci's, in a brick building with a neon sign of a cannoli that's been half burned out since the Clinton administration. For thirty-eight years, the Romanos and the Delluccis have not spoken. Not at church, not at the Italian festival, not at the funeral home. In 1987, at the Festa Italiana on the riverfront, my father-in-law Joe Dellucci accused Salvatore Romano of stealing his mother's ricotta recipe, and then he threw a cannoli at him. It hit Sal in the ear. The Lansing State Journal ran a photo.

I parked out front on Tuesday morning and went in.

Sal Romano was behind the counter in a white apron, seventy-two years old, bald as an egg, with forearms like hams. He looked up when the bell rang over the door, and his face went through about four expressions before it landed on suspicious.

"You're Joe's son-in-law," he said.

"Elliot Burgess."

"I know who you are. You run the place now. You put in those Italian ovens." He wiped his hands on his apron. "Joe's been dead three years. You come to throw something at me?"

"I came to talk business, Mr. Romano."

"With me?"

"With you."

He looked at me for a long time. Then he turned and yelled over his shoulder, "Angela! Watch the counter!" And to me: "In the back. Bring your own coffee, I'm not giving a Dellucci free coffee."

His office was a desk in the corner of the bakery between two flour bins, with a crucifix on the wall and a framed copy of that 1987 Lansing State Journal photo hanging right over the desk, Sal holding his ear and Joe in mid-throw.

"You keep that up?" I said.

"Every day for thirty-eight years," Sal said. "Reminds me why I get up in the morning. So. What business?"

I told him. All of it. The voicemail at the City Market. Garth Nolan. The book value. The operating agreement. Section eight-two. Section six-three. Section eleven.

He listened without interrupting, which I'd been told was not like him. When I finished, he sat back in his chair and folded his big arms.

"So you're telling me," he said slowly, "that for some money, I can own forty-five percent of Joe Dellucci's bakery."

"That's what I'm telling you."

"And his daughters can't sell it, can't borrow on it, can't hire nobody without asking me first."

"Not without your vote."

"And I can walk in there and look at the books whenever I want."

"Five days' notice."

Sal Romano put both hands flat on his desk and looked up at the photograph on the wall. Then he started to laugh. It started low in his belly and came up like a train, until he had to take out a handkerchief and wipe his eyes.

"Joe," he said to the photograph. "Joe, you son of a gun. You hear this?"

"There's a ten-day right of first refusal," I said. "They get the chance to match. I'm telling you that up front. They probably can't."

"How much?"

"Fair value for forty-five percent of a business Meijer offered four million for in 2019. One point five."

He didn't even blink. "One point five. I'll have my bank write the letter today." He leaned forward. "But Elliot. I got to tell you something first, so you don't think I'm cheating you."

"Okay."

"Those girls are already trying to sell it." He tapped the desk. "My flour man, Rudy from Great Lakes Milling, he does both our bakeries. He tells me two weeks ago, the Dellucci sisters had a man from Meijer in their back office. Corporate guy. With a tablet. Walking around measuring the kitchen." Sal shrugged. "I figured they were selling to Meijer and you were going along. Now you tell me they fired you on the speakerphone."

"Two weeks ago?"

"Two weeks. Before your voicemail. With a new guy, too, Rudy says. Tall, gray, loud. Wearing a Spartans quarter-zip like he was still on the team."

Garth Nolan. Two weeks before they fired me, Garth Nolan had walked a Meijer executive through my kitchen.

"They were going to fire me, buy me out at book value for three-eighty and sell the whole thing to Meijer for four," I said slowly.

"With your three-eighty as a down payment on their cruise," Sal said. "That's how I'd do it, if I was a Dellucci." He stood up and stuck out a hand like a catcher's mitt. "But I'm a Romano. One point five. And Elliot? You tell those girls I want my cannoli recipe back."

"Was it really their grandmother's?"

He grinned at me with every tooth he had.

"Not telling," he said.

The certified letter went out Wednesday, from the post office on Saginaw Highway. Nora wrote it and I signed it. It went to Dellucci Bakery LLC, attention the members, at the bakery's address on Michigan Avenue. It offered my forty-five percent interest to Wendy Burgess and Francesca Dellucci, under section eight-two of the operating agreement, at a price of one million five hundred thousand dollars, cash at closing, to be matched in writing within ten days of receipt, with a copy of Sal Romano's bank letter attached.

Marco signed for it Thursday at ten-forty in the morning. He called me after.

"It's on Wendy's desk," he said. "On top. In the middle. With a coffee cup on it so it won't blow away."

"Did she read it?"

"She looked at the envelope," Marco said. "She said it was probably from your lawyer and she'd let Barry deal with it. She put it in her tote bag. With the wedding magazines."

"Wedding magazines?"

Marco cleared his throat. "Elliot, I wasn't going to say anything."

"Say it."

"There's a stack of them on her desk. Bridal. For older brides, it says on one of them. With a picture of a lady about her age in a dress." He paused. "Garth was in here this morning, too. In your office. Sitting in your chair. He asked me where the fire extinguisher inspection certificates are kept."

"What'd you tell him?"

"I told him I'd have to ask the general manager," Marco said, "and he said, 'You're looking at him.' And I said, 'Not according to section six-three.'" Marco sniffed. "I read it last night. After you called. Your father-in-law was a smart man."

"He threw a cannoli at a guy."

"Smart men do dumb things," Marco said. "Same as anybody."

Wendy came to the house Saturday afternoon to get more of her clothes, and she brought Garth.

I was out back cleaning the gutters when I heard his truck in the driveway. A big gray Ram with a Spartans decal in the back window and a hitch he'd never pulled anything with. By the time I came around the side of the house, he was standing on my porch with his arms folded, and Wendy was inside going through the hall closet.

Garth Nolan is fifty-four, six-foot-two, with silver hair he blows dry and teeth he paid for. He looked at my ladder and my work gloves and smiled like I was the help.

"Elliot," he said. "No hard feelings, man."

"About what, Garth?"

"About all of it. The bakery. Wendy." He spread his hands. "It's just one of those things. High school sweethearts. Second chances. You know how it is."

"I don't, actually."

"Well, that's life." He leaned against my porch post. "Look, the sooner you take Barry's deal, the sooner everybody moves on. Three-eighty's good money for a guy who just made bread."

"I ran the business."

"You ran the ovens, man. I'm going to run the business." He grinned. "We've got plans. Big plans. You'll read about it."

"In the Meijer press release?"

The grin stayed on his face, but it stopped moving, like a picture of a grin. Inside the house, I heard the closet go quiet.

Wendy came out onto the porch with an armload of sweaters. She looked at Garth. Then she looked at me.

"Who told you about Meijer?" she said.

"Lansing's a small town, Wendy. Your flour man talks."

"It's nothing. It's preliminary. It's just a conversation."

"A conversation where a guy from corporate measures my kitchen two weeks before you fire me."

"It's not your kitchen," Garth said.

"Section six-three says it's at least forty-five percent my kitchen," I said. "Have you read it, Garth? It's very short. Your girlfriend's father typed it himself. Any sale of the company needs all three members to say yes. Not two. Three."

Garth looked at Wendy. Wendy looked at the sweaters.

"Barry says that's handled," she said. "Barry says once we buy you out at book value, it's just me and Fran, and it doesn't matter what six-three says."

"Barry's right," I said. "Once you buy me out. With three hundred eighty thousand dollars. Cash. At closing. Within thirty days. Section nine-four." I took off my work gloves. "Where's the cash coming from, Wendy?"

She opened her mouth. Then she closed it. Then she said, "Barry's handling it," again, quieter.

On my way past their truck to the garage, I saw her tote bag on the front seat, the canvas one from Whole Foods she carries everywhere. A stack of glossy magazines was sticking out of the top. And tucked between a bridal magazine and an issue of Food and Wine, its corner just showing, was a white envelope with a green certified-mail sticker and the return address of Nora Fiske, Attorney at Law.

Unopened.

I kept walking. I didn't say a word. Day two of ten.

Day ten came and went the Saturday after that. Nobody called. Nobody matched.

On Monday morning, two days after the deadline, I sat in Nora's office above the florist and signed my name eleven times on a stack of paper, and Sal Romano signed his eleven times right under mine in a big loopy hand, and his banker from Capitol National slid a cashier's check across the table that had more zeros on it than I'd ever seen on one piece of paper.

"That's it?" Sal said, looking at the stack.

"That's it," Nora said. "Congratulations, Mr. Romano. You own forty-five percent of Dellucci Bakery LLC."

Sal stood up. He tugged his sport coat down over his belly. He was wearing a tie for the first time in what I'd guess was ten years.

"Good," he said. "Then I'm going over there for coffee."

"Now?"

"Why not now? I'm an owner. Owners drink the coffee." He grinned at me. "You want to come?"

"I'm not allowed in," I said. "I've been fired."

"Then wait in the truck," Sal said. "And keep your phone on."

I parked my truck across Michigan Avenue from the bakery at nine-forty, in front of the dry cleaner, where I could see through the big front window. Sal went in at nine-forty-five. Three minutes later my phone rang.

"I'm putting you in my shirt pocket," Sal said. "On speaker. Don't say nothing."

I heard the bell over the door. I'd heard that bell eleven thousand times. I heard the espresso machine hiss and the morning rush, a mother asking for a dozen bomboloni, and then I heard Tasha's voice, very young and very confused, say, "Mr. Romano?"

"Good morning, sweetheart," Sal said. "Coffee, black. And go get your bosses, please. Tell them their new partner is here."

Through the window I watched Tasha go into the back. I watched Wendy come out of my office, which was now apparently Garth's office, with a coffee mug in her hand. I watched her see Sal Romano standing at her counter in a tie. I watched her stop like she'd walked into a sliding glass door.

"Sal Romano," she said. Through the phone, her voice was tinny but clear. "What are you doing in my bakery?"

"Drinking my coffee, Wendy. Very good coffee. Better than mine. Don't tell nobody."

Fran came out behind her. Then Garth, in his Spartans quarter-zip, holding a clipboard.

"Is there a problem?" Garth said, in his manager voice. "Sir, I'm the general manager here—"

"No you're not," Sal said pleasantly. "Section six-three. I didn't vote for you."

The whole bakery got quiet. The mother with the bomboloni stopped halfway out the door.

"What are you talking about?" Fran said.

Sal reached into his inside pocket. I watched him through the window unfold a paper and lay it on the glass case, on top of the cannoli.

"Assignment of membership interest," he said. "Forty-five percent of Dellucci Bakery LLC. From Elliot Burgess to Salvatore Romano. Signed this morning at nine. Recorded with your registered agent at nine-twenty. The right of first refusal was offered to both of you, in writing, by certified mail, on the sixth of the month." He tapped it. "You had ten days. You didn't match. Now I'm your partner. Ciao."

Wendy picked up the paper. Her hands were shaking. Fran read it over her shoulder.

"What right of first refusal?" Fran said. "What certified mail? Wendy, what is he talking about?"

"I don't know," Wendy said. "I didn't get any— Barry said—"

"Marco signed for it," Sal said. "Thursday the sixth, ten-forty in the morning. Put it right on your desk. With a coffee cup on it so it wouldn't blow away."

Fran turned to her sister. "Wendy. Did you get a letter from Elliot's lawyer?"

Wendy didn't answer.

"Did you get a letter, Wendy?"

"I gave it to Barry," Wendy said. Her voice was small. "I thought. It was in my bag. I thought it was about the divorce."

"You thought," Fran said.

Garth set his clipboard down on the counter. "Okay, this is obviously some kind of stunt. Elliot can't just sell his share to a competitor. There's got to be a noncompete or something."

"There's not," Sal said. "I read it. Twice. Joe didn't put in a noncompete because Joe never thought anybody in his family would be stupid enough to fire the only man who knew how to run the ovens." He sipped his coffee. "One more thing. Section eleven. Every member has the right to inspect and copy all books and records, on five days' notice." He laid a second paper on top of the first. "That's my notice. My accountant will be here next Monday. Please have everything ready. Bank statements, register tapes, deposit slips, two years. Marco can help."

Through the window, I saw Wendy go white. Not a little pale. Ghost-white, like she'd been dipped in powdered sugar.

"Why do you need the register tapes?" she said.

"Because I'm an owner," Sal said. "Owners count the money."

He put a five-dollar bill on the counter for his coffee, which was the funniest thing I'd ever seen, and walked out. The bell rang over the door. He crossed Michigan Avenue to my truck, opened the passenger door and climbed in with a grunt.

"Joe," he said, to the roof of my truck. "You see that?"

My phone started ringing before he'd even shut the door. It was Wendy.

I let it ring.

It rang again four minutes later. Then Fran. Then Wendy. Then Fran again. Then a number I didn't know, which turned out to be Barry Sutcliffe calling from his office, and then Wendy again, twice in a row.

By Monday night I had eleven missed calls. By Tuesday noon, twenty. By Wednesday morning, forty-eight hours after Sal Romano drank his coffee, I had twenty-nine missed calls on my phone from my wife and her sister, and seven voicemails, and I'd listened to every one.

"Elliot, call me back. We need to talk about this."

"Elliot, it's Fran. Please. Please just call."

"Elliot, Barry says you can't do this. Barry says it's not legal. Call me."

"Elliot, Barry says it might be legal. Call me back."

"It's Fran again. Elliot, I didn't know. I swear to God I didn't know about the letter. She never told me. Please call me. Please undo it. Please."

"Elliot, Garth left. He took his truck and he left. He says this isn't what he signed up for. Please call me."

And the last one, from Wendy, at six-fifteen Wednesday morning, very quiet, like she was in a car alone.

"Elliot. Sal's accountant is coming Monday. I need you to call me before Monday. Please. I need you to undo it. Just call Sal and undo it. You can do that, right? Undo it? Please, honey."

Honey. She hadn't called me honey since the Obama administration.

I didn't call back.

Fran came to the house Wednesday night alone.

She sat on my front steps in the dark in her bakery fleece and waited until I came out, because I didn't open the door when she knocked, and she'd decided to outlast me. Fran's three years younger than Wendy, never married, lives above a shoe repair shop in Old Town and has spent twenty years standing behind her big sister's shoulder agreeing with things. I'd always liked her better than I'd ever told anybody.

I sat down on the steps next to her.

"I didn't know about the letter," she said. "I want you to know that first."

"I believe you."

"She told me Barry had it handled. She told me you'd take three-eighty and go away and be relieved, because you always said you were tired." Fran stared at the streetlight. "She told me Meijer was my idea. That I'd said it first. I didn't, Elliot. I'd never even met the Meijer man until he was in the kitchen with Garth."

"When did Garth come into it?"

"Last spring. She said he was a consultant. Then she said he was a friend. Then I saw his truck at her condo at six in the morning." Fran laughed a little, not happily. "I'm not an idiot. I'm just a sister."

"What do you want, Fran?"

She turned and looked at me.

"I want to know why she's so scared of Sal's accountant," she said. "She's been throwing up since Monday. She called Marco at midnight and asked him if register tapes can be shredded. Marco called me. He said he wanted somebody else to know he'd said no."

I'd figured it was something like that. I'd figured it the second Wendy asked Sal why he needed the register tapes.

"Fran," I said. "Who makes the bank deposits?"

"Wendy does. Every night. Since Daddy died. She says she likes the drive."

"Who counts the drawer before the deposit?"

"Wendy does. She doesn't like anybody else counting it."

"And who's been paying Garth Nolan's truck payment since spring?"

Fran sat very still on my steps. I watched her do the math, the way I'd watched Garth do it, and Sal, and everyone else in this story who'd finally decided to count.

"She said he had a settlement from his divorce," she said quietly.

"Fran. He drove an Applebee's into the ground in Grand Rapids. There's no settlement."

She put her face in her hands.

"How much?" she said through her fingers.

"I don't know. I haven't seen the books in three weeks. I've been fired." I stood up. "But Sal's accountant is going to know by Monday. And Fran, Saturday's the sixtieth."

She looked up.

The sixtieth anniversary of Dellucci Bakery. Joe and Rose Dellucci opened the doors on Michigan Avenue in October of 1965 with one oven and a loan from Rose's brother. Wendy had been planning the party since August: a tent on the sidewalk, a cake shaped like the original storefront, a polka band from the Polish Hall, and the Lansing State Journal coming to take pictures.

"She's still having it," Fran said. "She says it would look bad to cancel. She says we have to look strong in front of the town."

"Then I guess we'll all be there," I said.

The tent went up on the sidewalk in front of Dellucci's at nine Saturday morning. By noon there were two hundred people on Michigan Avenue eating free cookies under red, white and green balloons, and the polka band was playing a song about beer that half the crowd knew the words to. A reporter from the State Journal was taking pictures of the cake, which was four feet long and shaped like the bakery the way it looked in 1965, right down to the striped awning.

I came at one, in a clean shirt, and stood at the back by the parking meters. Customers I'd been handing bread to for twenty-two years kept coming up to shake my hand. Mrs. Kowal from the Catholic church gave me a hug and said she'd heard what happened at the City Market and she'd stopped buying her paczki here on principle.

"They're the same paczki, Mrs. Kowal."

"Not without you they're not," she said.

Wendy was under the tent in a red dress, with a microphone, smiling for the reporter. She saw me at the back of the crowd and the smile slipped, just for a second, and then it came back on like a porch light.

Fran stood next to her in her bakery fleece, not smiling at all.

At one-thirty Wendy tapped the microphone.

"Thank you all so much for coming," she said. "Sixty years ago, my mother and father opened these doors with one oven and a lot of faith. Today my sister and I are proud to carry that legacy forward. Some of you may have heard there have been some changes. I want you to know Dellucci's is in strong hands, family hands, and we're excited about the future."

Some polite clapping.

Then Sal Romano came walking up Michigan Avenue from the direction of the dry cleaner, in his tie, carrying a white bakery box with a red string on it. Behind him, in his cardigan, carrying a manila folder, was Marco Abate.

The crowd parted for Sal like he was the mayor. Half of them knew him. All of them knew the photograph.

"Sal Romano!" somebody yelled. "Duck!"

Big laugh. Sal waved like a politician.

He walked right up under the tent and set the white box on the table next to the four-foot cake.

"Happy sixtieth," he said to Wendy. "From one partner to another. Cannoli. My recipe. Or your grandmother's. Depending who you ask."

Wendy didn't touch the box. "Sal. This isn't the time."

"No, it's a very good time." He turned around to the crowd. "Folks, I'm Sal Romano, Romano's Bakery on the east side, as of Monday I'm a forty-five-percent owner of this place. Bought it fair from the man who ran it. That fella in the back by the parking meter." He pointed. Two hundred heads turned and looked at me. "Joe Dellucci and me didn't talk for thirty-eight years. But I'll tell you something. He built a good bakery. And I'm not going to let nobody rob it."

The polka band stopped mid-song. The accordion player let out a long sad wheeze.

"Sal," Wendy said, very low, through her teeth. "Please."

Marco Abate stepped up beside Sal. He's not a man who likes crowds. His hands were shaking, holding the folder. But he cleared his throat and spoke loud enough for the first two rows.

"I've kept these books since 1994," he said. "Joe hired me. On Monday, Mrs. Burgess called me at midnight and asked me how to shred register tapes. I didn't shred them. I added them up." He opened the folder. "For twenty-six months, the register tapes say this bakery took in one hundred and forty-one thousand, six hundred dollars more in cash than was ever deposited in the bank. The deposits were made by one person." He closed the folder. "I'm sorry, Fran. I wanted you to hear it from me and not from an accountant."

Nobody said anything. Two hundred people stood on Michigan Avenue in the October sun with cookies in their hands and said absolutely nothing.

Fran turned to her sister. Very slowly.

"A hundred and forty-one thousand dollars," Fran said.

"Fran, it's not what it—"

"Garth's truck," Fran said. "That's Garth's truck. That's his condo deposit and his truck and that ring you've been hiding in your purse since August."

"It was a loan! It was going to come back when the sale went through!"

"From Daddy's registers?"

"From the business! It's our business!"

"It's forty-five percent Sal Romano's business now," Fran said, and her voice cracked and went up into a shout, a real Dellucci shout, the kind I'd heard their father use on bread delivery men who were late. "Because you couldn't read a letter! Because you fired the only man who ever counted anything in this family, on a speakerphone, to make your high school boyfriend a general manager!"

"Don't you dare yell at me in front of—"

"In front of who? The town? Wendy, the town is right here!"

Wendy shoved her. Not hard, but she did, two hands on her little sister's shoulders, under the tent, in front of the cake and the reporter and two hundred people. Fran stumbled back into the table. The white box of cannoli went over. One cannoli rolled off the edge of the table and landed at Sal Romano's feet.

Sal looked down at it. He looked at Wendy. And then, God help me, he started to laugh.

"Joe," he said, wiping his eyes. "Joe, they're throwing cannoli again."

The State Journal reporter took a picture. I found out later it ran on the front of the Sunday Local section.

Wendy came down off the tent through the crowd, and people got out of her way. She came all the way back to the parking meters in her red dress and grabbed my arm with both hands. Her mascara was running. She smelled like powdered sugar and wine.

"Elliot," she said. "Elliot, please. Undo it. Call Sal, buy it back, I'll pay you anything, I'll give you the house. I'll tell Fran it was me. I'll tell everybody. Just undo it, please, before Monday. Before the accountant. Please."

"I can't undo it, Wendy."

"You can. You can do anything. You ran the whole place."

"I ran it for twenty-two years. You fired me with a voicemail."

"I was wrong. I was stupid. Garth said—"

"Garth's gone."

She stopped. Her hands loosened on my arm.

"He left Tuesday," she said. "He took the truck."

"I know. You told me in the voicemail."

"You listened to them?"

"All seven," I said. "Twenty-nine calls. I listened to every one."

"Then why didn't you call me back?"

I gently took her hands off my arm and held them for a second, the way you hold something you used to carry every day.

"Because you told me I was out of the family business," I said. "And you told me to expect a call from your lawyer."

I let go of her hands. Behind her, under the tent, Fran was picking cannoli up off the sidewalk one at a time, and Sal Romano was helping her.

"Understood," I said, and I walked back to my truck.
