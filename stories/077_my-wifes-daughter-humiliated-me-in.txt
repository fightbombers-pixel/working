She'd found a room for spring in a house off Moro Street with Kayla and two other girls, three hundred and ten a month, no guarantor needed. She told me all of it on her first day at the parts counter, reading off a sticky note, like she was giving me an inspection report.

I was at the griddle in an apron that said KISS THE COOK, which Gus thought was the funniest thing he'd ever seen. Madison was on the syrup station in a dealership polo shirt with her name embroidered on it. She'd started in parts the Monday after Thanksgiving. Gus said she was the best counter help he'd had in six years, and that she'd already caught a wrong part number on an order he'd written himself.

At about nine thirty, the shop door opened and Cassandra came in.

She was dressed up. Good coat, her hair done, lipstick. She stood in the doorway for a moment looking at two hundred people eating pancakes, and I could see her working out the room. She found me at the griddle. Then she found Madison at the syrup table, and something in her face slipped.

She walked straight through the tables to me. People noticed. You couldn't not notice.

"Sam," she said. "Can we talk?"

"I'm making pancakes, Cass."

"Then I'll talk while you make pancakes."

I flipped three. "Okay."

"I got the papers."

"I know."

"Sam, please. This has gone too far. I know I said something awful. I know I did. But you can't throw away twenty years over one sentence."

"It wasn't one sentence. It was twenty years of the same one. You just finally said it out loud."

Earl Whitlock was at the nearest table with his wife. He put his fork down. So did the people next to him.

"I'll go to counseling," Cassandra said. "Whatever you want. We'll go to Father Mike. We'll do the whole thing."

"No."

"I'll tell everybody at church I was wrong. I'll stand up and say it."

"You don't need to do that for me."

"Then what do you want?" Her voice got higher. People three tables away were listening now. "What do you want from me? You want me to beg? In front of all these people? Fine! I'm begging! Come home, Sam!"

I set the spatula down.

"Cass," I said. "You told me in your mother's kitchen I'm not her father. You told me not to demand respect. You told her I didn't want to adopt her. You let her thank Lonnie for my paychecks for four years. I didn't demand anything. I just stopped paying for your story."

She stared at me. Her chin was shaking again.

"You turned her against me," she whispered.

"I've seen her for one limeade and a job interview," I said. "You did the rest yourself."

"Madison," Cassandra said, turning toward the syrup station. "Madison, honey, tell him. Tell him we're a family."

Madison set down the syrup pitcher. She walked over slowly, wiping her hands on a paper towel, and stood next to the griddle. Next to me. Two hundred people were watching now. Even the Ford dealer.

"Mom," she said. "You told him at Grandma's party that he's not my father."

"I didn't mean..."

"You were right," Madison said. "He's not."

Cassandra's face lit up, just for a second, like she'd won. She always won.

Then Madison reached into the back pocket of her jeans and took out four sheets of yellowed paper folded in thirds, and opened them to page four, and held the empty line up where her mother could see it.

"He's my dad," Madison said. "You just never signed."

Cassandra looked at the empty line for a long time. Then she turned around and walked out through two hundred people, and nobody said a word, and the shop door banged shut behind her.

Madison folded the papers back up and put them in her pocket. She looked at me. Her eyes were wet and she was trying very hard to look like a parts counter professional.

"Is that pancake burning?" she said.

I looked down. It was.

"Get back to the syrup," I said. "The Ford guy's going to tell everybody we burn our pancakes."
