"I think we married too young, Myron."

My wife said it at the welcome-home party for her high school sweetheart, at the Prairie Wind Country Club in Cheyenne, with about sixty people in the room and at least nine of them close enough to hear every word. She said it while holding a glass of champagne he'd paid for, standing about a foot from him, and she didn't lower her voice one bit.

I was holding a paper plate with two deviled eggs on it. I remember that because I was thinking how dumb a man looks holding deviled eggs while his wife ends a marriage. Thirty-nine years. We'd been married thirty-nine years. If that was too young, I'd hate to see what she called just right.

I set the plate down on the bar. Then I took off the plain gold ring her father had given me the night before our wedding and laid it next to the eggs.

"Maybe we did," I said.

Then I picked up one of the eggs, ate it, and nodded to the bartender like we'd just settled a bet.

Forty minutes later, the richest man in that room was standing in the parking lot unable to tell me what his own railroad car weighed, and his hand was shaking when he lit his cigarette.

What she didn't know was what was stenciled on the side of the railcar in the glossy brochure sitting on every table in that ballroom.

My name is Myron Garrett. I'm 62, and I spent thirty-eight years as a freight conductor out of Cheyenne, Wyoming, before I retired. People think a conductor just rides along in the back and waves at kids. A conductor's real job is knowing every car on that train by its number, what's inside it, what it weighs, and who's paying for the freight. Hold on to that.

The man of the hour was Monty Crane. Patsy and Monty went steady all through Cheyenne East, class of 1982, until he left for Arizona the summer after graduation and stopped writing by Christmas. I married her four years later. In thirty-nine years she brought him up maybe five times, always with a little smile, like he was a song she used to know the words to.

Now he was back. He'd made his money in Phoenix, he said, in land and luxury travel, and he'd come home to give something back to the old town. That's what the banner over the bar said, in fact. Welcome Home Monty. Giving Back To Cheyenne. He was 62 like us, with white teeth, a tan you can't get in Wyoming in May, and a camel-colored blazer that probably cost more than my first car.

He was also standing right there when Patsy said it. He heard every word. And he smiled at me like a man who's just been handed the winning ticket and is too polite to cash it in front of you.

"Myron," he said. "No hard feelings, I hope. These things happen."

"They sure do."

"Patsy and I, we've got history."

"I heard."

Patsy picked up the ring off the bar. She looked at it like she didn't know whose it was.

"You don't have to make a scene," she said.

"I ate an egg, Patsy. That's the whole scene."

A couple of people at the next table laughed and then pretended they were coughing. Patsy's face went pink under her makeup. She put the ring in her clutch purse and snapped it shut.

"We'll talk at home," she said.

"Sure," I said. "Whenever you're ready."

I went outside to get some air. That's the truth. I wasn't storming out, I wasn't making a statement, I just needed to stand somewhere that didn't smell like perfume and catered shrimp. I leaned on the tailgate of my truck and looked at the brochure I'd picked up off our table without thinking.

Frontier Rail Excursions. Luxury Rail Travel Returns To The High Plains. A glossy cover with a sunset and a long, gleaming train of vintage passenger cars in green and gold. Inside, there was a page that said Founding Investors: Own A Piece Of History, and a minimum buy-in of $50,000. On the back page there was a big color photo of a restored dining car, all brass and polished wood, with a number stenciled on the side.

4410.

I looked at that number for a long time.

"Beautiful, isn't she?"

Monty had come out the side door. He had his blazer off now and a cigarette in his hand, the kind of man who quit smoking in public and still does it in parking lots.

"She's something," I said.

"Patsy tells me you rode the rails for forty years."

"Thirty-eight."

"Then you know quality." He lit his cigarette. "I'm putting together the founding group this summer. First excursion runs the Saturday before Thanksgiving, Cheyenne to the Colorado line and back, champagne the whole way. I'd love to have a real railroad man on board. It'd look good for the investors."

"Would it."

"Fifty thousand gets you in. Your pension can handle that, right? Patsy says you've got railroad money just sitting there."

Patsy says. I'd been hearing that phrase a lot that night.

"Monty," I said. "What's a car like this weigh?"

"What?"

"The dining car. Number 4410. What's she weigh, empty?"

He looked at me, then at the brochure, then at his cigarette. And for about a second and a half, the smile slipped off his face like a bad decal.

"I've got people who handle that end," he said. "Engineers. Specialists."

"Sure you do."

"Why do you ask?"

"Just a railroad man's question," I said. "Have a good party, Monty."

He stood there and smoked, and I could see his hand wasn't quite steady. Then the side door opened and Patsy's voice came out, high and bright, calling his name like she was seventeen again, and he flicked the cigarette into the gravel and went back inside.

I drove home alone.

Two days later, on Monday morning, Patsy put the divorce papers on the kitchen table next to my coffee.

She'd had them drawn up by a lawyer in Fort Collins that Monty recommended, which told you everything about how long she'd been planning this. You don't get forty pages typed up over a weekend. She sat down across from me in her robe, with her reading glasses on her nose, and folded her hands like a woman about to pitch a timeshare.

"I want to do this simply," she said. "No fighting. No lawyers screaming at each other. We're adults."

"Okay."

"I want my half of the house in cash. You can keep the house, I know you love it, so you can refinance and pay me out. My half comes to about three hundred and ten thousand."

"Okay."

"And I'll take the Buick, and my half of the savings."

"Okay."

She stopped. She looked at me over her glasses.

"Okay?"

"You said you wanted it simple."

"I did. I just thought..." She took off the glasses. "Thirty-nine years, Myron. I thought you'd at least ask me why."

"You told me why. At the party. In front of the Hendersons and the bartender."

"I said we married too young."

"I heard you."

"That's all? You don't want to know about Monty?"

"I know about Monty. Everybody in that ballroom knows about Monty. There was a banner."

She went red. Patsy has a habit of keeping score, of remembering exactly who paid for what and bringing it up months later, so I knew what was coming next before she said it.

"Do you know how many anniversaries I've spent at the Outback because you had a coupon?" she said. "Do you know how many vacations we didn't take because you were on call? I gave you my whole life, Myron. I gave you my thirties and my forties, and I'm not giving you my sixties."

"That's fair."

"Stop saying things are fair!"

"Would you like me to say they're unfair?"

"I'd like you to act like you care!"

I put my coffee down. I looked at her, really looked, the way you look at a car coupling before you trust it at forty miles an hour.

"Patsy, I cared for thirty-nine years," I said. "You just told me in front of sixty people that you're done with it. I'm not going to beg you to stay with a man you're embarrassed of. I've got a little more pride than that, and you've got a little more class than to want me to."

She opened her mouth and closed it.

I flipped through the papers. Most of it was what she'd said. On page twenty-two there was a paragraph about my railroad retirement, and how she'd be entitled to a share of it under a court order. I read it twice.

"One change," I said.

"What?"

"You take the cash for the house, the Buick, the savings, the furniture, the good china. All of it. I keep my pension whole. You waive your share."

"Why would I do that?"

"Because you'll walk out of here with three hundred and ten thousand dollars cash in about sixty days, instead of a little check every month for the rest of your life. And because Monty's got big plans, and big plans need cash, not a monthly check from the railroad."

She looked at me like I'd said something in Russian. Then she looked at the paper. I could almost see the numbers going behind her eyes.

"Monty says the founding investors double their money in two years," she said.

"Then you'll have six hundred thousand by the time you're 63."

"You're being sarcastic."

"I'm being agreeable. It's a different thing."

She called the Fort Collins lawyer from the bedroom. I heard her through the door. "He wants to keep his pension. Yes. No, he's giving me everything else. No, I don't know why. I think he's in shock." Then a pause, and her voice dropping. "Monty, it's me. He's just signing it. He's just going to sign it."

The lawyer sent the change over by email that afternoon. Before I signed anything, I drove a copy downtown to Margo Shields, the lawyer who did our wills, a short gray-haired woman who reads contracts the way some people read the sports page. She went through it in twenty minutes.

"It's a bad deal for her and a fine one for you," she said. "Sign it before somebody explains that to her."

So I went home and printed it on the old inkjet in the den, which took about twenty minutes because the thing is older than our daughter. Then I sat down at the kitchen table, took the cap off a blue pen from the Union Pacific credit union, and signed every place there was a little yellow sticker.

Patsy watched me from the doorway with her arms crossed.

"You didn't even read it all," she said.

"I read the parts with numbers."

"You're really not going to fight."

"Patsy," I said, signing the last page, "you can't fight a train that's already left the yard. All you can do is get off the tracks."

I slid the papers across the table to her. She picked them up and held them to her chest. For one second, she looked like she might cry, and then she didn't.

"I'm going to stay at Monty's this week," she said.

"I figured."

She packed two suitcases. I carried them out to the Buick for her because thirty-nine years is a long habit, and she let me, and neither of us said anything about it.

That evening, I went over to Amos Riggs's place.

Amos was a brakeman on my crew for nineteen years. He's 64, built like a mailbox, and he has a laugh you can hear from the end of the block. He lives in a little brick house on the north side with a detached garage that's nicer than the house, and when I pulled in, he was sanding a rocking chair for his grandson with the radio playing the Eagles.

He saw my bare left hand on the steering wheel and turned the radio off.

"Where's Ed's ring?"

"In Patsy's purse."

"And Patsy?"

"At Monty Crane's."

Amos went to the old refrigerator in the corner, came back with two cans of Coors and handed me one.

"Start at the deviled eggs," he said.

So I told him. The party, the line, the eggs, the brochure, the parking lot, the papers, the pension. Amos didn't interrupt once, which for Amos is like a dog not barking at the mailman.

When I was done, he took a long drink.

"Maybe we did," he said. "That's what you said? Right there in front of the whole club?"

"That's what I said."

"And then you ate the egg."

"It was a good egg."

He started laughing. He laughed so hard he had to sit down in the rocking chair he'd just sanded and got sawdust all over his jeans, and that made him laugh harder. I'll admit I laughed too. It came up out of me like a cough and I couldn't stop it. Two old railroad men laughing in a garage while the sun went down over Cheyenne.

When we ran out, Amos wiped his eyes and got serious.

"Okay. Why'd you give her the house money? You could've fought for that pension split and kept half the cash."

"Because she's going to give it to him."

"To Monty?"

"Every dime. You watch. Founding investor."

"So why hand it to her?"

I pulled the brochure out of my jacket and opened it to the back page, to the dining car in green and gold, and put my finger on the number.

"Amos. Read me that car number."

He squinted. "Forty-four ten." Then he stopped. He took the brochure out of my hand and held it up to the shop light. "Forty-four ten. Myron. That's not..."

"It is."

"We took that car to Denver."

"We did."

"That car's a scrap pile."

"Since 2009," I said. "And the man's selling tickets on it."

Amos looked at the brochure for a long time. Then he looked at me.

"You going to tell her?"

"Once," I said. "I'll tell her once."

I told her nine days later.

She came by the house on a Wednesday afternoon to pick up the good china, the set with the little blue wheat pattern her mother left her, and she brought Monty's nephew to carry the boxes. The nephew was a big quiet kid of about twenty who loaded the Buick and then sat in it with the air conditioning running and his phone in his face.

Patsy stood in the kitchen wrapping teacups in newspaper. She'd had her hair done. She had a new pair of sunglasses pushed up on her head the way Monty wore his, and she smelled like a perfume I didn't know.

"You look good," I said, because she did.

"Monty took me to Denver. Shopping." She wrapped a saucer. "He says I've been dressing like a conductor's wife for forty years."

"You were a conductor's wife for forty years."

"Thirty-nine."

"Thirty-nine," I said. "Patsy, I want to show you something. Then I'll never bring it up again."

She sighed like I was a kid asking for one more story before bed. "If this is about Monty..."

"It's about a railcar."

I put two things on the kitchen table. The first was the brochure, open to the back page, the dining car in green and gold. The second was a little brown notebook with a rubber band around it. Every conductor I ever worked with kept one. You write down your train, your cars, your setouts, anything odd. I've got thirty-eight years of them in a footlocker in the basement.

I opened this one to a page from March of 2009 and turned it so she could read my handwriting.

"Car 4410," I said. "Old passenger diner, retired, sitting on a siding in Laramie for six years with the roof caving in. On March 14th, 2009, my crew pulled her to a scrap dealer in Commerce City, Colorado. Amos Riggs was my brakeman. They had her cut up by the end of the month. I watched them start."

She looked at the notebook. Then at the brochure.

"So he used an old picture," she said. "Big deal. Everybody uses old pictures."

"That isn't an old picture. Look at the windows. Look at the brass. That car never looked like that in her life. Somebody painted a number from a railroad list onto a picture of something else."

"Or you're wrong."

"I could be wrong about a lot of things, Patsy. Not about a car I took to the scrapyard."

She folded her arms. "Do you know what Monty said you'd do? He said, 'Watch, Myron's going to try to poison this. He's jealous and he's bitter and he's going to find something to pick at.' He said it word for word. And here you are."

"Here I am."

"With your little notebook."

"With my little notebook."

"You couldn't stand it, could you? That somebody finally wants to give me something. That somebody's going to make something of himself in this town besides a man who sat in a caboose for forty years."

"They took the cabooses off in the eighties, Patsy."

"Oh, my God!" She grabbed the brochure off the table, crumpled it in her fist and threw it in the kitchen trash can by the back door. "That is exactly what I'm talking about. That right there. Thirty-nine years of that."

"Okay."

"Don't okay me."

"Then I'll say it plain, one time. Don't give that man your house money. That's all. That's the whole speech."

She picked up the last box of teacups and stood there holding it, breathing hard.

"You don't get a vote anymore," she said. "You signed."

"I know. That's why I'm only saying it once."

She walked out. The Buick pulled away with the nephew driving and Patsy in the passenger seat, not looking back. I stood at the sink for a while and washed the one coffee cup she'd missed, a lot longer than one coffee cup needs.

Then I went to the trash can, fished out the brochure, smoothed it flat on the counter and put it in the drawer with the takeout menus.

The phone rang about an hour later. The house phone, the one on the kitchen wall that nobody but our dentist and Patsy's friends ever called.

"Myron? It's Darcy. Darcy Hughes."

Darcy has been Patsy's tennis partner for twenty years. She's 60, short and freckled, with a voice that carries across three courts, and in all that time she's called our house maybe a thousand times, always for Patsy. She'd never once asked for me.

"Hi, Darcy."

"Is she there?"

"She just left with the china."

"Good. Then I can say this." She took a breath. "I've been sick about it since the party. I should've told you in February."

"February?"

"That's when it started. There was a Facebook group for the class of '82, for the forty-year reunion that never happened, and Monty joined it, and the first person he messaged was Patsy. By March they were talking every night. She'd go sit in her car in the driveway so you wouldn't hear."

I thought about that. All spring Patsy had been going out to the car after supper. She said she liked to listen to her audiobooks in there because the kitchen echoed.

"She knew he was moving back," Darcy said. "She knew about the party. She practiced what she was going to say to you, Myron. She practiced it on us in the locker room. 'I think we married too young.' She asked if it sounded too mean."

"What'd you all tell her?"

"We told her it sounded honest." Darcy's voice cracked a little. "We laughed. I laughed. And then I watched you set that ring down and eat that egg and I wanted to crawl under the table."

"You don't owe me anything, Darcy."

"Yes, I do. Listen. My cousin Rita lives in Scottsdale. I asked her about Monty Crane, just to see. She says he had a company out there, some kind of luxury desert resort, and a lot of retired people put money in, and the resort never got built. She says there were lawsuits. She says he left Arizona about fifteen minutes before people started looking for him."

"Did you tell Patsy?"

"I tried. Last Thursday, after doubles. She said Rita's always been a liar and she said I was jealous." Darcy laughed, but it wasn't a happy one. "Jealous. Everybody's jealous of Patsy this week."

"She told me the same thing."

"What are you going to do?"

"Nothing," I said. "I told her once. She's a grown woman. And it's not my money."

"But it's everybody's money! Half the club's talking about writing checks. My husband wanted to. I had to hide the checkbook."

I didn't have an answer for that. I thanked her and hung up, and I stood in the kitchen for a minute looking at that drawer with the brochure in it.

The phone rang again before I'd even sat down.

"Myron Garrett?"

It was a voice like a gravel road. I knew it right away.

"Walt."

"I need to buy you a cup of coffee," Walt Kimball said. "Tomorrow, seven a.m., the diner downtown. I've got a question, and you're the only man in this town I trust to answer it straight."

"What's the question?"

"Whether I should put two hundred thousand dollars into Monty Crane's train."

Walt Kimball is 78. He ran cattle south of town for fifty years, he's been president of the club since before most of its members were born, and he knew Patsy's father, Ed, from the time they were boys. He's a big man gone thin with age, and he wears a pearl-snap shirt and a bolo tie to breakfast like it's a board meeting, which for Walt it usually is.

The diner downtown is an old trolley car with a counter and eight booths, and we took the back one. Walt ordered black coffee and dry wheat toast. I ordered eggs, and when they came I thought about the party and almost laughed.

"Something funny?"

"Just eggs, Walt."

"Huh." He sipped his coffee. "Crane came to see me last week. Sat in my living room for two hours. He wants me to be his anchor investor. Says when people see Walt Kimball's name on the list, they'll line up. He's offering me a seat on his board and a private car on the first run, the Saturday before Thanksgiving."

"Generous."

"That's what worried me." He set his cup down. "I've been around long enough to know a man who smiles that much is selling something. But my granddaughter says I'm an old fossil who doesn't trust anything new. So I'm asking you. You rode those rails for forty years."

"Thirty-eight."

"Don't be modest at my table. Is this thing real?"

I didn't answer right away. I want to be honest about this part. I'd told myself I wasn't going to run around town talking about Monty Crane, because that's what a bitter ex-husband does, and I wasn't going to be that man. But Walt had asked me straight, and I'd been raised to answer a straight question with a straight answer.

So I took the brochure out of my jacket, the one I'd fished out of the trash, and put it on the table. And next to it, my notebook from 2009.

"I'll tell you three things I know for a fact," I said. "Then you decide."

"Go."

"One. The dining car on the back of this brochure, number 4410, was cut up for scrap in Commerce City in 2009. I took it there myself. It's in my notebook, and Amos Riggs will tell you the same."

Walt picked up the notebook and read it, holding it at arm's length.

"Two. A private excursion train can't just roll out onto the main line on the busiest freight week of the year. You need an operating agreement with the railroad, insurance, a qualified crew, inspections. That takes a year or more. I've got friends still working in the dispatch office. Not one of them has ever heard the name Frontier Rail Excursions."

"And three?"

"Three. The brochure says the first run leaves from the old depot downtown. You sit on the depot foundation board, Walt. Has anybody signed a lease?"

Walt looked at me for a long moment. Then he took out a little flip phone, held it about a foot from his face, and dialed a number with one thick finger.

"Carol? Walt. Has anybody signed a lease for an excursion train out of the depot? Crane. Monty Crane." He listened. "Nobody's even asked? Not once? Thank you, Carol."

He put the phone away.

"Nobody's asked," he said.

"Huh," I said.

"Patsy put money in?"

"She's about to. Her half of the house. Three hundred and ten thousand."

Walt took a long breath through his nose. "Ed Lowery would roll over in his grave."

"Ed would've told her once. Then he'd have let her make up her own mind. He always said that about Patsy. Tell her once, Myron, and then step back, because she'll do the opposite of whatever you say twice."

Walt almost smiled. "That sounds like Ed."

He folded the brochure and put it in his shirt pocket, behind the pearl snaps.

"I'm not going to the sheriff," he said. "The sheriff's a nice boy, but he couldn't find fraud in a bank robbery. I'm going to call the securities division over at the Secretary of State's office. I've known the woman who runs it since she was in 4-H. I'll give her your name, if that's all right with you."

"It's all right."

"Good." He put a twenty on the table for a nine-dollar breakfast and stood up. "You did right at that party, son. I was there. Most men would've made a fool of themselves. You made a fool of nobody but him, and he doesn't even know it yet."

"He will."

"Oh, he will," Walt said. "And soon. A man like Crane hears everything. By tonight he'll know I asked about that lease."

He was right. I was out in the front yard that evening, pulling dandelions out of the strip by the sidewalk, when a white Cadillac Escalade with Arizona plates came down the street a lot faster than our street likes, and stopped crooked at my curb.

Monty Crane got out and came up my walk without his blazer, and without his smile.

"What did you tell Walt Kimball?"

I stood up slowly. My knees are 62.

"Evening, Monty."

"Don't 'evening' me. Walt Kimball pulled out. He called me at four o'clock and said he's not investing, and then the Hendersons called and said they want to wait, and then Gene Abernathy called and said he's going to 'think about it.' Everybody's thinking about it. So I'm asking you. What did you tell him?"

"He asked me if your train was real."

"And?"

"And I told him what I know."

"What you know." Monty laughed, but it came out too high. "What you know is how to ride in the back of a freight train. You don't know the first thing about luxury travel."

"No," I said. "I don't. But I know car 4410."

That stopped him. Just for a second, but it stopped him.

"That's a stock photo," he said. "My marketing people picked it."

"Your marketing people painted a scrapped car's number on somebody else's dining car."

"That's a typo."

"That's a pretty specific typo."

"You know what this is?" He pointed a finger at me. He had a big gold watch on that wrist, and it slid down his arm when he pointed. "This is defamation. You go around town spreading lies to hurt my business because I took your wife, and I'll sue you for every dime of that precious pension."

"I didn't go around town. Walt came to me."

"Same thing!"

"No, it isn't. A man asked me a question, and I answered it. If he hadn't asked, I'd have kept pulling dandelions."

"You're bitter."

"I'm sixty-two. My knees are bitter. The rest of me's fine."

He came up two steps. He was close enough that I could smell the cologne, and under it, cigarettes.

"She chose me, Myron. Not you. She stood up in front of the whole club and told you so. You were never enough for her, and you know it."

"Maybe I wasn't," I said. "But she was enough for me for thirty-nine years. That's the difference between you and me, Monty. I don't need her money to want her around."

He went red from the collar up. For a second, I thought he might take a swing at me, and I made sure my hands were where he could see them, open and down at my sides, with the neighbor across the street watching from her porch with her watering can frozen in midair.

He didn't swing. He stepped back down the steps.

"You think you're so smart," he said. "Here's something for you to chew on. Patsy's wiring me her money the day your divorce is final. Every penny. She's going to be a founding partner. Vice president. Her name's going on the company papers. And when that train pulls out of the depot the Saturday before Thanksgiving with her on it, drinking champagne, you're going to be standing on that platform watching it go."

"I'll be there," I said.

He got back in his Escalade and left a little rubber on my street.

The divorce was final on July 18th. I know because Patsy texted me a little after four that afternoon, which was the first time she'd texted me in seven weeks. It was a photo of a bank receipt. A wire transfer of $310,000, to Frontier Rail Excursions LLC.

Under it, she'd written: "Now you can stop being jealous."

I didn't answer that text. I put the phone face down on the counter and went out to the garage and changed the oil in the truck, which didn't need changing.

The summer went by the way summers do in Cheyenne, hot and windy and short. I fixed the back fence. I went fishing at Curt Gowdy with Amos twice and caught nothing both times, which Amos said was a talent. I learned to make my mother's green chili from a recipe card in her handwriting, and it came out too salty, and I ate it anyway for four days. People at the hardware store stopped giving me the sad look by August.

Patsy's face was all over Facebook that fall. Darcy showed me some of it on her phone when we ran into each other at the grocery store, not to gossip, she said, just so I'd know. Patsy in a hard hat at the depot, smiling. Patsy and Monty at a fancy steakhouse in Denver, toasting. Patsy in a white wool coat with a gold name tag pinned to it that said Frontier Rail Excursions, Vice President.

"She's selling it now," Darcy said. "She's the one calling people. She called my husband three times."

"Did he buy?"

"No, because I hid the checkbook in the freezer." Darcy put her phone away. "She says the train's coming up from Texas the week of the first run. She says it's already on the way."

"Does she."

"Myron. Is any of it real?"

"Ask Walt," I said.

On the Saturday before Thanksgiving, six months and two days after I signed those papers at my kitchen table, I stood on the platform of the old depot downtown with my hands in my coat pockets, and I watched Monty Crane's train not arrive.

Walt had asked me to come. He'd called the night before and said, "Be there at ten. Stand near me. Don't say a word unless somebody asks you a question." So I was there, next to Walt in his good sheepskin coat, at the back of a crowd of about ninety people in their Sunday best, holding little glasses of champagne in a wind that kept trying to steal them.

There was a podium with a banner on it. Frontier Rail Excursions, All Aboard For History. There was a string quartet from the college, shivering. There was a table of little sandwiches with the crusts cut off. And there was a set of empty tracks running off east toward Nebraska, shining in the cold sun.

Monty was at the podium in a long black overcoat and his sunglasses, even though it was November. Patsy stood a step behind him in the white wool coat with the gold name tag, holding a clipboard and smiling so hard it looked like it hurt.

"Folks, folks," Monty said into the microphone. "Just a little delay coming up from Texas. You know how the railroad is. They don't care about anybody's schedule but their own. Enjoy the champagne. She'll be here any minute."

A few people laughed. Walt didn't.

"He's been saying any minute for forty minutes," Walt said quietly.

"There's no train coming, Walt."

"I know, son. I'm just watching him find out that everybody else knows it too."

A crowd that's put money in is a funny animal. They're polite for about half an hour. After that, they start looking at their watches, and then at each other, and then at the man at the podium, and the champagne doesn't help. I watched a man in a cowboy hat walk up to Patsy and say something, and I watched her flip through her clipboard and say something back with that big smile, and I watched the man not smile back.

Then a woman in a navy blue parka came up the platform from the parking lot. She was in her fifties, with short gray hair and a leather folder under her arm, and behind her were two Cheyenne police officers in uniform.

Walt took his hands out of his pockets.

"That's her," he said. "The 4-H girl."

The woman walked straight through the crowd to the podium. People got out of her way without being asked. She held up an ID badge and said something to Monty I couldn't hear, and then she leaned past him to the microphone, because the microphone was the only way anybody would hear anything in that wind.

"Good morning. I'm with the securities division of the Wyoming Secretary of State's office. Mr. Crane, there is a warrant for your arrest for securities fraud and theft by deception. These officers are going to take you into custody now."

The string quartet stopped in the middle of a note.

Monty didn't move. Then he laughed into the microphone, a big hearty laugh like somebody'd told a joke at a banquet.

"This is a misunderstanding. Folks, this is a paperwork misunderstanding. I have attorneys..."

"You can call them from the station, sir," one of the officers said. "Turn around, please. Hands behind your back."

"There's a train coming!"

"No, sir," the woman from the state said. "There isn't. There's no train in Texas with your name on it. There's no operating agreement with the railroad. There's no lease on this depot. And the dining car in your brochure was scrapped in 2009."

A sound went through the crowd. Not a gasp exactly, more like ninety people breathing out at the same time.

The officer took Monty's arm and turned him. The sunglasses fell off his head and clattered on the podium. He looked out over the crowd while they put the cuffs on, and he found me at the back, next to Walt, and his face went tight and ugly.

"You," he said. "You did this."

Every head on that platform turned around to look at me. I didn't step forward. I just answered him, because he'd asked.

"No, Monty. You did this. I just knew the car."

They walked him down the platform toward the parking lot. He kept talking the whole way, about his attorneys and his rights and how they'd all be sorry, and his voice got smaller and smaller until a car door shut on it.

Then it was quiet, except for the wind and a champagne glass rolling across the concrete.

The woman from the state turned to the podium.

"Mrs. Garrett?"

Patsy was still standing there with her clipboard. She hadn't moved since the cuffs came out.

"It's... it's Lowery now," she said. "I'm going back to Lowery."

"Ma'am, you're listed as an officer of Frontier Rail Excursions. I'm going to need you to come to our office Monday morning at nine and answer some questions. You may want to bring an attorney."

"I didn't... I don't... I'm just the vice president. I don't know anything about the money. Monty handles the money."

"Then that's what you'll tell us on Monday, ma'am."

The crowd was starting to break up now, the way a crowd does after a fire drill, slowly and then all at once. The man in the cowboy hat walked past Patsy and said something to her, short and hard, and she flinched like he'd thrown his drink. Somebody else said, "Where's my fifty thousand, Patsy?" Somebody else said her name like a swear word.

She looked around for somebody, anybody, and her eyes landed on me.

"Myron," she said.

I put my hands back in my pockets. Walt touched my elbow.

"Let's go get some breakfast," he said. "I'm buying. Again."

My phone started ringing before Walt and I even reached the diner.

I let it ring. I sat in the back booth with Walt and ate a plate of eggs, and I set the phone face up on the table between the ketchup and the napkins, and every time it lit up with her name I looked at it.

Three missed calls by the time the eggs came.

Five by the time Walt paid.

"You going to answer that?" Walt asked.

"Not here."

"Good man."

By the time I got home, it was eleven missed calls and four voicemails. I listened to the first one standing in my kitchen.

"Myron, please call me back. Please. They froze the company account. They froze my account. They're saying all the money's gone. Myron, my money's gone. Three hundred and ten thousand dollars. He paid off people in Arizona with it. Please call me back."

The second one was crying, mostly. The third one said, "I know you're there."

The fourth one said, "I need your help. You're the only one who knows about the railroad. You have to tell them I didn't know. Please. I'll do anything."

I didn't call back. I sat at the kitchen table and drank a cup of coffee in my own kitchen and listened to the furnace kick on.

A little after seven, somebody knocked on the front door.

I knew the knock. Thirty-nine years will teach you a knock.

When I opened the door, Patsy was standing on the porch in the white wool coat. The gold name tag was gone, but you could still see the two little holes in the wool where it had been pinned. Her mascara had run and dried and run again. She had her purse in both hands, held in front of her like the deviled egg plate I'd been holding at the party six months ago.

"Can I come in?" she said.

"You can say what you need to say from there."

She looked down at the welcome mat. It was the one she'd bought. It said The Garretts in curly letters.

"They're saying I'm in on it," she said. "The lady from the state. She said officers of the company can be charged too. I signed things, Myron. He'd put papers in front of me and I'd sign them because he said it was just paperwork. I didn't read them."

"You never did like the long ones."

"Please don't." Her chin shook. "I have nothing. The Buick's in the company's name now, because he said it was a tax thing. Darcy won't pick up. The club called and said my membership's suspended. I have one hundred and eighty dollars in my purse and I don't have anywhere to stay tonight."

"What do you need, Patsy?"

"I need you to tell them I didn't know. You're the railroad man. You're the one who found out. If you tell them I didn't know anything, they'll believe you."

"I can't tell them that."

"Why not?"

I reached back into the kitchen, opened the drawer with the takeout menus and took out the brochure. It was still wrinkled from where she'd crumpled it in her fist and thrown it in my trash can. I'd smoothed it flat, but you can't ever get all the creases out of paper.

I held it out to her.

"Because you did know," I said. "I told you. In that kitchen, on a Wednesday afternoon, with your mother's teacups on the table. I showed you the car number and my notebook, and you threw this in the trash and called me jealous."

She stared at the brochure. She didn't take it.

"If that lady from the state asks me," I said, "I'll tell her the truth. That you were warned once and you didn't believe me. That you believed him because he told you what you wanted to hear. That's not the same as being a crook, Patsy. I'll say that too. But I won't lie for you."

"So you won't help me."

"I didn't say that." I took a business card out of my shirt pocket and laid it on top of the brochure, and held them both out to her. "That's Margo Shields. She's good, and she's honest, and she'll take your call tonight because I already asked her to. That's the help."

Patsy took the card and the brochure. Her hands were shaking. She looked down at the dining car on the back page, number 4410 in gold on the green paint, and she started to cry again, quietly this time.

"I was wrong," she said. "About everything. About him. About us. Maybe we didn't marry too young. Maybe I was just too stupid to know what I had."

"Patsy."

"Can't I just come in? Just for tonight? We can talk about it. Thirty-nine years, Myron. Doesn't that count for something?"

I looked at her for a long time, standing there on the welcome mat with our old name on it.

"It counted for thirty-nine years," I said. "You told sixty people at the club it was too young."

"I know what I said."

"I know you do. I was holding the deviled eggs."

She laughed, one wet surprised laugh, and then it turned back into crying.

"Please," she said. "Please, Myron."

I took my coat off the hook by the door, the warm one, and put it around her shoulders over the white wool, because it was nineteen degrees out and she'd always been cold. Then I took my truck keys out of the bowl on the hall table.

"Get in the truck," I said. "I'll drive you to Darcy's. She'll open the door for you, because she's a better friend than you've been to her. And Monday morning, you call Margo, and you tell that lady from the state the truth, all of it, including the part where you threw this in my trash."

"And after that?"

I pulled the front door shut behind me and locked it, and I tapped the brochure in her hand, right on the car number.

"After that, Patsy, you're on your own train. I'm just the man who knows where it ends up."
