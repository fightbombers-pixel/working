"I'll be back Monday, girls trip! My phone will be off!"

I read my wife's text out loud on a Friday afternoon in a conference room on the fourth floor of a law office in downtown Youngstown, Ohio, to three people sitting around a table covered in paper.

Two of them were lawyers. The third was a woman I'd met exactly four times, and every one of those times we'd been talking about her husband.

"Girls trip," Nicole Durant said. "That's cute."

Twenty-six years of marriage, and Lori had never once in her life turned her phone off. Not at church, not at her own father's funeral, not on the operating table when she had her gallbladder out. She texted me from recovery.

I typed my answer with my thumb, slowly, because I wanted it to come out exactly right.

"Enjoy! Oh, and say hi to Steve. From his wife. We're spending the weekend with the lawyers."

I turned the phone around and showed it to the table. Then I hit send, and set the phone face down on a stack of bank statements, and picked up my coffee.

Nineteen hours later, Lori would be standing on a dock at Pymatuning Lake with her phone very much on, screaming into it so loud that a man in a bass boat forty yards out turned his motor off to listen.

What she didn't know was what the second stack of papers on that conference table was. A cash-out refinance package for our house on Glenwood Avenue, closing Monday at ten in the morning, with every line signed but one.

Mine.

My name is Alvin Sweeney. I'm 54, and I fix commercial refrigeration for a living. Walk-in coolers, freezer cases, the ice machine at the hospital cafeteria, the meat case at Giant Eagle. Thirty years of that teaches you one thing above everything else. When the temperature starts creeping, something's leaking somewhere, and it doesn't matter what the display says. You go find the leak. Hold on to that.

"She's going to read that and call you," said Frances Tolbert. She's my lawyer. She's sixty-one, wears cardigans with deep pockets, and talks like every word costs her a nickel.

"Her phone's off," I said.

"Her phone's never off," Nicole said. "Steve told me she sends him memes at three in the morning. I've seen them. Most of them have minions in them."

Across the table, Nicole's lawyer, a big gray-haired man named Ray Benedetto, laughed so hard he had to take his glasses off.

The phone buzzed under the bank statements. Not mine.

Nicole's.

She turned it over and looked at the screen, and for a second her face didn't do anything at all. Then she held it up so we could all see.

It said "Steve."

"He thinks I'm at my sister's in Erie this weekend," Nicole said. "Should I answer it?"

"Put it on speaker," Ray said.

Frances held up one hand. "Ohio is a one-party consent state," she said. "Either one of you can record your own phone calls. You don't need anybody's permission. But nobody says anything they'd be embarrassed to hear read back in court."

Nicole set the phone in the middle of the table and pressed the green button.

"Hey, babe," Steve Durant said. His voice was easy and warm, like a man calling from a hammock. "Just checking in. How's Erie?"

"Erie's fine," Nicole said. "How's the lake?"

There was a pause on the line. A long one. In the background, I heard a screen door slap shut and somebody laugh. A woman.

I knew that laugh. I'd heard it at every Thanksgiving for twenty-six years.

"What do you mean, the lake?" Steve said. "I'm at the Boardman store. Doing inventory."

"Inventory," Nicole said. "At a car wash."

"Soap, babe. Wax. You know."

"Steve, tell Lori I said hi."

Another pause. Longer. The screen door slapped again.

"Who?" Steve said.

"Lori Sweeney. She's standing about ten feet from you. Her husband's sitting about two feet from me."

The call ended. Just like that. No goodbye, no click, the screen just went back to Nicole's lock screen, which was a picture of her two sons at a Browns game.

Ray put his glasses back on.

"Well," he said. "Now they know."

Nicole and I had known each other for exactly three weeks. You're probably wondering how a refrigeration tech from the north side and an ER nurse from Canfield end up in the same lawyer's office on a Friday afternoon. It was a compressor.

I service the walk-in cooler at a little Italian place in Canfield called Nicky's. It's owned by Nicole's brother, Anthony. In October the cooler's compressor died on a Saturday night, I drove out at eleven p.m., and Nicole was there, helping her brother move forty pounds of sausage into a borrowed chest freezer.

She looked at the name on my work shirt. Then she looked again.

"Sweeney," she said. "Are you married to a Lori?"

"Twenty-six years."

She didn't say anything else that night. But two days later she found me on Facebook, through her brother's page, where I'm tagged in a picture holding a busted compressor like a fish. Her message was four lines long.

"I'm sorry to do this to you. I think my husband is seeing your wife. Our doorbell camera has her car in our driveway on three Wednesdays in a row while I was on twelve-hour shifts. I can show you if you want to see."

I wanted to see.

So that's how it started. With a camera, a busted compressor, and a nurse who'd worked enough night shifts to know that nobody's car sits in your driveway for four hours on a Wednesday because they're dropping off a casserole.

"That," Frances said, "a judge would find very interesting."

"So what is it for?" Ray asked. "The refi. If not countertops."

"Okay."

You know when you've been chasing a slow leak in a walk-in for three days, and you finally put the electronic sniffer on the right fitting, and the thing starts screaming? It was like that. All of it, the Pinterest pictures, the quartz countertops, the way she'd put her hand on my arm in September and said "We deserve something nice for once." It was all a leak. Lori.

"Hi, Steve," Nicole said. "Ray has something for you."

Ray set a letter on the table in front of Steve. Steve picked it up and read it, and his sunburn went a shade redder.

"Removed as managing member," Steve read. "Effective eight a.m. Company cards canceled. Demand for return of ten thousand dollars in company funds paid to..." He looked up. "You can't do this. It's my company."

"It's my father's company," Nicole said. "You've worked there. You've never owned it. Fifty-one percent mine, twenty-five percent Anthony's, twenty-four percent yours, and the operating agreement my dad wrote in 2009 says two members can remove a manager for cause. Using company money to put a deposit on your girlfriend's car wash is cause."

"Shine On," Ray said. "Cartoon bubble with sunglasses. We've seen the logo."

Lori looked up from her divorce papers.

"Steve," she said slowly, "you told me the cottage was yours. You told me the company was yours. You told me we'd be fine."

"You told me your husband would sign!"

"You told me you owned a lake house!"

"I practically do! I've been mowing that lawn for twenty-two years!"

"That's not owning it, Steve. That's mowing it."

He turned to Nicole. Then he turned back to Lori. I watched him do the same math I'd done in Tammy's booth at the Panera, the math every man eventually does when the pressure's gone out of a system. No company. No cards. No cottage. No eighty thousand dollars from my house. And a woman sitting next to him with divorce papers in her lap and twenty-two thousand dollars in her half of an account that was already shrinking.

"I need some air," Steve said.

"Steve."

"I'll call you."

"Steve, don't you dare walk out of..."

He walked out. Through the lobby, past the bowl of butterscotch candies, out the glass door, into the parking lot. Through the window we watched him stand by the Speedway sign for a minute with his phone at his ear. Then he got into a cab that someone must have called for him, because his car, it turned out, was a company car too.

Nicole watched him go. Then she looked at me across the closing table, and there was nothing triumphant in her face. Just tired.

"Twenty-two years," she said.

"Twenty-six," I said.

"We should've had kids with better people, Alvin."

"We should've had coolers with better seals."

That actually made her laugh. Then she and Ray left, and Frances gathered her papers, and Janelle Hardy closed the file folder with the pink flag on top and wrote "CANCELLED" across the front in black marker.

I walked out to my truck. It was a cold, bright November morning, and the Speedway was doing a brisk business in coffee.

Lori followed me out.

She caught up with me at the driver's door, the divorce papers crushed in one hand and the useless power of attorney in the other. Her mascara had run. Her good navy blazer had a butterscotch wrapper stuck to the sleeve.

"Alvin," she said. "Alvin, please. Please, just wait."

"I'm waiting."

"I made a mistake. A big one. I know. I know that. But Steve's gone, you saw him, he's gone. It was never real. I was bored and I was stupid, and he made promises, and I believed him. That's all it was." She grabbed my sleeve. "Come home. Please. We can fix it. We'll go to counseling. I'll do the kitchen with my own money. I'll turn my phone off for real. Just don't do this. Don't throw away twenty-six years over one weekend."

"It wasn't one weekend, Lori."

"Three months. Okay. Three months."

"It was eighty thousand dollars," I said. "Out of our house. For a car wash with his name on it. And you'd have signed my name for me to get it."

She let go of my sleeve.

"So that's it?" she said. "After everything? That's all you've got to say to me?"

I opened the door of my truck and climbed in. I put the key in the ignition. Then I rolled the window down, and looked at my wife one more time standing there in the Valley Title parking lot with a phone in her hand that had been on the whole time.

"Enjoy," I said.
