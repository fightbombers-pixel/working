"I'm going back to my ex. I never really loved you."

My wife said it on the deck of our marina on Lake of the Ozarks, on the last Saturday of the season, at the staff cookout, with 31 people standing around the grill holding paper plates. She said it loud, the way you'd announce the raffle winner. Then she smiled.

I had a pair of tongs in one hand and a bratwurst on them. Fifteen years of marriage, and the bratwurst was the thing I noticed. It was burning. I'd been married to her for 15 years and one week, and she'd picked the one day of the year every single person who worked for me would be standing on my deck.

I took the bratwurst off the grill and set it on a bun. Then I put the tongs down, slid my wedding ring off, walked over and laid it in the palm of her hand and closed her fingers over it.

And I nodded. That's all I did. I nodded.

Nineteen hours later her own lawyer was on my phone, and a grown man with 30 years at the bar could barely get his words out.

What she didn't know was what was printed on the first page of the blue binder in the safe in my office, and whose name was on it.

My name is Thad Barker. I'm 54, and I own Barker's Point Marina near Osage Beach, Missouri, 140 boat slips, a fuel dock, a bait shop and a ramp that's been in my family since my granddad dug it out with a mule in 1952. A marina teaches you one thing above all. The water always tells you who's tied up right and who isn't, and it tells you the night the storm comes. Hold on to that.

"Did you hear me, Thad?" Sabrina said. She held up the ring between two fingers like it was a dead minnow. "I said I'm going back to Shane."

"I heard you."

"That's it? You're just going to nod?"

Behind her, my dock manager, a gray-bearded man named Earl Fentress, put his plate down on the rail. My bait shop girl, Kelsey, had both hands over her mouth. My son Dalton was standing by the cooler with a Coke halfway to his lips, and he wasn't drinking it.

"What do you want me to say?" I asked.

"I don't know. Something! Fifteen years!"

"You just told me you never loved me for any of them."

She laughed, a short hard laugh. Sabrina is 47, and she's still the best-looking woman at any party on the lake, and she knows it the way a bass boat knows it's fast. She'd kept the books for the marina since the year we married, signed the checks, paid the fuel man, handled the slip renters' deposits. She knew where every dollar of my business went. At least she thought she did.

"I'm being honest," she said. "Shane and I were together before you. He was always the one. He's back in town, he's doing great, Lakeview Pointe is going to be the biggest condo project on the 19-mile marker, and he wants me back. I'm not going to lie anymore. I'm moving in with him tonight."

"Okay."

"Okay?" Her voice went up. "Okay? That's what you've got?"

"Take what's yours," I said. "All of it. I won't fight you."

She stared at me. You could see her trying to work out the trick, and not finding one, and deciding there wasn't any. Then she smiled again, slower, and a little meaner.

"You heard him," she said to the deck, to all 31 of them. "Everybody heard him. He said take what's mine."

"I heard him," Earl Fentress said quietly.

She turned on her heel and walked down the steps to the parking lot, and I watched her get into her white Escalade, the one she'd picked out in 2021, and I watched her drive out past the bait shop and turn left on Route KK toward the water.

Thirty-one people looked at me. Nobody said a thing. Then Earl picked his plate back up.

"Well," Earl said. "Brats are burning, Thad."

I went back to the grill and flipped them, and my hand was perfectly steady, and I want you to know I've never been so surprised by anything in my life as I was by that steady hand.

Dalton found me on the end of C Dock about an hour after dark, after everybody had gone home. I was sitting on an overturned bait bucket watching the lights across the cove, and he sat down on the dock boards next to me with his legs hanging over the water, like he used to when he was six.

Dalton is 17. His mother, my first wife, Lisa, died of breast cancer when he was two, and Sabrina came along when he was going on three, and for most of his life she's been the only mother he knew. I'd been worried all evening about how he'd take it.

"You okay, Dad?"

"I'm okay. Are you?"

He thought about it. Dalton thinks about things. He gets that from his mother.

"I knew," he said.

I turned and looked at him.

"Not that she'd say it like that," he said. "In front of everybody. That was messed up. But I knew it was Shane." He picked at a splinter on the dock board. "Dad, Shane took me fishing two Saturdays ago."

"Shane Dobbs took you fishing."

"Yeah. Mom set it up. She said he was an old friend who wanted to get to know me, since he was going to be doing business on the lake." He shook his head. "We went out on his boat to the Gravois arm. We didn't catch anything. I don't think he even knew how to tie on a jig. He just kept asking me questions."

"What kind of questions?"

"About you. About the marina. Who owns the land. Whether you had a lawyer. Whether Mom signs all the checks. Whether you ever talked about selling." Dalton looked at me. "He asked if there was a trust. He said that word. Trust. Like he already knew something."

I didn't say anything. Out on the main channel, a cruiser went by too fast, and its wake came rolling into the cove and slapped the pilings under us.

"What did you tell him?" I said.

"I told him you keep all your money in a coffee can in the bait shop and you're scared of banks." A little grin came and went. "He believed it for like a whole minute."

I laughed out loud. I couldn't help it. It echoed across the cove.

"But Dad," Dalton said, and the grin was gone. "There's something else. When we got back to his dock, he got a phone call, and he walked up on the deck to take it, but the wind was coming my way and I heard him. He said, 'Once she moves it, it's mine, and she can whistle for it.' And then he said, 'Lakeview Pointe closes or the bank takes everything by Christmas. I need that marina frontage.'"

He pulled his phone out of his pocket.

"And I recorded it," he said. "Missouri's a one-party state. I looked it up. As long as I'm part of the conversation, I can record. And I figured standing on his dock counted."

I took the phone out of his hand and looked at it. I didn't play it. Not yet.

"You sat on this for two weeks?"

"I almost told you a bunch of times. At supper. In the truck." He looked down at the water. "But she's been my mom since I was three, Dad. I kept thinking maybe I heard it wrong. Maybe 'she' was somebody else."

"And today?"

"Today she told everybody on the deck she never loved you," Dalton said. "So I quit thinking I heard it wrong."

I didn't say anything to that. There wasn't anything to say that would have made it any better, so I put my hand on the back of his neck the way my dad used to do with me, and left it there.

"Dad?"

"Yeah."

"I'm sorry I didn't tell you sooner."

"You told me in time," I said. "That's the part that counts."

We sat there a long while, the two of us, listening to the water under the dock. Then my own phone buzzed in my shirt pocket. It was coming up on midnight. I took it out and looked at the screen.

It was a text alert from the bank, Lake Regional Bank, from the business account. The one Sabrina had signed on for 15 years.

"Outgoing wire initiated: $380,000.00. Barker's Point Marina LLC Operating to Dobbs Development Group LLC. If you did not authorize this transaction, call us immediately."

Dalton read it over my shoulder.

"Dad," he said. "Dad, she's taking everything."

"She's taking what she thinks is hers," I said.

"Aren't you going to call the bank?"

I looked out at the black water, at the dock lines on all 140 slips, every one of them tied the way I'd taught Earl to tie them, every one of them holding.

"Not tonight," I said. "Tonight I'm going to sleep. Tomorrow I'll call the bank, and the sheriff, and Mr. Ledbetter at the bank, and a lawyer. And tomorrow, son, somebody's going to read that blue binder."

Vern Ledbetter has been president of Lake Regional Bank since 1998 and goes to the same Methodist church I do, and a little after seven Sunday morning he answered his home phone in his bathrobe.

"Thad? It's Sunday."

"I know it, Vern. I need you to put a hold on a wire."

I told him. He was quiet a long time.

"It was initiated after hours Saturday," he said finally. "So it won't actually go out until the Fed opens Monday morning. I can flag it as disputed from here. But Thad, she's a signer on that account. Has been for 15 years. If she authorized it, I can't just..."

"She's an employee signer, Vern. Read the resolution on file. The LLC's manager is the only one who can authorize a transfer over $25,000 to a non-vendor. It's been that way since my dad. And Dobbs Development isn't a vendor."

I heard him put the coffee down. I heard a drawer open.

"I'll be at the branch in half an hour," he said. "Meet me there. Bring the binder."

So I did. Dalton came with me, because he wouldn't stay home, and because I figured he'd earned it. Vern opened the side door of the branch on Osage Beach Parkway in his church clothes, and we sat in his office with the lights half on while he read the corporate resolution, and then the first page of the blue binder.

He read part of it out loud, the way some men do when they want to be sure of the words.

"'The Lisa Marie Barker Irrevocable Trust. Established March 2010. Sole beneficiary, Dalton James Barker, a minor. Trustee, Thaddeus Barker. The trust holds one hundred percent of the membership interest in Barker's Point Marina LLC, including all real property, improvements, vessels, accounts and equipment.'" He looked up at Dalton over the top of his glasses. "Son, do you know what this says?"

Dalton shook his head.

"It says you own a marina."

Dalton looked at me. I'd never told him. Lisa and I had set it up the winter she got sick, with the life insurance money and my dad's blessing, so that whatever happened to me, the point would go to her boy and nobody else. I'd always meant to sit him down and explain it when he turned 18. I just hadn't gotten there yet.

"Dad," he said slowly. "Does Mom know?"

"She's had a copy in her desk drawer for 15 years," I said. "She never opened it."

Vern Ledbetter took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes.

"Well," he said. "That's that, then. The wire's held. I'm marking it unauthorized." He looked at me. "You know she's going to have a problem, Thad. A real one. This isn't a divorce thing anymore."

"I know."

"Did you know she'd do this?"

I thought about how to answer that honestly.

"I knew she thought everything was hers," I said. "I didn't know how fast she'd prove it."

The deputy from the Camden County Sheriff's Office was a big, slow-talking young man named Deputy Ray Tull, who took my report at the kitchen table of the house afterward, and wrote it all down, and asked twice to see the text from the bank.

"Ma'am didn't come home last night?" he asked.

"She's at Shane Dobbs's place. Lakeview Pointe. She told 31 people at the cookout."

"And you told her to take what's hers."

"Yes, sir."

"Huh," Deputy Tull said, and wrote that down too, and underlined it.

Sabrina called mid-morning. I put it on speaker so Dalton could hear. I figured he had a right.

"Did you get my text?" she said. She sounded like she'd been up all night and enjoyed every minute of it.

"Which one?"

"The bank one. The wire. I just want to be clear, Thad, that I did exactly what you said. In front of witnesses. You said take what's yours. I took my half of the business account. That's fair. That's more than fair, honestly. I could have taken all of it."

"Your half."

"I kept those books for 15 years. Half that marina is mine. Missouri's a marital property state. Shane's lawyer says so."

"Shane's lawyer."

"He's my lawyer now. Mort Ellwood. He's the best in Camden County. Shane's paying." She laughed. "And I want the house, too, so you'd better start packing. You said you wouldn't fight me."

"I won't."

"Good."

"Sabrina," I said, "the wire's on hold. Vern Ledbetter flagged it this morning."

There was a sound on the line like somebody dropping a coffee cup.

"He can't do that!"

"He did it."

"I'm a signer! I've been a signer for 15 years!"

"You're an employee signer. You never read the resolution. You never read anything you didn't have to."

"You set me up! You said take what's mine!"

"I did say that," I said. "I meant it. Take what's yours. That's still the deal."

"What does that even mean?"

"Ask Mort Ellwood," I said. "Tell him to look up who owns the house."

She hung up. Dalton was looking at me from across the table with his mouth a little open.

"Dad," he said. "That was cold."

"It was true."

"Cold and true," he said, "is kind of the worst combination."

He wasn't wrong. I'll be honest with you. Sitting there in my granddad's kitchen with the sun coming off the lake, I didn't feel like I'd won anything. I felt like I'd watched a woman I'd lived with for 15 years walk out onto thin ice in a fur coat, laughing, and I hadn't yelled at her to stop.

About three that afternoon a gray cruiser with a Lakeview Pointe banner on the bow rail came idling into the cove and nosed up to my fuel dock, and Shane Dobbs stepped off it like he was stepping onto a stage.

I was on the fuel dock checking the pump meter. Earl was coiling hose. Dalton was sitting on the bait shop steps, and when he saw who it was he stood up.

"Thad Barker," Shane said, and held out his hand. "Been a long time."

"We've never met."

"Well, we have now." He left the hand out a while, then put it in his pocket like that was what he'd meant to do all along. He's a big man, 50, with very white teeth and a tan in October, and sunglasses pushed up on his head. "Look, I'm not here to cause trouble. I'm here to make this easy on everybody. Especially Sabrina."

"Go ahead."

"I'll buy the marina. Today. Cash offer, one point two million, as is. You and Sabrina split it, everybody walks away clean, nobody has to go to court. I'll even let you keep running the bait shop if you want. You can sell night crawlers to my condo people."

Earl stopped coiling hose.

"It's not for sale," I said.

"Everything's for sale."

"Not this. It's not mine to sell."

Shane laughed. "Come on. Sabrina told me how this works. Your granddad, your dad, you. Three generations of Barkers on this point. It's yours, all right."

"It's not."

"Then whose is it?"

I didn't answer. I just looked at him. And I watched him look past me, up at the bait shop steps, where Dalton was standing with his hands in his hoodie pocket.

"Hey, sport," Shane said, with a big grin. "Caught anything since our trip?"

"Yeah," Dalton said. "Something."

Shane's grin stayed where it was, but his eyes moved, back and forth between Dalton and me, the way a fish looks at a lure it's not sure about.

"One point two," he said to me again, lower. "That's a good number, Barker. It's going to look a whole lot better than whatever you end up with after a judge splits it. And I'd take it today, before the Fed opens tomorrow. Today."

"Why today?"

He didn't have an answer for that. He put his sunglasses back down over his eyes.

"Offer's open till midnight," he said. "After that, I stop being nice."

He got back on his cruiser and backed it off the fuel dock too fast, and his wake slopped up over the boards and soaked Earl's boots.

"Before the Fed opens," Earl said, looking down at his wet feet. "Now why would a man with a million two in cash need anything before the Fed opens?"

"Because he doesn't have a million two," I said. "He's got nine weeks and a wire on hold."

My phone rang that evening a little after seven. Nineteen hours, almost to the minute, since the cookout.

The man on the other end cleared his throat twice before he got anything out.

"Mr. Barker? This is Mort Ellwood. I'm... I represent your wife." Another throat clear. "I represent Sabrina."

"Evening, Mr. Ellwood."

"Mr. Barker, I've just spent four hours at my office. On a Sunday. Reading the Camden County recorder's site and the Secretary of State filings and a certification of trust that your wife brought me herself, out of her own desk at the marina, to prove what she was owed. She'd had it in that drawer for 15 years." He stopped. His voice was shaking. I've heard a lot of men's voices shake on the lake, in storms, when a dock lets go, and this sounded like that. "The house. The accounts. My God, what did you tell her yesterday?"

"I told her to take what's hers," I said.

"She told me that. She told me that like it was a gift. She came into my office this afternoon saying her husband had given her everything in front of 31 witnesses." He took a breath. "Mr. Barker, the house isn't marital property. It's titled to Barker's Point Marina LLC. The LLC's sole member is the Lisa Barker Irrevocable Trust, established in 2010, the year before you married my client. The beneficiary is your son. You're the trustee. You don't own the marina. Your son does. You run it."

"That's right."

"Your wife has been an employee of that LLC for 15 years. She doesn't own a single slip. She doesn't own a single board of that dock. And last night, just before midnight, she initiated a $380,000 wire out of an operating account belonging to a minor's trust." His voice went up a notch. "Mr. Barker, $61,000 of that is customer slip deposits. Twenty-two thousand of it is Missouri sales tax held in trust for the state. She tried to send it to the personal company of the man she's living with."

"Yes, sir."

"That's not a divorce. That's a felony. Possibly two."

"I know."

"And she has nothing. Do you understand? Nothing. There's no marital estate to split except her car and your retirement account, and even the car..."

"The car's leased through the LLC."

He made a sound like a man who's just been hit in the stomach.

"Why didn't you tell her?" he said. "Why didn't you just tell her?"

"Because in 15 years, Mr. Ellwood, she never once asked," I said. "She kept the books. She signed the checks. She never once opened the binder. She never wanted to know whose it was. She only wanted to know how much."

There was a long quiet on the line.

"I'm going to have to withdraw," he said finally. "I can't... There's a conflict. I took a retainer from Mr. Dobbs." He stopped. "Mr. Barker, I'm going to tell you something I shouldn't, because I've got a daughter about your boy's age. Shane Dobbs called me twice this afternoon while I was reading. The second time, when I told him the wire was held and the marina wasn't hers, he said something I'm not going to repeat. Then he said, 'Fine. Then I'll take what I can carry.'"

"What does that mean?"

"I don't know," Mort Ellwood said. "But if I were you, I'd sleep down at the docks tonight."

I called Earl Fentress, and Earl came, because Earl has come every time I've called him for 26 years, including the night of the 2017 flood when the water came up over the fuel dock and we tied off 140 boats in the dark with flashlights in our teeth.

He showed up at the bait shop a little after nine with a thermos and a spotlight and a 12-gauge he didn't load and didn't need to.

"You think he's coming for the boats?" Earl said.

"I think he's coming for the one he can sell fastest."

We both looked at the same slip. A-1, the end slip by the fuel dock, where the marina's new demo boat sat on its lift under a blue cover. A 24-foot wake surf boat with a 450-horse engine and a sticker price of $142,000. We'd taken delivery in August to show to customers in the spring. It was titled to the LLC. Sabrina had signed the floor plan papers, so Sabrina knew exactly what it was worth, and Sabrina knew where the spare key was kept, because Sabrina was the one who'd hung it on the hook behind the register.

I went and looked at the hook. The spare key was gone.

"Well," Earl said. "That answers that."

"When did you last see it?"

"Friday. Before the cookout." He spat off the dock. "She was in here Saturday morning, Thad. Said she was getting change for the grill."

Dalton came down at ten with a blanket and a bag of Funyuns and refused to go back up to the house, and I didn't have it in me to make him. The three of us sat in the dark in the bait shop with the lights off, watching A-1 through the window.

It was a cold night for October, 41 degrees, with fog starting to come off the water in sheets. You couldn't see the far side of the cove by eleven. By midnight you couldn't see the end of C Dock.

A little before one, Dalton grabbed my sleeve.

"Dad. Listen."

An outboard. Low, idling, out in the fog, coming into the cove from the main channel with no running lights. Small boat. Jon boat maybe, or a skiff. It came in slow and bumped against the fuel dock, and somebody climbed out onto the boards.

One man. Tall. A ball cap pulled low. He walked straight down the dock to A-1 like he'd done it before, pulled the cover half off, hit the lift switch, and started lowering the boat into the water.

"Shane Dobbs," Earl said. "Don't even need the spotlight."

"Call 911," I said to Dalton. "Tell them it's a boat theft in progress at Barker's Point. Tell them to send the Water Patrol."

"Dad, wait for the..."

"Call."

I went out the bait shop door and down the ramp onto the fuel dock. The boat was halfway down the lift. Shane had his back to me, fumbling with the spare key at the helm.

"That's not yours, Shane."

He spun around. He had on a quarter-zip pullover that cost more than Earl's truck, and he looked at me the way men look at a dog that's gotten loose. He didn't look scared. That surprised me.

"Barker," he said. "Go back to bed."

"Get off the boat."

"This boat's half Sabrina's. She told me so. You told her to take what's hers."

"That boat belongs to a 17-year-old boy's trust fund. So does the dock you're standing on. So does the money you tried to take last night."

"Tried," Shane said, and his face twisted. "Tried. You know what you cost me, you smug son of a witch? I've got $4 million in notes coming due on Lakeview Pointe in nine weeks. I needed that money Monday. I needed this marina by spring. I spent two years setting this up."

"Two years?"

He stopped. He'd said too much and he knew it.

"Two years," I said. "So she wasn't going back to you. You went back to her. You came looking for her because she was married to the man with the frontage."

"She'll do what I tell her," Shane said. "She always did."

The lift hit bottom and the boat floated free. He turned the key, and that 450 roared up like a jet in the fog, and he threw the throttle forward before the stern line was even off.

The line snapped tight on the cleat and the boat slewed sideways, and I grabbed for the gunwale and caught it, and for about two seconds I was hanging off a moving $142,000 boat by my fingertips with my boots dragging on the fuel dock.

Then the cleat let go, or the line did, and I went into Lake of the Ozarks in October.

I want to tell you it was dramatic. It was mostly cold. Forty-one degrees in the air and maybe 60 in the water, and it took my breath like a punch. I came up spitting next to the fuel dock pilings and heard the big engine screaming away into the fog toward the main channel.

Earl had me by the collar before I could go under again. He hauled me out onto the dock like a catfish.

"You're too old for that," he said.

"Get the patrol boat."

"Thad, you're soaked."

"Earl, get the patrol boat."

The patrol boat is a 19-foot center console with a 150 on the back and BARKER'S POINT painted on the side, and we use it to tow in dead boats and chase off drunks. Earl had it running by the time I'd stripped off my wet coat. Dalton came pounding down the ramp with the blanket and his phone.

"Water Patrol's coming from the Grand Glaize Bridge," he yelled. "Ten minutes, they said!"

"Stay here!"

"No!"

He jumped into the bow. There wasn't time to argue. Earl threw off the lines and we went out of the cove into the fog after the sound of that engine.

You can't see anything on the lake at night in fog. You go by sound and by knowing the water, and I've known that water since before I could walk. Shane Dobbs had bought his lakefront two years ago from a man in Kansas City.

"He's heading up the main channel," Earl said, cutting the engine to listen. "Toward the 19-mile marker."

"Toward Lakeview Pointe," Dalton said.

"He's going to put it in his own boathouse," I said. "And swear she gave it to him."

Earl throttled up. Dalton stood in the bow with the spotlight, sweeping the fog. We could hear him ahead of us, that big engine, louder and louder, and then all of a sudden we couldn't, because he'd cut it way back, and I understood why a second before Dalton did.

"He's lost," I said. "He can't find the channel markers in this."

"Dad! Light!"

Dalton's spotlight caught the white stern of the demo boat about fifty yards ahead, idling, turning in a slow circle. Shane was standing at the helm with his phone up, trying to read his map in the fog.

He saw our light, and he jammed the throttle down and turned hard to starboard, away from us, toward the dark shoreline.

"No," I said. "Not that way. Earl, he's heading for the flats."

There's a shallow spot off the point below Lakeview Pointe, a rock shelf that comes up to about two feet in the fall when the Corps draws the lake down. Everybody who lives on the lake knows it. Every channel chart marks it. Shane Dobbs had never looked at a chart in his life.

We heard it before we saw it. A long ugly grinding crunch, like a garbage truck eating a refrigerator, and then the engine screaming as the prop came out of the water, and then nothing.

Dalton's light found the boat sitting on the rock shelf at an angle, its bow pointed up at the sky, its hull cracked open on one side. Shane was on his hands and knees in the cockpit, holding his forehead where he'd hit the windshield.

Then blue lights came through the fog behind us, and a siren whooped once, and the Missouri State Highway Patrol Water Patrol boat came alongside with a spotlight twice as big as ours.

Sergeant Jolene Doolin stood in the bow in her flotation vest with a hand on her holster.

"Evening, Thad," she called. "That your boat on the rocks?"

"It's my son's," I said. "That's not my son on it."

Sergeant Doolin looked at Shane, and at the boat, and at me standing there in my undershirt with lake water still dripping off my nose.

"You went in?" she said.

"He went in," Earl said. "He's too old for it."

Sergeant Doolin shook her head, and then she lifted her bullhorn.

"Sir on the boat, stay where you are with your hands visible. Missouri State Highway Patrol."

Shane Dobbs stood up in the tilted cockpit with blood running down his face, raised his hands and shouted across the water.

"It's half hers! Ask Sabrina! He told her to take what's hers!"

It took Sergeant Doolin and her partner twenty minutes to get him off that rock shelf, because the water around it was four feet deep and the shelf itself was slick with moss. They put him in the back of the patrol boat in a blanket, and she cuffed him there, right in front of the spotlight, with his hands behind his back and blood drying on his eyebrow.

Shane Dobbs looked over at me across about ten feet of black water.

"She'll tell them," he said. "She'll tell them you gave it to her."

"She can tell them anything she likes," I said. "The boat's still on the rocks."

Sergeant Doolin read him his rights, and the charge, which was tampering with a motor vehicle in the first degree, which is what Missouri calls stealing a boat, plus operating a vessel without lights and leaving the scene of property damage, which she said she'd think of more of on the ride in. Then they took him away up the channel toward the Grand Glaize Bridge, and the fog closed in behind their blue lights.

Earl got us home a little after three. Dalton fell asleep in the bow with the spotlight in his lap.

Sabrina came to the marina at nine the next morning.

I was at my desk in the office behind the bait shop with a cup of coffee and a fresh shirt and a bruise on my ribs the size of a dinner plate, from the boat gunwale. Dalton was on the couch by the window eating cereal out of the box, because he'd announced at breakfast that he wasn't going to school, and I hadn't argued.

She came in without knocking. She was wearing yesterday's clothes and her hair was in a clip and she hadn't put on any makeup, and I'd never in 15 years seen Sabrina leave a house without makeup.

"Shane's in jail," she said.

"I know. I was there."

"They won't let me see him. They say he's got a $50,000 bond and his bank won't release anything because his accounts are tied up with his lender." She took a breath. "Thad, I need you to tell Deputy Tull you authorized the wire."

"No."

"You did authorize it! You said take what's yours! Thirty-one people heard you say it!"

"I said take what's yours. I didn't say take what's Dalton's."

"Dalton's?" She swung around and looked at him on the couch. Dalton stopped chewing. "What does he have to do with anything?"

"He owns the marina, Sabrina. He has since he was three. His mother left it to him in a trust. I just run it for him till he's 25. It's in the binder in the safe. It's in the bank resolution. It's in your desk drawer, the drawer you took it out of yesterday to show your lawyer."

"I never read that!"

"I know."

"Nobody told me!"

"You never asked. Fifteen years, and you never asked one time whose name was on anything. You asked me how much we cleared in July. You asked me how much the house would appraise for. You never asked whose it was."

Her chin was shaking now. She put her hand on the corner of my desk.

"You know why I never asked?" she said. "Because you're boring, Thad. You smell like two-stroke oil and bait. You talk about dock lines at dinner. Nobody in their right mind would ask a man like you about anything."

"Shane asked," I said. "He asked my son."

She flinched like I'd thrown water on her.

"Mort Ellwood quit," she said. "He called me last night and said he had a conflict and he couldn't represent me anymore. He said I should get a criminal lawyer. A criminal lawyer, Thad. Me."

"I'm sorry."

"You are not sorry! You set me up! You stood there and nodded and let me walk right into it!"

"You walked into it the day you started setting it up with Shane," I said. "You just walked faster after I nodded."

"I didn't set anything up! Shane loves me! He came back for me!"

"He came back for the frontage."

"That's a lie!"

Dalton put down the cereal box. He pulled his phone out of his hoodie pocket. He looked at me, and I nodded, and he got up and walked over and set the phone on the corner of the desk in front of her and pushed play.

The wind was in it. Water lapping against a dock. A man's voice, a little far off, but clear enough. You could hear every word.

"Once she moves it, it's mine, and she can whistle for it. No, listen to me. Lakeview Pointe closes or the bank takes everything by Christmas. I need that marina frontage. She's just the key to the gate, Danny. I'm not marrying her again, are you kidding me? I did that once."

Then a laugh. Shane's laugh. You could hear him laugh like he'd just told the best joke in the world.

Dalton reached over and stopped it.

"Two Saturdays ago," he said. "On his dock. After he took me fishing to find out if Dad had a trust." He swallowed. "You set that up, Mom. You told me he just wanted to get to know me."

Sabrina was staring at the phone like it had bitten her.

"That's... that's not... Shane wouldn't..."

"He did," Dalton said.

The office door opened behind her. Deputy Ray Tull stood in the doorway in his tan uniform, holding his hat in one hand and a folded piece of paper in the other. Behind him, out on the dock, Earl Fentress was pretending to coil a line.

"Mrs. Barker," Deputy Tull said. "I was told I might find you here. I need you to come down to the station with me, ma'am. There's a warrant out of Camden County for attempted stealing by deceit, over $25,000, from Barker's Point Marina LLC."

Sabrina turned and looked at him. Then she looked at me. Then she looked at Dalton, last and longest.

"Thad," she said. "Thad, please. Tell him. Tell him it was a misunderstanding. Tell him you said I could. You said take what's mine. You said you wouldn't fight me."

"I'm not fighting you," I said.

"Then help me!"

"I'm not doing that either."

Deputy Tull stepped in and put his hand on her arm, gently, the way you'd guide somebody off a boat onto a dock. He didn't cuff her. He didn't need to. She went with him to the door, and at the door she stopped and turned around, and her mascara-less face was wet all the way down.

"Fifteen years," she said. "I was your mother for fifteen years, Dalton."

Dalton stood there in my office in his socks with the phone in his hand. He was 17, and he looked 12, and he looked 40.

"You could have just asked whose it was," he said. "Fifteen years, Mom. Dad would have told you. He'd have told you everything. All you had to do was ask."

She opened her mouth. Nothing came out. Deputy Tull walked her down the dock past Earl and the bait shop and the fuel pumps, past the empty A-1 slip with its lift still down in the water, and put her in the back of his cruiser in the gravel lot.

Dalton watched the cruiser all the way out to Route KK. Then he turned around and looked at me.

"Dad," he said. "When I turn 25, and it's really mine. Do I have to run it like you do?"

"You don't have to do anything."

"No, I mean it." He sat back down on the couch and picked up the cereal box. "I want to run it like you do. Except I'm going to read every single thing I sign."

I came around the desk and sat down next to him, and took a handful of cereal out of the box.

"Son," I said, "that's the only rule I've got."
