"See? Told you he'd still be here, like a pathetic puppy."

My wife said that at a quarter to eleven on a Saturday night, at a white-tablecloth restaurant on Broad Street, on our 27th wedding anniversary, loud enough for the four friends behind her, the waiter, the bartender and two full tables of strangers to hear every word. Her friends laughed. Kendall Ames laughed so hard she had to hold on to the back of my chair.

I'd been sitting at that table for three hours and four minutes. The reservation was for seven-thirty. I'd ordered one bourbon at seven-thirty, a second at eight-fifteen, and I'd let the bread basket go stale around nine because I didn't want to eat before she got there. Twenty-seven years, and I still didn't want to eat before she got there.

Simone was wearing the green dress I bought her for Christmas. Her lipstick was fresh and her eyes were not. She'd come from somewhere with a lot of wine in it.

I smiled at her. Then I lifted my empty glass toward the waiter, a skinny kid named Caleb who'd been checking on me every twenty minutes with a face like he was visiting me in the hospital.

"Another Maker's, please," I said. "And Caleb, when you get a second, I'd like to close out my card. Just my part."

Forty minutes later, Kendall Ames was standing at the host stand with four credit cards fanned out in her hand like a losing poker hand, and the manager was explaining something to her very slowly.

What she didn't know, and what my wife didn't know, was what was inside the cream-colored envelope I'd handed the bartender at eight o'clock and asked her to hold for me.

My name is Elijah Carver. I'm 53, and I've run Carver Heating and Air out of a cinderblock building on Gordon Highway in Augusta, Georgia, for twenty-two years. My trade taught me that the loudest unit in the house is almost never the one that's broken. It's the quiet one in the attic that's been running too hard for too long. Hold on to that.

Simone pulled out the chair across from me and sat down like she was doing me a favor. Kendall and the other three, Paige Dorsett, Tami something from Simone's spin class, and a woman in a sequined jacket I'd never seen in my life, stood around the table in a half circle, still giggling. Kendall had her phone up.

"Smile for the camera, Elijah," Kendall said. "Simone, say happy anniversary."

"Happy anniversary," Simone said into the phone, and wiggled her fingers.

Caleb set the bourbon down in front of me. His hand wasn't quite steady.

"Thank you, Caleb," I said.

"Sir, the, uh..." He leaned down. "The rest of the party. They called this afternoon and added four guests to your reservation. And two bottles of the Veuve. And the chef's tasting for six." He swallowed. "On the card you put down for the deposit."

I looked up at him. He looked like he wanted to be anywhere else in Georgia.

"Who called?"

"A woman. She said she was Mrs. Carver's friend and it was all a surprise for you."

Across the table, Kendall lowered her phone half an inch.

"It is a surprise," Simone said. "Lighten up. It's a party. You're always saying we never go out."

"I said that," I said. "I did say that."

"So we're out." She reached for the bread basket, picked up a roll, made a face at it and dropped it back. "God, it's cold. How long have you been sitting here?"

"Since seven-thirty."

The woman in the sequins said, "Oh my Lord," and started laughing again.

"Three hours?" Simone said. She turned to Kendall. "Three hours. You owe me a hundred dollars."

Kendall slapped her arm. "Simone!"

"What? You said he'd leave after one."

The whole half circle cracked up. Paige Dorsett was the only one who didn't. She was looking at the floor.

I took a sip of my bourbon. It's a funny thing. You'd think a moment like that would feel like being hit by a truck, and maybe later it does. Right then it felt like the moment on a service call when you pull the panel off the furnace and finally see the cracked heat exchanger you've been smelling for an hour. You don't yell at it. You just know.

Caleb came back with a little black folder and set it at my elbow. I opened it. Two bourbons, one Maker's, the bread nobody ate, and the $150 deposit for a table for two. Underneath, in Caleb's handwriting, was a note: "Rest of party not on this. Talked to my manager. Your call, sir."

I signed it. I put a hundred dollars cash in the folder for Caleb, because that boy had earned it twice over, and I stood up and took my jacket off the back of the chair.

"Where are you going?" Simone said.

"Home."

"We just got here."

"I know," I said. "I've been here."

"Oh, come on. Don't be a baby about it. It was a joke."

"You told them I'd still be here," I said, "and I was. You were right, Simone. Enjoy your party."

I put my jacket on and walked to the bar.

Ruthie Pardo has been tending bar at Marchetti's since it was a steakhouse with a different name. She's 64, wears her reading glasses on top of her head, and has the kind of face that's heard every story in the world and believed about a third of them. At eight o'clock, when it was clear my wife was going to be late, I'd handed her a cream-colored envelope and asked her to keep it behind the bar so I wouldn't fidget with it.

She already had it out. She slid it across to me without a word.

"Thank you, Ruthie."

"Honey," she said quietly, "I've been doing this thirty-one years. I have seen a lot of anniversaries." She tipped her head toward the table. "That's the worst one."

"It's not over yet."

"Mm. That's what I'm afraid of."

I put the envelope inside my jacket and headed for the door. On my way past the host stand, the manager, a tall, silver-haired man named Vincent Bruno, stopped me.

"Mr. Carver. I'm so sorry about tonight." He kept his voice low. "Your card on file. The lady who called this afternoon added quite a bit to it. Champagne, the tasting menu. It comes to about nineteen hundred and forty dollars before gratuity. I wanted to ask you directly."

"Did I authorize any of that?"

"No, sir. You booked a table for two."

"Then I'd like to withdraw the card," I said. "Everything but my table."

"Understood." He nodded once, like a man who'd been hoping I'd say exactly that. "I'll let the party know."

Behind me, I heard the pop of a champagne cork, and Kendall's voice rising over the room. "To Simone! And to puppies!"

I walked out onto Broad Street into a warm April night, got into my truck, and sat there with my hands on the wheel. I didn't start it. I watched the door of Marchetti's through my windshield, and about ten minutes later I watched Vincent Bruno walk to the big table with a small black folder and lean down to say something to Kendall Ames.

I couldn't hear it. I didn't need to. I saw Kendall's mouth fall open. I saw her turn to Simone. I saw Simone reach into her purse for her phone.

My phone buzzed in the cupholder.

I started the truck.

I didn't go home that night. I want to be clear about why. It wasn't to punish anybody. It was because I knew if I walked in that door at midnight, she'd walk in at one, and we'd say things to each other that you can't take back, and I wanted to be the one who didn't say them. So I drove out Washington Road to the Hampton Inn, paid $189 for a room that smelled like carpet shampoo, turned my phone face down on the nightstand and slept like a dead man.

When I woke up at six-thirty, the room was gray and quiet, and my phone was lit up so bright it looked like a little television.

Seventy-three missed calls.

I sat on the edge of the bed and scrolled through them. Fifty-one from Simone. Nine from Kendall. Four from a number I didn't know. Two from Simone's mother. Seven from Marla, Simone's younger sister, all of them after two in the morning.

Under the calls were the texts. I read them in order, like a man reading a weather report after a storm already passed.

Simone, 11:31 p.m.: "Are you SERIOUS right now."

Simone, 11:34 p.m.: "They're saying your card won't cover it. Fix this."

Simone, 11:52 p.m.: "Kendall had to put $900 on her card. Do you know how embarrassing that is?"

Simone, 12:40 a.m.: "You humiliated me in front of my friends."

Simone, 1:15 a.m.: "Where are you. You're not home."

Simone, 2:02 a.m.: "Fine. Be a child."

Simone, 4:47 a.m.: "Elijah please call me."

There was one from Kendall that just said, "You owe me $900," which I thought showed real nerve.

And then there were Marla's. The first six were "Call me." The last one came in at 5:58 a.m., and it was longer.

"Elijah. Please call me before you call her. I need to tell you what that was. It was a bet. It wasn't the first time. I'm so sorry. I should have told you a year ago."

I looked at that message for a long time. Then I got up, made a cup of that awful hotel-room coffee, and called my sister-in-law.

Marla picked up on the first ring. She's 47, four years younger than Simone, a high school guidance counselor in Grovetown, and she has always been the one in that family who says the thing nobody else will say at Thanksgiving. I've liked her since the day I met her, when she was nineteen and told me I had good shoes for a man who worked in attics.

"Elijah," she said. "Oh, thank God. Are you okay?"

"I'm at the Hampton Inn drinking coffee that tastes like a penny. I'm fine. What bet, Marla?"

She took a breath. "Kendall bet Simone a hundred dollars that you'd leave after an hour. Simone bet you'd sit there all night. They were at Kendall's house drinking wine from six o'clock on. They were watching the clock. Kendall kept saying 'Check if he's still there,' and Simone would text you 'Running late, babe' and laugh."

I remembered those texts. "Running late, babe." Seven-forty. "Almost there." Eight-thirty. "Traffic, so sorry." Nine-fifteen. There's no traffic on a Saturday night in Augusta unless the Masters is in town, and the Masters had been over for a week.

"How do you know all that?" I asked.

"Because Simone sent the group text a photo of the clock on Kendall's microwave at ten o'clock with the caption 'Day three of puppy watch.' I'm in that group text. I've been in it two years. I keep meaning to leave and I never do." Her voice cracked a little. "They call you the Puppy in there. All of them. Have for a long time."

I didn't say anything. I watched a man in the parking lot outside load a cooler into a minivan.

"Elijah, it's not the first time she's done something like this. Remember your fiftieth birthday? When she said she was stuck at work and you ate at Olive Garden by yourself?"

"I remember."

"She was at Kendall's pool. They had a pitcher of margaritas. She posted a picture of you sitting alone in the booth that somebody at the next table sent her, because Paige's cousin was a server there." Marla was crying now, quietly. "And the lake trip in March, when she 'forgot' to tell you it was couples and you stood in that parking lot for an hour. And Christmas, the thing with your gift. That was a bet too."

"Marla."

"I know. I know. I'm so sorry."

"Why are you telling me now?"

"Because last night she called me at two in the morning screaming that you'd left her with the bill and humiliated her, and I asked her what happened, and she told me. All of it. And she was laughing, Elijah. In the middle of crying about the bill, she was laughing about the puppy line. She said the look on your face was 'priceless.'" Marla blew her nose. "And because there's something else, and I can't keep it in anymore."

"Go ahead."

"She was going to file. After the trip."

"What trip?"

"The anniversary trip. She thinks you're taking her somewhere big this year. She's been telling everybody. Kendall said you'd probably do Italy, since Simone's been talking about Rome for twenty years, and Simone said, 'If he does, I'll take the trip and then I'll file when we get home. At least I'll get Rome out of it.'"

My hand went to my jacket on the chair, to the inside pocket, where the cream-colored envelope was still sitting. Two first-class tickets, Atlanta to Rome, the second week of June. $3,800. I'd been putting money aside for those since last August, two hundred dollars at a time.

"Elijah? Are you still there?"

"I'm here."

"What are you going to do?"

I took the envelope out and set it on the nightstand next to my phone, which had just lit up again.

Simone. Call number seventy-four.

"I'm going to go home," I said. "And I'm going to answer her once."

Our house is a brick ranch on Wedgewood Drive with a carport I built myself and a heat pump I've rebuilt twice. When I pulled in a little after nine, Simone's Camry was in the carport, so Kendall must have driven her home. The front door was unlocked. She was sitting at the kitchen table in yesterday's makeup and one of my old T-shirts, with her phone in front of her and a cup of coffee she hadn't touched.

She stood up so fast her chair scraped the tile.

"Where were you?"

"Hampton Inn."

"All night? You couldn't answer one call? I thought you were dead in a ditch."

"You texted me 'Be a child' at two in the morning," I said. "People don't usually say that to a man in a ditch."

She crossed her arms. "Do you have any idea what you did to me last night?"

"I went home early from my own anniversary dinner."

"You left me with a two-thousand-dollar bill in front of my friends."

"Nineteen hundred and forty," I said. "And I didn't order any of it."

"It was a surprise, Elijah. For you. A party."

"For me."

"Yes, for you!"

"With a bet on how long I'd sit there."

She stopped. It was small, just a hitch, like a compressor that skips a beat before it catches. Then she came right back.

"Who told you that?"

"Does it matter?"

"It was a joke. It was a stupid joke. Kendall started it. You know how she is."

"You told her she owed you a hundred dollars. At the table. Out loud."

"Because it was funny!" Her voice went up. "You used to have a sense of humor. You used to be fun. Now you sit there like a... like..."

"Like a puppy," I said.

That one landed. She looked away first.

"You're making this into something it's not," she said.

"Okay. Let me make it into exactly what it is." I pulled out a chair and sat down across from her, and I kept my hands flat on the table the way I do when I'm explaining to a homeowner why their system needs replacing and not repairing. "Three things, Simone. One. You were three hours late to our anniversary on purpose, and you texted me 'traffic' while you were watching a clock at Kendall's house. Two. Your friends put nineteen hundred dollars on my card without asking me, and I said no. Three. You called me a pathetic puppy in front of a room full of people, and they laughed, and so did you."

"Elijah—"

"That's all of it. That's the whole thing. I'm not going to yell about it."

She stared at me. For a second I saw something real in her face, something scared. Then it was gone, and her chin came up the way it does.

"So what, you're mad. Fine. You'll get over it. You always get over it."

"Not this time."

"What does that mean?"

"It means I'm done waiting at tables."

She laughed, short and sharp. "Oh, so now you're leaving me? You? Where would you even go? You've lived in this house since 1998. You eat the same thing every Friday at the same diner. You can't even pick out your own shirts."

"I picked out that dress you wore last night."

"You're not leaving me, Elijah."

"I'm not leaving the house either," I said. "It's half mine. I'm going to be in the guest room until a lawyer tells me different. You can stay or you can go to Kendall's. That's up to you."

She grabbed her phone and her purse off the counter and stood there breathing hard.

"You're going to regret this," she said. "When you're sitting alone in some apartment eating microwave dinners, you're going to remember this."

"I sat alone for three hours last night," I said. "I got pretty good at it."

The front door slammed hard enough that the wreath fell off. A minute later the Camry backed out of the carport too fast and clipped the trash can on the way.

I picked up the wreath and hung it back on its nail.

Monday morning, I was standing outside the Georgia Bank and Trust on Walton Way at five minutes to seven, before they unlocked the doors. The teller was a nice young woman who looked like she hadn't had her coffee yet either.

"I'd like to open an account in my name only," I said. "And I'd like to move my direct deposit into it."

"Of course, sir. Will you be closing the joint account?"

"No ma'am. Leave her money where it is. I just want mine coming in somewhere else from now on."

It took twenty minutes. Simone's paycheck from the dental office still went to the joint account. So did half the bills, the way it always had. I didn't touch a penny of what was in there. I've seen men clean out a joint account the morning after a fight, and every one of them looked like a thief in front of the judge. I just stopped pouring my own money into a bucket somebody else was using to buy champagne.

By ten o'clock I'd called a lawyer. By Friday I'd have an appointment. And by Wednesday, the first of Simone's friends had found her way to my shop.

It wasn't Kendall. It was Paige Dorsett.

She came in at lunchtime, when it was just me and Dot, my office manager, who's sixty-eight and has worked for me for fifteen years. Paige stood in the doorway in her hospital scrubs, holding her car keys in both hands like a steering wheel.

"Elijah," she said. "I'm not here for Simone. I need to say something, and then I'll go."

Dot found an urgent reason to be in the parts room.

"Go ahead, Paige."

"I'm sorry." Her face went red. "I didn't laugh. I want you to know that. But I didn't say anything either, and I was standing right there, and I've been in that group chat for two years, and I never said anything there either. That's the same thing as laughing. My husband told me so last night, and he's right."

"Your husband sounds like a smart man."

"He said if anybody ever made a bet on how long he'd wait for me, he'd want somebody to tell him." She took a breath. "So I'm telling you. It wasn't just last night. It's been going on a long time. Kendall started calling you the Puppy at the Fourth of July thing two years ago, because you brought Simone a plate and waited by her chair. And Simone laughed, and it just... stuck."

"I know about the chat, Paige."

She looked surprised, then relieved, then ashamed all over again. "Okay. Then I just wanted you to know I left it. Last night. I left the group."

"Thank you."

"And Elijah? Kendall's telling everybody you stuck Simone with a two-thousand-dollar bill out of spite. That you've always been cheap and controlling with money."

"Is anybody believing her?"

Paige almost smiled. "Not the people who were there."

She left. Dot came back out of the parts room carrying exactly nothing.

"Well," Dot said. "That was the first decent thing a friend of hers has done in twenty years."

"She's not a friend of hers anymore."

"Then that's two decent things."

Kendall came on Thursday.

She didn't come to the shop. She came to a job I was running in Evans, a two-story colonial where I was swapping out an old fifteen-year-old system for a new one, and she found it because my truck has my name painted on both doors and Evans isn't that big. I was on my knees in the side yard setting a condenser pad when a white Lexus pulled up to the curb and Kendall Ames got out in big sunglasses and a tennis skirt.

"Elijah. We need to talk."

"I'm working, Kendall."

"This'll take two minutes." She walked right across the homeowner's grass in her white sneakers. "You need to call Simone. She's been sleeping in my guest room for five days, and she's a wreck."

"She's got a phone."

"She's called you a hundred times!"

"I answered once."

"You answered once and told her you were done. After twenty-seven years. Over a joke."

I set down my level. "Kendall, you called my wife's anniversary dinner 'puppy watch.' You bet a hundred dollars on how long I'd sit there. You put champagne on my credit card. Which part was the joke?"

She pushed her sunglasses up on her head. "Oh, don't you play the victim. You know what you did? You made her pay for that dinner. You made me pay for it. Nine hundred dollars, Elijah, on my card. And Tami's still not speaking to me because she had to put four hundred on hers, and she's a widow on a fixed income."

"Then maybe Tami shouldn't have ordered the tasting menu."

"It was supposed to be your treat!"

"Says who?"

"Says Simone!" Kendall threw her hands up. "She said you'd pay. She said you always pay. She said, and I quote, 'He'll pick up the check and say thank you for coming.'"

I looked at her for a long moment.

"She was right about that for twenty-seven years," I said. "She just picked the wrong night to bet on it."

Kendall's face went tight. She leaned in. "You know what she says about you? She says being married to you is like being married to a recliner. Comfortable. Always there. Never goes anywhere."

"Then I guess she won't mind if this recliner gets up."

The homeowner, a retired schoolteacher named Mrs. Lattimore, had come out on her front porch with a glass of iced tea and was watching us with open interest. Kendall noticed her and lowered her voice.

"Simone is going to take you for half of everything," she said. "Half the house. Half your precious business. Half your truck. And I'm going to help her."

"The business was mine before I married her. My lawyer's going to tell her that. You can help her hear it."

"We'll see." Kendall pulled her sunglasses back down. "And you still owe me nine hundred dollars."

"Send me the bill," I said. "I'll put it next to the one for my anniversary."

She stomped back across Mrs. Lattimore's lawn and peeled away from the curb. Mrs. Lattimore took a long sip of her tea.

"Young man," she said, "I taught eighth grade for thirty-four years. I know a mean girl when I see one. That was a mean girl at fifty."

"Yes ma'am."

"Would you like some tea?"

"I'd love some."

Tuesday of the next week, nine days after the anniversary, I came home from a service call at a quarter past six and there were two cars in front of my house. Simone's Camry. And a burgundy Buick I knew too well.

Simone's mother, Irene, was sitting in my porch swing like it was a throne. Simone was standing behind her, arms crossed, wearing sunglasses at six in the evening, which in that family means somebody's been crying or wants you to think they have.

Irene is 76. She taught Sunday school at Trinity for forty years, raised three daughters on a mail carrier's salary after her husband died, and has never once in my life said a sentence she didn't mean. She used to like me. I wasn't sure which way she'd come down.

"Elijah," she said. "Sit."

I sat on the porch step below her.

"My daughter tells me you've abandoned her," Irene said. "Over a joke at dinner."

"Yes ma'am. That's what she'd tell you."

"Is it true?"

"I'm sleeping in the guest room of my own house. She's sleeping at Kendall Ames's. I don't know who abandoned who."

Simone made a noise behind her mother. "Mama, he's twisting it. This is what he does."

Irene held up one finger without turning around, and Simone stopped. Some powers don't fade with age.

"Elijah," Irene said. "I've known you twenty-nine years. Tell me what happened at that restaurant. Just what happened."

So I told her. The seven-thirty reservation. The texts about traffic. Three hours and four minutes. The friends, the bet, the champagne on my card. And then, word for word, the line.

When I got to "pathetic puppy," I watched Irene's face. She didn't gasp. She didn't do anything. She just got very still, the way a woman does who's spent forty years watching children lie to her in Sunday school.

"Simone," she said, without turning around. "Did you say that?"

"Mama, it was a joke, everybody was laughing—"

"Did you say it?"

"...Yes."

"In front of people."

"It was Kendall's idea!"

"I didn't ask whose idea it was." Irene turned in the swing, slowly, and took a long look at her daughter. "Your daddy waited for me at the bus station in Waycross for four hours once in 1968 because my bus broke down outside Jesup. He was holding a box of chocolates the whole time. They melted. He gave them to me anyway." Her voice was very quiet. "If anybody had laughed at him for it, I'd have slapped them. Even if it was me."

"Mama—"

"I'm not finished." Irene turned back to me. "Elijah. Is there anything she can do?"

"No ma'am."

"Nothing?"

"I've been waiting twenty-seven years, Irene. Last Saturday I found out somebody was holding a stopwatch."

Irene nodded slowly. She put her hand on the arm of the swing and pushed herself up. She was shorter than I remembered. She came down the two steps and put her hand on my cheek, the way she used to do when Simone and I came for Sunday dinner, and she said, "I'm sorry, son."

Then she walked to her Buick.

"Mama!" Simone came down off the porch after her. "Mama, you're supposed to be on my side!"

"I'm on the side of whoever's telling the truth," Irene said, and opened her car door. "That's never been a hard side to find in this family. It just isn't usually yours."

The Buick pulled away. Simone stood in my front yard with her sunglasses in her hand, staring after it like she'd been left at a bus station.

She turned around and looked at me. Then she looked past me, at my kitchen window, where the cream-colored envelope was propped against the toaster. I'd taken it out that morning to call the airline and hadn't put it away.

"What's that?" she said.

I didn't answer.

She walked right past me up the steps, through the front door, into the kitchen. I heard the envelope tear. I heard nothing for a long moment. Then I heard her voice, very small, from the kitchen.

"Elijah. These are for Rome."

I went in. She was standing at the counter with the two tickets in one hand and the itinerary in the other. First class, Atlanta to Rome, June the ninth. A hotel near the Spanish Steps. A day trip to Florence with a cooking class, because twenty-seven years ago, on our honeymoon in Gatlinburg, she'd told me that someday she wanted to learn to make real pasta from an Italian grandmother.

"You remembered that," she said.

"I remember most things."

"You were going to take me to Rome." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet for real now. "For our anniversary. That's what was in your jacket. All night."

"With the bartender. From eight o'clock on."

"Why didn't you give it to me?"

"You were three hours late, Simone. And when you got there, you were busy."

She pressed the tickets against her chest. "Okay. Okay. I was awful. I was drunk and awful and Kendall egged me on, and I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. Can we just... we can still go. June ninth. We'll go and we'll talk and we'll fix it."

"I cancelled them this morning."

She blinked. "What?"

"I booked them refundable. Just in case something came up. Something came up." I took my coffee cup out of the sink and rinsed it. "The refund goes back to my card in seven to ten business days."

"You can't cancel Rome."

"I already did."

"That was my trip!"

"It was our trip. And Marla tells me you were planning to take it and then file for divorce when you got home." I set the cup in the drainer. "'At least I'll get Rome out of it.' That's what you said, isn't it?"

Her whole face went still, like somebody had flipped a breaker. She didn't ask how I knew. She knew how I knew. There was only one person in her life who'd tell me that.

"Marla," she said. "That little traitor."

"She's your sister. She cried for an hour telling me."

"She's always been jealous of me."

"Simone." I dried my hands. "You bet on me. You bet I'd wait. You won. You collected your hundred dollars. I just don't play anymore."

She dropped the tickets on the counter. They slid off onto the floor, and neither of us picked them up.

"What are you going to do with the money?" she asked, in a voice that had gone flat and hard.

"There's a 2011 Tracker bass boat sitting in Gil Henley's side yard out in Harlem. Seventeen feet, runs good, he wants thirty-one hundred. I've driven past it every week for two years."

"A boat."

"A boat."

"You'd rather have a boat than Rome with your wife."

"I'd rather have a boat," I said, "than wait three hours for somebody who's laughing at me."

She picked up her purse and left without saying another word. She didn't slam the door that time. That bothered me more than if she had.

Saturday morning, I hitched that Tracker to my truck and pulled it home, and I sat in it in the carport with a cup of coffee and a radio playing the Allman Brothers, and I laughed out loud at nothing for about a minute. Dot would have said I'd finally lost it. Dot would have been half right.

The ladder started that week. Every day a new rung.

Sunday it was nostalgia. Simone sent a photo of our wedding cake, the one with the little bride and groom on top that we still had in a box in the attic. "Remember this?" I did. I didn't answer.

Monday it was the honeymoon. A picture of us at Dollywood in 1999, me with a sunburn like a stop sign. "We were so happy." Twelve missed calls that day. I counted them on my lunch break in the truck.

Tuesday, she showed up at seven in the evening on my porch with a bag of takeout from the Chinese place on Washington Road, the General Tso's I've ordered every Friday for twenty years. She rang the bell for forty minutes. I was in the back, in the boat, changing the lower unit oil. I could hear the bell the whole time. Eventually I heard her car door.

When I came around front, the bag was sitting on the welcome mat. There was a note stapled to it. "You can't stay mad at me forever. You love me. You always come back."

I ate the General Tso's. It would've been a sin not to. I threw the note away.

Wednesday, nineteen missed calls.

Thursday, Day sixteen since the restaurant, I was on a roof in Martinez at eleven in the morning when my phone buzzed with a text from Simone. Just a text, which was new.

"Having chest pains. Can't breathe. Please come. I'm scared."

I sat down on the roof next to the condenser I was mounting. I'm not a cold man. My first instinct, after twenty-seven years, was to get off that roof and get in the truck. I had one foot on the ladder.

Then I stopped, took out my phone and called Marla instead.

"Elijah? What's wrong?"

"Simone just texted me she's having chest pains. Is somebody with her?"

There was a pause on the other end. Then Marla said, very carefully, "Elijah, I'm looking at her right now."

"What?"

"Kendall's having a pool thing for her birthday. Simone invited me because she's trying to make up with me. I came to keep the peace." I heard the splash of somebody jumping in. "She's in a lounge chair with a frozen margarita. She was just showing Kendall her phone and they were giggling."

"Okay," I said.

"Elijah, I'm so sorry."

"Don't be. Tell her I hope her chest feels better."

"I'm not telling her that."

"That's fine, Marla."

I hung up. I finished mounting the condenser. Then I texted Simone back, the only text I'd sent her in sixteen days.

"Marla says you look fine. Enjoy the pool."

She called me eleven times between noon and one o'clock. On the twelfth call, I answered.

"You had my sister spy on me?" she said. No hello.

"I called your sister because you said you couldn't breathe."

"You should have come yourself!"

"If I'd come myself, I'd be standing at a pool party right now, wouldn't I? With Kendall filming."

She didn't say anything.

"That's what I thought," I said. "Goodbye, Simone."

My lawyer, a no-nonsense woman named Harriet Dunbar who keeps her reading glasses on a chain and calls everybody "Mister" no matter how long she's known them, filed the divorce petition that Friday. A process server handed Simone the papers at the front desk of Dr. Bell's dental office on Monday morning, in front of two patients and a hygienist. I didn't arrange that part. Harriet said it's simply where people are on Monday mornings.

I heard about it the same way I hear about most things in Augusta. Dot's cousin's daughter was the hygienist.

Six weeks after our anniversary, on a Saturday night, I walked back into Marchetti's on Broad Street.

I wasn't there for any reason except that I'd liked the bourbon and I'd liked Ruthie, and because a man ought to be able to sit in a restaurant without remembering the worst three hours of his life every time he drives past it. I took the stool at the end of the bar. Ruthie looked up, and her face did something nice.

"Well, look who came back," she said. "The usual?"

"I've only been here once."

"Honey, you made an impression. The usual's a Maker's, neat, and a basket of bread you're actually going to eat this time."

She brought both. Caleb walked by with a tray, saw me, and grinned like I was his favorite uncle. Vincent Bruno came out from the back and shook my hand without saying a word about anything. I like a man who knows when not to talk.

I was about halfway through the bourbon when the front door opened and Simone walked in.

She was alone.

She looked thinner. Her hair was pulled back in a way she never wore it, like she'd done it in somebody else's bathroom. She saw me at the bar and stopped just inside the door, and I knew, the way you know a storm's coming by the smell, that she hadn't come there by accident. It was a small town. Somebody had seen my truck.

She walked over and stood beside my stool. Ruthie, without seeming to move, drifted down the bar until she was close enough to polish a glass that didn't need polishing. At the two-top behind me, an older couple stopped talking.

"Hi," Simone said.

"Hi."

"Can I sit?"

"It's a free country. It's not my stool."

She sat. She put her purse on the bar and didn't open it.

"I'm staying at Marla's now," she said. "Kendall put my things on her porch on Wednesday. In trash bags. She said I owed her rent and nine hundred dollars and she was tired of me." She laughed, but there was nothing in it. "She posted about it. 'Some people don't know how to be a guest.' Everybody commented."

I didn't say anything. I didn't need to. Ruthie set a glass of water in front of Simone without being asked.

"Elijah, I came to ask you something, and I need you to let me finish."

"Okay."

"I was wrong. About everything. The restaurant, the bet, the... the thing I said. The puppy thing." Her voice wobbled on the word. "I've said it to myself every night for six weeks. 'Told you he'd still be here.' I thought it made me look like I had the upper hand. Like I had a husband who'd wait for me no matter what. I didn't understand that was the best thing about you. I made it a joke. I made it a bet." She turned on the stool to face me. People at the tables were openly listening now. She didn't care. "I want one more chance. Just one. I'll do counseling, I'll quit Kendall forever, I'll do anything. Please. I'm asking you in front of all these people, because I humiliated you in front of all these people. Please."

The room was quiet. Even the kitchen seemed quiet.

I set my glass down.

"Simone," I said. "Six weeks ago, you walked in that door and told your friends I'd still be here like a pathetic puppy."

She closed her eyes.

"You were right. I was still here. For twenty-seven years, I was still here. At every table. At every birthday. In every parking lot you forgot to come to." I picked up my glass again. "But a puppy waits because it doesn't know any better. I do now."

"Elijah—"

"The answer's no. I'm sorry, and I mean that. But it's no."

She sat there for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and picked up her purse and got off the stool. She walked to the door without looking back, and the bell over it jingled when she went out.

Nobody at the bar said anything for a while. The older couple went back to their salads. Caleb went back to his tray. I finished my bourbon.

Ruthie came down the bar and took my empty glass, and set a fresh one in front of me that I hadn't ordered.

"That one's on the house," she said, loud enough for the whole bar. "Thirty-one years behind this bar, and that's the first time I ever saw somebody stop waiting."
