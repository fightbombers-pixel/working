"I crashed your car. Don't be mad. My ex was driving and we were just catching up."

My girlfriend sent me that at seven-fifteen on a Sunday morning, and I read it standing on my own car lot on Asher Avenue, with my lot manager on one side of me and a young couple from Benton on the other, both of them waiting for me to tell them what I'd take for a 2016 Camry.

Three people watched me read it. Junior read it over my shoulder. The young wife from Benton read it too, because I was holding the phone at about her eye level, and she said "Oh no" before I did.

The car she meant was a 1970 Chevelle SS with a 454 under the hood. My father bought it new the year I was born. I spent six years and a little over $28,000 bringing it back from a rust bucket in a barn in Conway to the prettiest thing on four wheels in Pulaski County. I'd let her borrow it for one night, so she could drive her mother to a church dinner in style.

I typed back with one thumb.

"Is everyone safe?"

Then I dialed my insurance company's claims line, put it on speaker, and set the phone on the hood of the Camry where all three of them could hear it ring.

Thirty-one days later, her ex was sitting at a long table in a downtown law office with a stack of papers in front of him, and his boss was sitting right next to him, and the number on the bottom of the top page was $40,000.

What she didn't know when she sent that text was what was hanging on a nail by my office door.

My name is Reggie Dawson. I'm 57, and I own Dawson Motors, a used car lot on Asher Avenue in Little Rock, Arkansas. Forty cars on a good week, mostly sedans and pickups for working people who need something that starts in the morning. I've been selling cars for thirty-three years, and the one thing I know for certain is that every car has a paper trail. Who had the keys, when, and for how long. A car can't lie about where it's been. Remember that.

"State Farm claims, this is Gerald."

"Gerald, my name's Reggie Dawson. I need to report an accident involving my car. I wasn't driving and I wasn't there."

"Was anyone injured, sir?"

"I don't know yet," I said. "I asked."

My phone buzzed against the Camry's hood while Gerald was typing. Felicia.

"Everyone's fine. We're at the hospital but it's just bruises. Please don't be mad. Quincy feels terrible."

"The driver," I said to Gerald, "is a man named Quincy Barlow. I don't know him. I've never met him. He's my girlfriend's ex-husband."

Junior Matlock, my lot manager, let out a long, slow whistle. He's 33, built like a refrigerator, and he's been working for me since he was a teenager washing cars for gas money.

"Ex-husband," Junior said. "She said ex. She didn't say ex-husband. That's a whole different animal."

"Sir," Gerald said, "did you give Mr. Barlow permission to drive your vehicle?"

"No."

"Did you give the person you loaned it to permission to let anyone else drive it?"

"No," I said. "In writing."

There was a little pause on the line.

"In writing?"

"I'll explain when your adjuster calls me."

Gerald gave me a claim number. I wrote it on the back of one of my business cards. The young couple from Benton were still standing there, very politely pretending they hadn't heard every word.

"I'm sorry," the wife said. "Do you want us to come back another time?"

"No, ma'am," I said. "The Camry's ninety-two hundred. For you, eighty-eight. It's got new tires and the timing belt was done at ninety thousand."

They bought it. I did the paperwork with my hands perfectly steady. Junior watched me the whole time like a man watching a dog that's about to bite somebody.

When they drove off, he said, "Boss. You okay?"

"No."

"You want to go to the hospital?"

"No. I want to go see my car."

Felicia had texted me the name of the tow yard. Arkansas Wrecker on Roosevelt Road. A trooper had called it from the scene on Highway 10 out past Pinnacle Mountain.

"You want me to come?" Junior asked.

"Somebody's got to watch the lot."

"Lot's closed on Sunday, boss. We're only here because you came in to sell the Camry." He was already pulling his keys out. "I'm driving. You're not driving angry in a borrowed truck."

The Chevelle was sitting in the back corner of the wrecker yard under a sycamore tree, and when I saw it, I had to stop walking.

The front end was pushed back almost to the windshield. The passenger side was caved in like somebody had hit it with a wrecking ball. Cortez Silver paint I'd had sprayed in a booth in North Little Rock for $4,200 was peeled and scraped down to gray primer. The chrome bumper was bent in a V. Glass all over the seats. On the dash, the little framed picture of my father standing next to this same car in 1970 was face down on the floor mat.

I reached in through the broken window and picked it up. The glass in the frame was cracked straight through my father's face.

"Oh, man," Junior said quietly. "Oh, Reggie. I'm sorry."

A tow yard man came over wiping his hands. "You the owner?"

"Yes, sir."

"Trooper said it went off the road on that curve by the Maumelle bridge about two in the morning. Took out the fence at Hendrix Tire and their whole sign. Rolled it once and landed on its wheels. Folks inside walked away. That's a tough old car."

"It was."

"It's a total," he said, not unkindly. "Frame's bent. You're not fixing that."

"I know."

A dark gray Arkansas State Police cruiser pulled into the lot then, and a trooper got out with a clipboard. He was tall and young and had a crew cut you could set a level on.

"Mr. Dawson? I'm Trooper Neal Coffman. I worked the wreck. Thought you'd want a copy of the report, and I've got a couple of questions."

"I've got a couple too."

He handed me the report. I read it standing in the gravel. Driver: Quincy L. Barlow, age 41. Passenger: Felicia A. Grant, age 39. Vehicle owner: Reginald Dawson. Time of crash: a few minutes before two in the morning. Speed: estimated 70 in a 45.

"Seventy," I said.

"At least," Trooper Coffman said. "He told me a deer ran out. I didn't see any deer tracks. I saw skid marks that started about a hundred feet after the curve."

"Was he drinking?"

"He blew under the limit. Barely. She'd been drinking more than him. That's probably why he was driving." Coffman tapped one line on the report. "But here's what you need to know, Mr. Dawson. I cited him for driving on a suspended license. His license was suspended in March for unpaid tickets in Saline County. He told me he didn't know. They always say they didn't know."

"So he shouldn't have been driving anything."

"He shouldn't have been driving a shopping cart." Coffman looked at the Chevelle. "Did you give him permission to drive your car?"

"I've never met the man."

"That's what I figured." He wrote something down. "She told me at the scene the car belonged to her boyfriend. Then she said 'please don't tell Reggie.' Then she cried for about twenty minutes."

Junior made a noise in his throat.

"Where are they now?" I asked.

"Baptist Health on Kanis. She's got a cut on her head, a few stitches. He's got a sprained wrist. They'll both be out by noon." The trooper hesitated. "Mr. Dawson. Can I give you some advice I'm not supposed to give?"

"Go ahead."

"Talk to your insurance company before you talk to either of them. Don't sign anything she brings you. And keep that report somewhere safe."

"I've got a place," I said.

Felicia called me at a quarter past one. She'd been released. She wanted to come to my house. She wanted to explain.

I met her at the curb, not inside. She got out of her sister's car with a white bandage on her forehead and her arm in a cheap sling, and her sister stayed in the driver's seat with the engine running and her sunglasses on, staring straight ahead.

Felicia is 39, a pharmacy tech at the Walgreens on Cantrell, with dark curly hair and a dimple when she smiles. We'd been together almost two years. I'd met her when she came on my lot looking for a Corolla with low miles, and I'd sold her one for six hundred less than I should have, and she'd laughed when she figured that out and asked me to dinner.

She wasn't smiling now.

"Reggie, baby, I'm so sorry. I'm so, so sorry."

"Are you okay?"

"Four stitches. I'm fine. Quincy's wrist is sprained." She looked at my face. "You're mad."

"I asked you if everyone was safe."

"I know. That's so you. That's so sweet. I knew you wouldn't freak out."

"Felicia, why was your ex-husband driving my father's car at two in the morning on Highway 10?"

"He's not my ex-husband," she said fast. "I mean, he is. But it was eleven years ago. We were married for like a year. It's ancient history."

"That's not what I asked."

She took a breath. "Okay. After the church dinner I took Mom home, and then Quincy texted me. He's been going through a really hard time. His mom's sick. He just wanted to talk. So I met him at the Cajun's Wharf, just to talk, I swear, and he saw the Chevelle in the parking lot and he went nuts over it. He used to be a car guy. And he asked if he could drive it, just around the block, and I said no, Reggie would kill me. And he kept asking. And it was late and I'd had a couple of drinks and I thought, okay, it's better if he drives than me."

"Around the block."

"Around the block."

"Highway 10 is eleven miles from Cajun's Wharf."

She didn't have an answer for that.

"He didn't have a license," I said. "Did you know that?"

"What?"

"It was suspended in March. The trooper cited him."

Her face went blank. "He didn't tell me that."

"He didn't tell the trooper either."

"Reggie, I swear I didn't know. Okay? I didn't know. And it was an accident. A deer ran out."

"The trooper says there wasn't any deer."

Her sister honked the horn once, short. Felicia jumped.

"Okay," Felicia said, "so what happens now? Your insurance pays for it, right? That's what insurance is for. I mean, it's a lot of money, but you've got full coverage on it, right? I've heard you talk about it a hundred times. Agreed value."

"It does," I said. "Thirty-one five, agreed value."

"Okay. So it's fine. They pay, you get your money, and maybe you find another one. Right?"

"Maybe."

She stepped in close, put her good hand on my chest. "I love you. I'm so sorry about your dad's car. I'll make it up to you."

"Felicia."

"Yeah?"

"Did Quincy ask you anything about my insurance? Last night?"

Her hand stopped moving on my chest. Just for a second. Then she laughed, a little too high.

"What? No. Why would he ask about your insurance?"

"No reason," I said.

She kissed my cheek and got back in her sister's car. Her sister pulled away from the curb without ever once turning her head toward me.

I went inside and put my father's cracked picture on the kitchen table. Then I sat there and looked at it for a long time. My phone buzzed. It was a number I didn't know.

"Mr. Dawson? This is Denise Whitmire, State Farm. I'm the adjuster on your Chevelle claim. I'd like to come by your lot tomorrow morning, if that works." A pause. "And I should tell you, I already got a call about this claim today. Not from you. From a man named Quincy Barlow. He wanted to know if you'd listed him as a permissive driver."

Denise Whitmire showed up at Dawson Motors at nine sharp Monday morning in a white State Farm Malibu with a tablet under her arm. She was in her fifties, with gray braids pinned up and the steady, tired eyes of a woman who has heard every kind of lie a person can tell about a car wreck.

Junior offered her coffee. She took it black.

"I'm going to be honest with you, Mr. Dawson," she said, sitting down in the customer chair in my office. "This claim is about to get complicated. And it's going to get complicated because of one question. Who was allowed to drive your car?"

"Felicia was. For one night. Nobody else."

"That's what you say. Here's what Mr. Barlow says." She tapped her tablet. "He called our claims line yesterday at four in the afternoon. He said, and I'm reading the note, 'The owner told his girlfriend she could let anybody drive it. He's a car dealer, he lets people drive his cars all the time.'"

"He's never met me."

"I know. That's what made me curious."

"Why does it matter so much to him?"

Denise leaned back.

"Because," she said, "if Mr. Barlow was a permissive driver, then under your policy he counts as an insured. And we can't go after our own insured. We'd pay you for the car, we'd pay Hendrix Tire for their fence and their sign, and that would be the end of it. Mr. Barlow walks away clean." She took a sip of coffee. "But if he wasn't permitted, he's just a man who took somebody else's car and wrecked it. Then we pay you, and we turn around and sue him for every penny. That's called subrogation. It means we step into your shoes and collect from the person who caused the damage."

"How much would that be?"

"Your agreed value is thirty-one five. Hendrix Tire's fence and sign come to about eighty-five hundred. Call it forty thousand." She set the tablet on my desk. "Forty thousand dollars is a lot of money to a paralegal with a suspended license. So he's very, very interested in what you told your girlfriend."

"He's a paralegal?"

"At Stidham and Rowe. Downtown. He mentioned it twice on the phone. He wanted me to know he understands how this works."

Junior, who was standing in the doorway, said, "Oh, he understands how it works. That's why he's scared."

Denise looked at me. "So. What did you tell her? Exactly. Because if it comes down to your word against theirs, it's two against one."

I got up, walked to the door of my office, and took the clipboard off the nail.

Every car that leaves my lot without me in it gets logged on that clipboard. Test drives, loaners, my own cars, I don't care. Who took the keys, what car, what time, and who's allowed behind the wheel. My father kept one at his Texaco station in Pine Bluff, and I've kept one for thirty-three years. Customers make fun of it. Felicia made fun of it too.

I set it down in front of Denise and flipped back two pages.

There it was, Saturday evening, in Felicia's loopy handwriting, with a little heart dotting the i.

"Saturday 5:40 p.m. 1970 Chevelle SS. Keys out to: Felicia Grant. Back by: Sunday noon. Authorized drivers: Felicia only. No other drivers." And under that, her signature, and under that, mine.

Denise read it twice. Then she took a picture of it with her tablet, and then another one, closer.

"She wrote 'no other drivers' herself?"

"I made her. She laughed at me. She said, 'Reggie, who else would I let drive it, the Pope?'"

Denise didn't smile, but something in her face loosened.

"Mr. Dawson," she said, "I need you to keep this clipboard somewhere very safe. Don't make copies for anybody but me. Don't show it to your girlfriend. And don't tell Mr. Barlow it exists."

"Why not?"

"Because people who are about to owe forty thousand dollars do stupid things." She stood up and shook my hand. "We'll process your payment this week. You'll get the agreed value, minus your five hundred deductible. Then our subrogation lawyer is going to want to talk to you."

After she left, Junior came in and sat on the edge of my desk.

"The Pope," he said. "She said the Pope."

"She did."

"Boss, that clipboard just saved you forty thousand dollars. Well. Saved State Farm. Same thing."

"It didn't save anything. It's just the truth."

"Yeah. Well. The truth's got her signature on it with a little heart."

My phone buzzed. Felicia.

I picked up.

"Reggie," she said. She was talking fast and low, like somebody was in the room with her. "Baby, I need to ask you a huge favor. The insurance lady is going to call you. Or she already did. Quincy says if you tell them you gave me permission to let other people drive, everything just goes away. Insurance pays, it's done, nobody gets sued. That's all you have to say. You told me I could let somebody else drive if I had to."

"I didn't tell you that."

"I know, but you could have. It's basically the same thing. You're so laid back about cars."

"I'm not laid back about that car."

"Reggie, please. Quincy says if they sue him he'll lose his apartment. He says his boss could fire him. He's got a sick mother." Her voice cracked. "Please. For me. It's just one little sentence."

"Felicia," I said. "Is he there right now?"

Silence.

"Is Quincy standing next to you while you're asking me this?"

"No," she said. Then, quieter, "He's in the kitchen."

"In your kitchen."

"He came over to check on my head."

"At ten in the morning. With a sprained wrist."

"Reggie..."

"I'm not going to lie to an insurance company for him, Felicia. And I'm not going to lie for you either."

"I'm not asking you to lie! I'm asking you to remember it differently!"

"I remember it exactly right," I said. "Is everyone safe over there?"

"What? What does that even mean?"

"It means I hope your head's okay," I said, and hung up.

Quincy Barlow came to my lot nine days later, on a Wednesday afternoon, in a white Nissan Altima driven by a heavyset friend who stayed in the car with the engine running.

I knew it was him before he said a word. He had a navy blue wrist brace on his right arm and the kind of good gray suit a paralegal buys at the Dillard's outlet to look like a lawyer. He was 41, tall and thin, with a careful little beard and a leather portfolio under his good arm.

There were three customers on the lot. A retired teacher looking at a Buick, a young guy kicking tires on an F-150, and a mother with a little boy who was climbing in and out of a minivan. Junior was with the teacher. I was standing by the office door.

Quincy walked straight up to me and put out his left hand, since the right one was in the brace.

"Mr. Dawson. Quincy Barlow. I think we need to have a conversation, man to man."

I didn't shake it. "About what?"

"About the Chevelle. About making this whole thing go away for everybody." He smiled. He had very white teeth. "I work in the law, Mr. Dawson. I've seen a hundred of these. Here's the thing. You've got your insurance money coming. You're made whole. There's no reason for anybody else to get hurt."

"Anybody else. Like who?"

"Like me. Like Felicia. If State Farm sues me, they'll depose Felicia. She'll have to testify. Under oath. In front of a court reporter. About what she was doing out at two in the morning with her ex-husband." He spread his left hand. "Do you really want to put her through that? Do you want to hear all that, Mr. Dawson? On the record?"

"I already know what she was doing. You told the trooper. She told me."

"Not all of it," he said, and smiled a little wider.

The young guy by the F-150 had stopped kicking tires. The mother had her hand on her boy's shoulder.

"Here's what I'm asking," Quincy said, softer, like we were friends. "One sentence. You tell your adjuster you told Felicia she could let somebody else drive in an emergency. That's all. She'd had a few drinks. I was sober. It was safer for me to drive. You'd want her to be safe, right? That's what you texted her. 'Is everyone safe.' That shows you cared about safety more than the car."

"You read my texts?"

"She showed me."

"You wrecked my father's car at seventy miles an hour on a suspended license."

"Allegedly seventy."

"With skid marks a hundred feet past the curve."

"A deer."

"There wasn't any deer."

"You weren't there."

"Neither was the deer," Junior said from over by the Buick. The retired teacher laughed out loud and then covered her mouth.

Quincy's smile slipped.

"Look," he said. "I'm trying to be reasonable. If you won't do it the easy way, Felicia's going to tell them you gave her permission. She'll swear to it. And then it's your word against hers, and she was there when you handed her the keys, and you weren't there at the wreck, and a jury looks at a nice pharmacy tech with stitches in her head and they look at a used car salesman."

"Used car dealer."

"Same thing to a jury."

"Is it?"

"Twelve people off the street? Believe me. I work for lawyers. I know juries." He leaned in. "So here's my question. Where do you keep your paperwork? Your title, your insurance card, your registration. Because my boss is going to want copies for discovery, and I'd hate to have to subpoena your whole office."

I looked at him for a long, long moment.

"My paperwork's in my office," I said. "Where it belongs."

"Good. Great." He stepped back and adjusted his wrist brace. "Think about it, Mr. Dawson. One sentence. Nobody gets hurt. Or you can make this ugly, and Felicia's going to be the one who gets hurt the worst."

He walked back to the Altima. The heavyset friend backed out so fast he almost clipped my sign.

Junior came over. "Boss. Did he just ask where you keep your files?"

"He did."

"In front of four witnesses."

"Five, counting the kid."

Junior looked at my office door. At the nail where the clipboard used to hang before I put it in the safe.

"I'm sleeping here tonight," he said.

"You don't have to do that."

"I know I don't. I've got a cot in the detail bay and a bag of Funyuns. I'm sleeping here."

It took two nights.

Friday, a little after two in the morning, I was asleep on the couch in my office with my boots on, because Junior had bullied me into taking the second shift, when I heard glass break in the front showroom.

I didn't turn on a light. I've walked that floor in the dark a thousand times. I picked up the big Maglite I keep under my desk and stepped out into the showroom.

Somebody had busted out the little window in the side door and reached in to flip the deadbolt. A man in a black hoodie was crouched by my sales desk with a flashlight in his teeth, yanking open drawers and throwing folders on the floor. He had a navy blue brace on his right wrist.

"Looking for something, Quincy?"

He spun around. The flashlight fell out of his mouth and rolled under a desk.

For a second neither of us moved. Then he came at me.

He had a tire iron. I didn't see it until it was swinging at my head.

I got the Maglite up just in time. The iron hit it with a clang that rang all the way down my arm into my shoulder, and the Maglite went flying across the showroom and the light spun crazy circles on the ceiling. He swung again, backhand, and caught me on the left forearm, and I felt something in there go hot and wrong.

He was younger than me and he was scared, and scared men swing hard. But he was swinging with his left hand, because his right was in a brace. And I've spent thirty-three years pulling transmissions and wrestling batteries and hauling tires.

The third time he swung, I stepped in close, inside the arc, where a long iron is no good to anybody. I grabbed his left wrist with my good hand and drove my shoulder into his chest and we both went over my sales desk. Folders and pens and a stapler went everywhere. The tire iron hit the floor and slid under the desk with the flashlight.

He clawed at my face. I got my knee on his good arm.

"Get off me!"

"You broke into my place of business," I said. "You stay right where you are."

"You don't understand! You don't understand, I need that paper!"

"What paper?"

He went still for half a second, like he'd heard himself.

Then the lights came on, all of them at once, every fluorescent tube in the showroom, and Junior was standing in the doorway to the detail bay in his socks and a Razorbacks T-shirt with a pressure washer wand in his hands like a baseball bat.

"Boss!"

Quincy saw his chance. He bucked his hips, threw me sideways, and scrambled for the side door. He went out through the door he'd broken into, and on the way he caught his good hand on the jagged glass left in the frame. He yelled. I heard his feet in the gravel, and then an engine starting somewhere on Asher, and tires squealing out of the Sonic parking lot next door.

Junior ran to the door. "White Altima! Same one! I got half the plate!"

"Don't chase him."

"I'm not chasing him. I'm in my socks."

I sat on the floor among my spilled folders, holding my left arm against my chest. It was already swelling.

Junior knelt down next to me. "You okay? Reggie. Talk to me."

"I think he broke my arm."

"With what?"

"Tire iron." I nodded at the desk. "It's under there. Don't touch it."

Junior looked at the floor by the side door. There was blood on the glass, and a trail of drops across the showroom tile. And lying just inside the door, where Quincy had gone through it, was a key ring. A Nissan key, a house key, and a little gray plastic fob with a logo printed on it.

STIDHAM & ROWE, ATTORNEYS AT LAW. PARKING LEVEL 2.

"Well," Junior said. "That's convenient."

"Don't touch that either."

"I'm calling 911."

"Call them," I said. "And then call Denise Whitmire's office and leave a message. She's going to want to know the clipboard was in the safe."

Little Rock police got there in eleven minutes. Two patrol officers and then, about forty minutes later, a detective named Lamar Hightower, heavyset and sleepy, in a sport coat over a golf shirt. He looked at the broken window and the blood on the glass and the key ring, and then he looked at my arm.

"Who do you think did this?"

"I don't think," I said. "I know. Quincy Barlow. He works at Stidham and Rowe. He was on my lot Wednesday in front of five witnesses asking where I keep my paperwork."

"Why would a paralegal break into a used car lot?"

So I told him. The Chevelle, the trooper, the suspended license, the insurance, the forty thousand dollars. The clipboard.

Hightower listened without writing much. When I was done he picked up the key ring with a pen through the loop and dropped it into an evidence bag.

"A man who works for lawyers," he said, "breaks into the office of the guy who can cost him forty grand, wearing a wrist brace everybody's seen, and leaves his firm's parking fob on the floor in his own blood." He shook his head. "I've been doing this nineteen years, Mr. Dawson. The dumb ones always think they're the smart ones."

"Are you going to arrest him?"

"I'm going to get a warrant for his apartment and that Altima and a swab of that blood. And then, yes, sir, I'm going to arrest him." He looked at my arm again. "Go to the ER. You've got a busted ulna. I can see it from here."

He was right. Two fractures in the forearm. I got a cast up to my elbow and a prescription I didn't fill.

Felicia didn't call that day. Or the next.

Saturday afternoon, her sister did.

"This is Monique," she said. "Felicia's sister. I drove her to your house that day."

"I remember. You didn't look at me."

"I didn't look at you because I was so ashamed of my sister I couldn't look at anybody." She sighed. "Mr. Dawson, Quincy got arrested this morning. At Felicia's apartment. He's been staying there. The police came at seven with a warrant. He had stitches in his left hand and a story about a broken glass at a bar."

"Okay."

"Felicia's falling apart. She's been telling me for two weeks that you gave her permission and Quincy's a victim and you're a cold, mean man who cares more about an old car than people. And this morning she watched them walk him out in handcuffs, and the detective told her what he was doing at two in the morning at your lot."

"What did she say?"

"She said, 'He told me he was going to the gym.'" Monique laughed, sour. "At two in the morning. On a Friday. With a sprained wrist."

"Why are you calling me, Monique?"

"Because she's going to have to sit for a deposition. State Farm's lawyer is suing Quincy, and they're going to put Felicia under oath. And Quincy's been coaching her for two weeks. Every night. What to say, how to say it, how to cry at the right time. He wrote it all down for her on a legal pad." She paused. "I've got the legal pad. I took it out of her kitchen drawer this morning while the police were there. I'm not giving it to anybody. But I wanted you to know it exists, so when she sits down at that table, she knows somebody in her own family knows the truth."

"Monique."

"Yeah?"

"Tell her the clipboard's real. Tell her it's got her handwriting on it. And her little heart over the i."

Monique was quiet for a second.

"She knows," she said. "That's why she's been crying."

State Farm's lawsuit was filed in Pulaski County Circuit Court twenty-four days after the wreck. Arlene Baskin, their subrogation lawyer, sent me a copy. "State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Company and Hendrix Tire & Wheel, LLC, Plaintiffs, versus Quincy L. Barlow, Defendant." Count one, conversion of a motor vehicle. Count two, negligence. Damages: $40,000, plus costs.

Quincy was served at his desk at Stidham and Rowe. Arlene told me later that the process server said he'd never seen a paralegal read his own lawsuit with his mouth hanging open before.

Seven days after that, on a Thursday morning, I sat in a conference room on the ninth floor of a bank building on Capitol Avenue with my arm in a cast and a cup of bad coffee.

Arlene Baskin sat at the head of the long table. She was in her sixties, small, with short silver hair and half-glasses, and she had a way of turning pages that made you nervous. Denise Whitmire sat next to her with a laptop. A court reporter with a little stenograph machine sat in the corner.

Across the table sat Quincy Barlow, out on bond, with a fresh bandage on his left hand and his wrist brace on his right. And next to him, in a gray three-piece suit, with white hair combed straight back and a face like a closed door, sat Walter Stidham himself.

Arlene had told me what to expect. Quincy couldn't afford a lawyer for a civil case, and Stidham had agreed to defend him at a discount, as a favor to a man who'd worked in his office for nine years. Stidham was 66 and had been practicing law in Little Rock for forty of those years. He did not look like a man who did a lot of favors.

"We'll begin with the defendant's witness," Arlene said. "Ms. Felicia Grant."

The door opened and Felicia came in.

She was wearing a navy dress I'd never seen and her hair was pulled back tight, and there was a pink scar on her forehead where the stitches had been. She sat down at the far end of the table, between Quincy's side and ours, and she didn't look at either of us. She looked at her hands.

The court reporter swore her in. Felicia said, "I do," so quietly the reporter asked her to say it again.

Stidham went first, because she was his witness. He was smooth and slow and kind, the way an old lawyer is kind when he thinks he's got a good witness.

"Ms. Grant. On the night in question, when Mr. Dawson gave you the keys to his Chevelle, did he say anything about who else might drive it?"

Felicia was quiet for so long that Stidham repeated the question.

"He said," Felicia said. She stopped. "He said I could..."

Quincy was staring at her across the table. He nodded, just a little. Go on.

Felicia looked at him. Then she looked at the cast on my arm. I watched her eyes stop on it.

"Ms. Grant?" Stidham said.

"He made me sign a clipboard," Felicia said.

Stidham blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"He has a clipboard. By his office door. Everybody makes fun of it. I made fun of it." Her voice was shaking but she kept going. "I wrote my name. I wrote 'Felicia only, no other drivers.' I wrote it myself. I put a heart over the i because I thought it was funny."

Quincy's face went stiff. "Felicia..."

"Mr. Barlow," Arlene said, without even looking up, "you'll have your turn."

Stidham turned slowly toward his client. "Quincy. You told me there was no written agreement. You told me the permission was verbal and the owner would be your word against hers."

"I didn't know about any clipboard," Quincy said.

"Yes, you did," Felicia said.

The whole room turned to her.

"You did," she said. She was crying now, but her voice had steadied. "You asked me that night. At the Cajun's Wharf. Before you asked to drive. You asked if the Chevelle had full coverage, and I said yes, agreed value, he's always talking about it. And you asked if I had to sign anything to take it, and I told you about the clipboard and laughed. And you said, 'Then don't mention the clipboard if anything happens.' Before anything happened. You said that before you ever got behind the wheel."

Arlene put down her pen. Denise stopped typing. The court reporter's fingers kept moving, steady and fast, taking down every word.

"That is not true," Quincy said.

"Then why did you break into his lot?" Felicia said. "Why did you come home with your hand cut open at three in the morning? Why did you write me two pages on a legal pad about what to say today? Monique's got it, Quincy. My sister's got your handwriting."

Quincy turned to Stidham. "Walter, she's upset. She hit her head. She doesn't..."

Stidham held up one hand, and Quincy stopped like somebody had unplugged him.

"Ms. Baskin," Stidham said. His voice had gone very quiet. "I'd like a ten-minute recess. I need to speak with my client. Privately."

"Take fifteen," Arlene said.

They went out into the hall. The door didn't quite close behind them, and in that quiet ninth-floor office, with the air conditioning humming and nobody at the table saying a word, we could all hear Walter Stidham.

He never raised his voice. He didn't have to.

"Nine years, Quincy. Nine years I've vouched for you in this town. I let you carry my firm's name into every courthouse in this county. And you used my parking garage fob to commit a burglary?"

"Walter, it's not..."

"Detective Hightower called me Monday. He told me where they found that fob. He was polite enough to call before he came to my office with a subpoena for the garage records. I'm the one who told him which nights you badged out at one in the morning."

Felicia covered her mouth with her hand.

"I'm going back into that room," Stidham said in the hall, "and I'm going to withdraw as your counsel. On the record. Then you can find yourself another lawyer. Not one in my office. Not one I've ever had lunch with."

"Walter, please. I'll lose everything."

"You lost everything when you picked up that tire iron."

The door opened. Stidham walked back in alone and stood at the end of the table. Quincy came in a few steps behind him and stayed by the door, gray in the face, holding his bandaged hand against his stomach.

"Ms. Baskin," Stidham said. "For the record, I am withdrawing as counsel for the defendant, effective immediately, due to a conflict that I am not at liberty to describe." He turned to Quincy. "And for the record, Mr. Barlow, your employment at Stidham and Rowe is terminated as of this morning. Your desk will be boxed. Leave your building badge with the receptionist on your way out."

Quincy opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

"So noted," Arlene said, and wrote something down.

Stidham picked up his briefcase. Then, on his way to the door, he stopped next to my chair and looked down at my cast.

"Mr. Dawson," he said. "My father ran a filling station in Pine Bluff. Kept a key log on a nail by the register for thirty years. I used to make fun of it too."

"So did I," I said. "Till I was old enough to know better."

He nodded once and walked out.

Arlene took off her half-glasses and looked down the table at Quincy, standing by the door with nobody next to him.

"Mr. Barlow," she said. "You can find a new attorney and continue this deposition at a later date, or I can tell you what happens next. Since you work in the law, I'll assume you already know. But I'll say it anyway, for the record. You have a sworn statement from the only other person in that car that you were never given permission to drive it. You have a signed key log in her handwriting. You have a burglary charge pending for attempting to destroy that key log. And you have a suspended license that the Arkansas State Police were kind enough to write down for me." She folded her glasses. "State Farm will be seeking the full forty thousand. Plus costs. Plus interest. And I'd expect the prosecutor will be interested in Ms. Grant's testimony too."

"I don't have forty thousand dollars," Quincy said.

"Then I'd expect your wages to be garnished for a very long time," Arlene said. "Once you have wages again."

He looked at Felicia. She didn't look back. He turned around, opened the door with his good hand, and walked out without another word. We all heard the elevator ding.

Felicia stood up shakily. "Can I go?"

"You're excused, Ms. Grant," Arlene said. "Thank you for telling the truth. I know that wasn't easy."

Felicia came around the table. I thought she was going to walk past me. Instead she stopped by my chair, the same way Stidham had, and looked at my arm.

"He did that to you?"

"With a tire iron."

"I'm so sorry, Reggie." Her voice was barely there. "I'm sorry about your dad's car. I'm sorry about the clipboard. I'm sorry I asked you to lie. I'm sorry for all of it." She swallowed. "Is there any way... I know it's stupid. I know. But is there any way you could ever..."

"Felicia."

"I know. I know." She wiped her eyes. "Can I at least... can I call you sometime? Just to check on you? On your arm?"

I thought about my father's picture with the glass cracked straight through his face. I'd had it reframed. It was sitting on my desk at the lot now, right next to the safe where the clipboard lived.

"That Sunday morning," I said, "you crashed my car and let a man with no license drive it at seventy miles an hour. And the first thing I asked you was whether everybody was safe."

"I know. I remember."

"You were safe. You walked away. That's all I needed to know about you, Felicia. You don't need to check on me."

She stood there a moment longer. Then she nodded, turned and walked out of the conference room, and I heard her start crying again in the hall, softly, as the elevator doors closed.

Denise Whitmire closed her laptop. Arlene Baskin capped her pen and looked at me over the top of the long, empty table.

"Mr. Dawson," Arlene said, "in thirty-one years of doing this, I have never once won a case because of a clipboard. Go home and hang it back on its nail."

I picked up my cup of bad coffee with my good hand, finished it, and walked out of that building into the hot Arkansas noon, where Junior was waiting at the curb in my truck with the windows down and the radio on, grinning like he already knew.
