"You'll be happier outside the corporate structure, Roland."

My wife said it at the head of the boardroom table at Tolman Lumber and Millwork, five days after we buried her father, in front of four other people and a tray of grocery store muffins nobody had touched. Then she asked for a show of hands.

Four hands went up. Hers. The one she raised for her father's estate, because she was the executor. The one belonging to a man from Spokane named Blaine Corwin, who'd been appointed to our board eleven days earlier. And our son Brody's.

Brody's hand went up last. He didn't look at me when he raised it.

Thirty-one years I'd worked for that company. Fourteen of them as chief operating officer. And it took my family about nine seconds to vote me out.

I didn't argue. I unclipped my badge from my shirt pocket and laid it on the table. Then I took the brass master key for the Bonner mill off my key ring, the one that opens every door from the log yard to the planer shed, and I set it on top of the badge.

"Okay," I said.

Five days later, at eight o'clock on a Monday morning, the president of First Montana Bank would walk into that same boardroom with a manila envelope under his arm, and my wife would be the one asking somebody to please get her a glass of water.

What she didn't know was what was written in Section 7.4 of a credit agreement her father had signed with that bank in 2011. She'd never read it. Neither had Blaine Corwin. I'd written half of it myself.

My name is Roland Keene. I'm fifty-nine years old, and I started at the Tolman mill in Bonner, Montana, as a millwright when I was twenty-eight, fixing headrigs and conveyor chains at three in the morning. A millwright learns one thing early. In every machine, no matter how big, there's one bolt that holds the rest together. You don't notice it until somebody takes it out. Hold on to that.

Colette slid a blue folder across the table to me.

"Your separation agreement," she said. "It's very generous. Dad would want you taken care of."

"Your dad's been gone nine days."

"Which is why I'm doing what he would've done." She folded her hands. "Sign it by Friday, please. Blaine's people need clean paperwork."

"Blaine's people," I said.

Blaine Corwin smiled at me across the muffins. He was forty-eight, tan in a way you don't get in Missoula in October, and he had the habit of putting air quotes around words he didn't believe in.

"Nothing personal, Roland," he said. "This is about 'legacy.' Colette wants to protect her father's 'vision.' Sometimes that means fresh eyes."

"Fresh eyes," I said.

"Exactly."

I looked at my son. Brody was twenty-four, worked inside sales, and he had his grandfather's chin and my bad habit of chewing the inside of his cheek when he was nervous. He was chewing it now.

"Brody," I said.

"Dad, don't." He finally looked up. "Just don't make this harder."

I picked up the blue folder, tucked it under my arm, and walked out of the boardroom. I didn't slam the door. I've fixed too many doors to slam one.

I was halfway across the gravel lot to my truck when I heard boots behind me. Brody.

"Dad. Wait."

I stopped and waited. The wind was coming down the Blackfoot, cold enough to smell the snow up high, and the log yard was loud with a loader stacking Doug fir.

"I didn't want to do it," he said.

"But you did it."

"Mom said you've been forgetting things." He shoved his hands in his jacket. "The Hendricks order. Two hundred thousand board feet of fir to Bozeman, and somebody changed it to hemlock, and we ate forty-one thousand dollars. She said it was you. She said Grandpa saw it coming and was worried. She said you've been 'slipping.'"

"I didn't change the Hendricks order."

"She said you'd say that."

"Brody, I've been reading orders for thirty-one years. I don't confuse fir and hemlock. Your grandpa used to say I could smell the difference blindfolded." I looked at him. "Somebody changed that order. Ask yourself who had a reason to make me look like I was slipping."

"Dad—"

"Ask the computer," I said. "Every change in that order system has a login and a time on it. I don't know who did it. The computer does."

He stood there in the wind with his mouth half open. Then he turned around and walked back toward the office.

I didn't watch him go. I walked out past the debarker to the head saw building, because Teo Ruiz was going to hear about this one way or another, and I wanted him to hear it from me.

Teo has been head sawyer at the Bonner mill since 1993. He's sixty-one, he's got a gray mustache like a push broom and three fingers on his left hand, and there's nobody in Montana who can read a log better. He was standing in the booth with his earmuffs around his neck when I came in.

"I heard," he said.

"Already?"

"The girls in payroll called me before you were out of the parking lot." He shut the booth door so we could hear each other. "Roland. That son of a witch Corwin."

"He's on the board."

"He's on more than the board." Teo glanced at the window, out toward the office. "I didn't want to say nothing with Harvey sick. But my nephew drives a delivery truck. He's dropped siding at your house twice this month on a Tuesday afternoon, and both times there was a gray Range Rover with Washington plates in your driveway."

I didn't say anything for a while.

"Tuesdays," I said. "I'm at the Frenchtown yard on Tuesdays."

"Yeah. I figured you were."

"Washington plates."

"Spokane." Teo leaned on the console. "And there's more. You know what Corwin's outfit does? Inland Crest Capital? My cousin works at a mill in Sandpoint they bought in 2021. They bought it, they sold the timber rights, they sold the land to a developer, and they closed the mill in fourteen months. A hundred and ten guys."

"How many guys at Bonner?"

"A hundred and sixty-two. Counting me." He looked at me hard. "Roland, if Colette sells this mill to Inland Crest, Bonner's done. Harvey would come out of that grave."

I thought about Harvey Tolman in his hospital bed in September, eighty-four years old and smaller than I'd ever seen him, gripping my wrist with what strength he had left.

"Remember 7.4," he'd said. I thought he was confused. He wasn't.

"Teo," I said. "Don't do anything. Don't say anything to anybody. Just run the saw."

"That's it? That's your plan? Run the saw?"

"The company has five business days to do something, and they don't know they have to do it," I said. "Let's see if they do it."

"What happens if they don't?"

"Then on Monday," I said, "somebody's going to find out where the bolt was."

Thea Marchand practices law out of a converted house on the north side, two blocks from the train tracks, with a woodstove in her waiting room and a framed fly she tied herself over her desk. She did my parents' wills and Brody's speeding ticket in high school. She's sixty-eight and she doesn't take new clients, she says, except the ones she likes.

She read the separation agreement Thursday morning while I sat in a leather chair that had been sat in by half of western Montana.

"A hundred eighty thousand dollars," she said. "Twelve months of salary. That's the generous part. Here's the rest." She flipped pages. "Three-year non-compete anywhere in Montana, Idaho, Washington or Oregon. You can't work in lumber. You can't consult in lumber. And this one." She tapped a paragraph. "Non-disparagement. You can't say a negative word about the company or its directors, in public or private, ever. And it says you're resigning voluntarily for personal health reasons."

"My health is fine."

"Your health is excellent. I've seen you split wood." She put the agreement down. "Don't sign it."

"I wasn't going to."

"Good. Now tell me why you look like a man who's holding a straight flush."

I told her about the 2011 refinance.

In 2010, the housing market had been dead for three years, and Tolman Lumber was losing two hundred thousand dollars a month. The old bank called its loans. Harvey was seventy, and he was ready to padlock the gate. I spent four months working with a loan officer at First Montana named Warren Sayles to put together a new credit line. Nine point four million dollars. The bank didn't trust a lumber mill in 2011, and it really didn't trust a seventy-year-old owner. They trusted the guy who'd been running the floor and had a plan to cut costs and switch half the production to fence boards and pallet stock.

So they wrote me into the loan.

"Section 7.4," I said. "Key person covenant. It names me, by name, as chief operating officer. If the company removes me without the bank's written consent, it's an event of default. The bank can call the whole line in thirty days."

Thea took her reading glasses off. "And the company has to tell the bank?"

"Within five business days of any change in key personnel. Section 7.4, paragraph c. It was Wednesday. They've got until next Wednesday. If they tell the bank, the bank can sit down with them and talk. If they don't tell the bank, and the bank finds out on its own—"

"Then the bank's lawyers get very excited." Thea smiled. "Who knows about 7.4?"

"Harvey knew. He signed it. Warren Sayles knows. He's the bank president now. And me."

"Not Colette?"

"Colette doesn't read loan documents. She says her father always handled the 'boring parts.'" I shrugged. "And Blaine Corwin's people did due diligence on the company for a month, but they got the files from Colette. And Colette gave them what Colette thought mattered."

"You put yourself in a bank loan." Thea shook her head slowly. "My father used to say the quietest dog is the one sleeping on the porch."

"I'm not a dog, Thea."

"No," she said. "You're the porch."

That evening I was in the kitchen when Colette came home. She came in the back door from the garage with two grocery bags and her phone tucked against her shoulder, and when she saw me at the table, she hung up without saying goodbye.

"Who was that?" I asked.

"Brody."

I didn't say anything. She set the bags down on the counter.

"Did you look at the agreement?" she asked.

"I looked at it."

"And?"

"It says I'm resigning for health reasons."

"It's a kindness, Roland. People will talk. This way they'll think you chose to step back after Dad passed."

"My health is fine."

"Is it?" She started putting cans in the pantry. "You've been tired. You've been short with people. The Hendricks order."

"I didn't touch the Hendricks order."

"Dad always said a man doesn't know when he's slipping. That's what makes it slipping."

"Your dad never said that."

"He said it to me. In the hospital."

That stopped me for a second, because I'd sat by that hospital bed every night for two weeks, and Colette had come twice.

"Colette," I said. "Who drives a gray Range Rover with Washington plates?"

She set down a can of green beans very carefully.

"Lots of people."

"Who parks it in our driveway on Tuesday afternoons?"

"Blaine's been here a few times. For meetings. About the company. I'm the executor, Roland. I have to meet with people."

"In our house."

"Where else am I supposed to meet him? The office is full of your people. They'd talk."

"Talk about what?"

"About business!" She turned around. Her face was flushed. "Do you hear yourself? My father just died. I'm trying to hold this company together, and you're standing in my kitchen accusing me of—of what? Say it."

"I'm asking."

"Blaine Corwin is a business consultant. He's helping me. That's all he is." She picked up her purse. "And I'm not going to stand here and be interrogated by a man who couldn't tell fir from hemlock."

She went upstairs. I heard the bedroom door lock.

I sat in the kitchen and looked out the window at the dark where the driveway was. Then my phone buzzed on the table, and I looked down.

It was a news alert from the Missoulian's business page. I'd signed up for it years ago so I'd know if anybody wrote anything about the mill.

TOLMAN LUMBER COO KEENE STEPS DOWN FOR HEALTH REASONS, it said.

I read the story. It quoted a press release issued by the company that afternoon. It quoted Colette as board chair. "Roland has given thirty-one years to this company, and we fully support his decision to focus on his health," she said. It said the company was "exploring strategic options" with "a partner experienced in timber assets."

I read it twice. Then I forwarded it to Thea with one line.

"They told the newspaper. They didn't tell the bank."

Friday morning, I didn't have anywhere to be for the first time since 1994. So I did what I always do when I don't know what to do. I went out to the shop behind the house and sharpened every chisel I owned.

I was on the fourth one when Brody's pickup came up the driveway too fast and stopped crooked.

He came into the shop without knocking. He had a sheet of paper in his hand, folded in quarters, and his face was the color of the sawdust on the floor.

"I asked the computer," he said.

"Okay."

"I went to the IT guy yesterday after lunch. I told him I needed the change history on the Hendricks order for a customer complaint. He didn't even ask why. He just printed it." He unfolded the paper and held it out. His hand wasn't steady. "Read it."

I wiped my hands on a rag and took the sheet.

It was a log printout. Order number, customer Hendricks Construction, Bozeman. Original entry, Douglas fir, two hundred thousand board feet, entered by R. Keene, September 9. Modified, species changed to Western hemlock, September 23, a little before eleven at night. Modified by user C. Keene. Remote login. IP address registered to the internet account at our house.

September 23. Harvey had been in the hospital that week. I'd been sitting next to his bed until midnight every night.

"Mom did it," Brody said. "From the house. While you were at the hospital with Grandpa."

"Looks that way."

"Why would she do that?"

"I don't know, son. Why do you think?"

He sat down hard on my sawhorse. He was quiet for a while, and I let him be quiet. You don't push a man who's figuring something out. You just hand him the next tool.

"So you'd look like you were slipping," he said finally. "So I'd vote you out."

I didn't say anything.

"She cried, Dad. When she told me. She said Grandpa was worried about you. She said it was the hardest thing she'd ever have to do." He put his face in his hands. "I believed her. I raised my hand."

"You trusted your mother," I said. "That's not a crime."

"It feels like one."

"Then do something about it. Don't sit on my sawhorse feeling bad."

He looked up. "What do I do?"

"Go to work. Keep your mouth shut. And Monday morning at eight, be in the boardroom. Bring that sheet."

"Is there a board meeting Monday?"

"There will be," I said.

My phone rang on the workbench. Colette. I put it on speaker and set it next to the chisels.

"Did you sign the agreement?" she said, no hello.

"No."

"Roland, the deadline is today."

"I know what day it is."

"If you don't sign, there's no severance. Do you understand that? Nothing. You'll walk away with nothing after thirty-one years. Is that what you want?"

"I heard you."

"Blaine's firm signs the letter of intent on Monday. After that, it doesn't matter what you do. Sign it, Roland. Take the money. Be smart for once in your life."

"Okay," I said.

"Okay, you'll sign?"

"Okay, I heard you."

She made a sound like she was strangling the phone and hung up.

Brody was staring at me.

"She said Blaine's firm signs a letter of intent Monday," he said. "Dad, are they selling the mill?"

"They're trying."

"To Inland Crest? Grandpa hated those guys. He said they'd sell their mother for timber rights."

"He said that about me once, too, back in '96," I said. "He was wrong about me."

Brody got up and went to the door. Then he stopped.

"Dad. Did Mom really change the order so I'd vote? Or so Blaine would get the mill?"

Before I could answer, my phone rang again. A number I didn't know, with a 509 area code. Spokane.

I answered it. A woman's voice, low and careful, like she'd rehearsed the first sentence in the car.

"Is this Roland Keene? From Tolman Lumber?"

"It is."

"My name is Rosalyn Corwin," she said. "I got your number off your company's website. You're still on the leadership page." A pause. "I think my husband is sleeping with your wife."

Rosalyn Corwin drove the three hours from Spokane on Saturday morning. We met at a coffee shop on Higgins Avenue, at a table in the back, under a poster for a bluegrass festival that had been over since August.

She was about forty-five, small and neat, with short gray hair she didn't bother to color, and a laptop bag she held in her lap like it might run off. Brody came with me. I'd asked him to. If he was going to stand in that boardroom Monday, he needed to hear this with his own ears.

"I'm sorry," she said, first thing. "I know you don't know me, and I'm sorry to bring you this."

"You're not the one who should be sorry," I said.

"I keep telling myself that." She opened the laptop. "Blaine and I have been married nineteen years. We have two girls in high school. He travels a lot for work, so I didn't think much about it. Then three weeks ago he left his laptop open on the kitchen island, and an email popped up. From Colette Keene. The subject line was 'tonight.'"

She turned the screen toward us.

I'm not going to tell you what was in all of them. There were thirty-some emails going back to June. Some of them were the kind of thing a man doesn't need to read about his wife. Some of them were business.

The business ones were worse.

"Here," Rosalyn said, scrolling. "This is the plan. Blaine wrote it out for her on September 20."

It was a numbered list. Step one, get Roland out before the sale; he'll fight it. Step two, Brody is the swing vote on the board; give him a reason. Step three, Inland Crest buys at a discount, sells the timber rights to Pacific Fiber, sells the Bonner land, carried interest to Blaine. Step four, you and me, Coeur d'Alene, the lake house.

Brody read it over my shoulder. I heard him breathe in.

"Give him a reason," he said. "September 20. She changed the order on the 23rd."

"Three days," I said.

"And this one," Rosalyn said. "He drafted what she should say at the board meeting. She was nervous. He wrote her a script."

She clicked an email dated the Monday after the funeral. Blaine to Colette. Subject: "Wednesday, word for word."

The first line of the script, in Blaine's words, was: "You'll be happier outside the corporate structure, Roland."

Brody sat back in his chair like somebody had pushed him.

"Those weren't even her words," he said. "Mom read it off a script."

"She did a fine job of it," I said.

Rosalyn closed the laptop. "I've already talked to a lawyer in Spokane. I'm filing next week. But I wanted you to have this first." She took a flash drive out of the laptop bag and slid it across the table. "Everything's on there. I thought about mailing it, but I wanted to see your face. I wanted to know somebody else was real."

"I'm real," I said.

"Are you going to use it?"

"Not the personal ones. The business ones. Monday morning."

"What happens Monday morning?"

I looked at Brody, and then back at her.

"Mrs. Corwin, do you want to see your husband's face when it happens?"

She thought about it for exactly one second.

"Yes," she said.

That afternoon, Colette and I were supposed to start cleaning out Harvey's house on Greenough Drive. It had been on the calendar for two weeks. I don't think she expected me to show up. I showed up.

She was in Harvey's study with a box of files when I walked in. She didn't look up.

"I didn't think you'd come."

"I told your dad I'd help with his things."

"You told my dad a lot of things."

I started on the bookshelf. Harvey had kept every Farmer's Almanac since 1961, and every one of them had his notes in the margins about rain and the price of logs. I stacked them in a box. Colette stacked files in another one.

"Brody's been acting strange," she said after a while. "He wouldn't come to dinner last night."

"Maybe he had something on his mind."

"Did you say something to him?"

"I told him to ask the computer about the Hendricks order."

"You turned my son against me."

"You did that with a keyboard."

Thirty years of 'the mill this' and 'the mill that.' Dad always loved you more than me, you know that? You were the son he wanted. I was just the daughter who married the help."

That one landed. I'm not going to pretend it didn't.

I set down the almanac in my hand.

"You want to know what I gave?" I said. "In 2011, the bank wanted collateral for the refinance. Your dad didn't have enough. So I put up our house. Our house, Colette. My name and yours. You signed the deed of trust. You didn't read it, but you signed it. For six years, if that mill had gone under, we'd have lost our home. I never told you, because I didn't want you to worry. I worked eighty hours a week for six years so you wouldn't have to worry."

She stared at me.

"That's not true."

"Go look at the county records. 2011. Deed of trust, First Montana Bank. Released in 2017."

The Tolman Lumber boardroom is on the second floor of the office building at the Bonner yard, with a long window that looks down on the log decks and the river. Colette sat at the head, in Harvey's chair. Blaine Corwin sat on her right, with a leather portfolio and a fresh haircut. Brody sat halfway down, with a folded sheet of paper in front of him. "He said it at Thanksgiving," Brody went on. "Two years ago. He said Inland Crest bought the best mill in Idaho and gutted it for the timber rights. He was mad about it all through dinner." He looked at his mother. "You were there, Mom."

Colette didn't answer.

"And while we're talking about things people said," Brody said, and he unfolded the sheet of paper in front of him, "I'd like to put something in the minutes."

"Brody," Colette said. "Not now."

"Yes, now." He slid the paper to the company lawyer. "That's the change log for the Hendricks order. The one we lost forty-one thousand dollars on. The one Mom told me was Dad's fault. The one I voted on. It says the order was changed by C. Keene, from the house, on September 23, at night. Dad was at the hospital with Grandpa that night. Mom was home."

The company lawyer read it, and his eyebrows went up into his hairline.

Colette stood up. "That's a computer error."

"It's your login, Mom."

"Anybody could use my login."

"Who else was at the house?" Brody asked.

She opened her mouth. Then she looked at Blaine. It was a very short look. But everybody in that room saw it, and Blaine saw everybody see it.

"This is getting off track," Blaine said. "Colette, let's take a short recess—"

The door opened again.

Rosalyn Corwin stood in the doorway with her laptop bag over her shoulder. She'd driven over from Spokane again that morning, before dawn. I'd told her eight o'clock. She was right on time.

Blaine's face did something I'll remember for a long time. It was the face of a man who has walked into a room he thought he owned and found his wife standing in it.

"Ros," he said. "What are you doing here?"

"Hello, Blaine." She walked in and set the laptop bag on the larch table. "I'm returning something of yours."

She took out a stack of paper, maybe forty pages, held together with a binder clip, and she set it down in the middle of the table.

"Those are emails," she said, to the whole room. "From my husband to Mrs. Keene. Not the personal ones. I took those out. These are just the business ones. There's a plan in there from September 20 about how to get Mr. Keene off the board before the sale, and how to give their son 'a reason' to vote. And there's a script from last Monday that my husband wrote for Mrs. Keene, so she'd know what to say at Wednesday's meeting." She looked at Colette. "Your first line was his, Colette. Word for word."

Colette sat down very slowly.

Blaine stood up. "Ros, this is a private business matter. You need to leave."

"I'm not done." Rosalyn took out her phone. "Your managing partner called me last night, Blaine. I sent him the same emails on Saturday. He wanted to talk to you this morning. I told him where you'd be."

She put the phone on the table, on speaker. A man's voice came out of it, tired and very angry. I never did learn his name, and I didn't need to.

"Blaine. Are you in the room?"

Blaine swallowed. "I'm here. Listen, this is all a misunderstanding—"

"Stop talking. I've read the emails. You had an undisclosed personal relationship with the board chair of a company we were acquiring. You helped her remove the company's chief operating officer so we could buy it cheaper. You wrote her a script. Our lawyers are going to be reading this for a month." A pause. "Inland Crest is withdrawing the letter of intent, effective now. Mr. Sayles, I apologize to your bank. And Blaine, you're terminated. Don't come into the Spokane office. Your things will be sent to you."

"You can't do that. I have carried interest in three funds—"

"Read your partnership agreement, Blaine," the voice said. "Section nine. Termination for cause." The line went dead.

Blaine stood there holding his leather portfolio. He looked at Colette. He looked at his wife. He looked at the slab of Montana larch, like it might open up and let him through.

Colette's hand was shaking. She pressed it flat on the table, and then she looked up at nobody in particular.

"Could somebody," she said, "please get me a glass of water?"

Brody got up and went to the sideboard and poured her one. He set it in front of her. She didn't drink it.

Warren cleared his throat.

"Here's where the bank stands," he said. "The default stays in place unless this board does two things today. One, reinstate Roland Keene as chief operating officer, with full operating authority over the mills, effective immediately. Two, remove Mr. Corwin from this board. Do those two things, and I'll put a waiver in writing by noon. Don't do them, and the bank calls the line in thirty days, and this company goes to auction."

Colette looked at the glass of water.

"I'll move to reinstate," Brody said. "And to remove Blaine Corwin."

"Second," said the company lawyer, and then caught himself. "I'm sorry, I'm not a director."

"I'll second it," Colette said. Her voice was barely there. "I'll second it. All in favor."

Brody's hand went up. Then Colette's. And then, a second later, she raised it again for her father's estate. Three to one. The one was Blaine, who didn't raise anything, because there was nothing left to raise it for.

"Motion carries," Colette said.

She looked down the table at me for the first time that morning.

"Roland," she said. "Will you come back?"

I stood up. I took Harvey's letter out of my shirt pocket, and I unfolded it, and I read the one line that mattered out loud, so the minutes would have it.

"'Don't come back cheap.'"

Then I folded it up again.

"I'll come back," I said. "My terms. We'll talk about them with Thea this afternoon. And Colette, the separation agreement in the blue folder? It's in my truck. You can have it back. I never signed it."

She didn't say anything. She just nodded at the water glass.

Blaine was packing his portfolio. He snapped it shut and walked toward the door, and Rosalyn stepped aside to let him pass.

"Ros," he said quietly. "Where am I supposed to go?"

"The girls are with my mother," she said. "Your things are in the garage. The locks are new. You'll be happier outside the house, Blaine."

He stood in the doorway a second. Then he looked back at me, standing by the larch table with Harvey's letter in my hand, and I could see him trying to think of one last smooth thing to say.

I saved him the trouble.

"Blaine," I said. "You wrote the line yourself. You'll be happier outside the corporate structure."

He walked out. Down the table, Brody reached into his jacket pocket and took out the brass master key for the Bonner mill. He'd been carrying it since Wednesday. He slid it down the larch table to me, and I put it back on my key ring.
