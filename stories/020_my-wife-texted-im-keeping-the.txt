"I'm keeping the house in the divorce. You can find somewhere else to live."

That was the text my wife sent me on a Saturday night while I was sitting at a card table in the back room of the Elks Lodge in Waco, Texas, with five men I've played poker with every second Saturday for nineteen years. I read it out loud, because that's the rule at our table. Anybody's phone goes off, you read it to the room or you buy the next round.

The room went quiet. Big Joe Salcido put his cards face down. Even the ceiling fan seemed to slow down.

Twenty-three years of marriage, and she'd ended it by text message, between my pair of jacks and the river card.

I typed back four words with one thumb, set the phone face up in the middle of the table where everybody could see it, and said, "Next round's on me anyway, boys."

The reply on the screen said, "Good luck with that."

Fourteen days later, at a little after nine in the morning, her lawyer was going to call my cell phone from his office, and he was going to say the word please four times in the first minute.

What she didn't know, that Saturday night, was that there was a blue folder at the bottom of my mother's cedar chest in our spare bedroom, and that she'd walked past it every day for twenty-three years without once opening the lid.

My name is Lyle Benford. I'm sixty-one. I've been a locksmith in Waco for thirty-four years, and I run the business out of a metal shop building behind my house on Lake Shore Drive. You'd be surprised what a locksmith learns about people. The main thing is this: a man can change the lock on a door, but he can't change who owns the house it's on. Remember that.

The phone buzzed again on the felt before anybody had picked their cards back up.

It was a picture this time. My recliner. My brown leather recliner, the one my kids bought me for my sixtieth birthday. And in it, with his boots on my footrest and one of my Shiner Bocks in his hand, was a man about forty with a sunburned neck and a big grin, holding the beer up at the camera like a toast.

Under it, she'd typed, "Tanner says thanks for the beer."

Big Joe leaned over to look. Then he looked at me.

"Lyle," he said, "isn't that the pool guy?"

It was the pool guy. Tanner Voss. Voss Pools and Outdoor Living. Thirty-nine years old, four trucks with his face on the side, and an estimate in my desk drawer for a sixty-two-thousand-dollar pool my wife had wanted since February. I'd said no to the pool. Apparently she'd found another way to get the pool guy.

"You want me to drive you home?" Joe asked.

"No," I said. "I want to finish this hand."

I won it, too. Pair of jacks held up. I left forty-one dollars richer and drove home along the lake with the windows down.

When I pulled into my driveway a little before eleven, there was a white Ford F-250 parked in my spot with a magnetic sign on the door. Voss Pools. Tanner's face on it, smiling, like a man who'd never been told no.

The porch light was on. I walked up and put my key in the front door.

It didn't turn.

I stood there and looked at the lock. It was a brand-new Kwikset, brushed nickel, the cheap kind they sell in blister packs at Lowe's for thirty-four dollars. I could see the scratch marks on the old strike plate where somebody had taken the old one out in a hurry with the wrong screwdriver.

My wife had changed the locks on a locksmith.

I'll be honest with you. That's when I laughed. Standing on my own porch at eleven at night, I laughed so hard I had to put a hand on the doorframe.

The door opened from the inside. Sheila stood there in her bathrobe with her arms folded. She's fifty-seven, blond out of a box, and still pretty in a way that makes waitresses compliment her earrings.

"I told you," she said. "Find somewhere else to live."

"Sheila, you put a thirty-four-dollar Kwikset on a house owned by a locksmith."

"I didn't ask your opinion on the hardware."

"I could have that open in about forty seconds with a paper clip."

"Then you'd be breaking into my house, and I'd call the police."

"It's not your house."

Behind her, Tanner Voss came down the hallway in his socks with my beer still in his hand. He was bigger up close. Gym shoulders, a tan line from a ball cap, a gold chain at his neck.

"There a problem, old man?" he said.

"Not yet," I said. "You're in my socks, though."

He looked down. He was, in fact, wearing my gray wool socks, the good ones from Cabela's. He'd gone through my dresser.

"Sheila said I could borrow some," he said.

"Sheila says a lot of things."

He stepped up next to her in the doorway, chest out. "Look, man. It's over. She doesn't want you here. You can make it easy or you can make it hard."

"How would hard go?"

"Hard would go with you on your back in this flower bed."

I looked at my wife. "You going to let him talk to me like that on my own porch?"

"It's my porch now, Lyle."

"Twenty-three years," I said. "You couldn't call me? You had to text me during poker?"

"I knew you'd read it out loud," she said. "I wanted the whole lodge to hear it."

That one landed. I'll admit it. She'd known exactly what she was doing.

I took a long breath. I am sixty-one years old and I have a bad knee and a worse back, and Tanner Voss was thirty-nine and outweighed me by forty pounds of protein shakes. And none of that mattered, because I wasn't going to fight a man in my wife's bathrobe doorway at eleven at night so he could tell the deputies I started it.

"Okay," I said. "I'll sleep in the shop."

"The shop's mine too," Sheila said.

"The shop has a lock you didn't change," I said, "because you don't have the key. And you've never once in twenty-three years asked me where I keep it."

I walked around the side of the house in the dark to my metal building in the back. The shop has a cot in it from when I used to do overnight emergency calls, a coffee maker, a TV that gets four channels and twelve hundred keys on a pegboard. I unlocked it with the key I keep on a chain around my neck, went in, and lay down on the cot with my boots on.

I didn't sleep. Around one in the morning, I heard Tanner's truck door open and close in the driveway, and his voice carrying across the yard, low, the way men talk on the phone when they think nobody's awake.

"Yeah, she did it. Locks are changed. He's out back in his shed like a dog." A laugh. "No, she's good. She signed the affidavit yesterday. I recorded the memorandum this morning at the county. Closing's set for the twentieth. Two-eighty in cash from the guys in Austin, we put a pool in, flip it by fall for five-fifty."

I sat up on the cot.

"Lyle? Lyle doesn't know squat. Sheila says he never even reads the mail."

The truck door shut. The engine started. Tanner pulled out of my driveway and went down Lake Shore Drive toward town.

I got up, turned on the light over my workbench and wrote it all down on the back of an invoice pad. Affidavit. Memorandum. County. The twentieth. Two-eighty. Austin.

Then I went to the window and looked across the street at Mr. Odell's house, where a little red light was blinking under the eave of his garage, the way it had blinked every night for three years, ever since some kids stole his riding mower and he bought a camera that could read a license plate from the moon.

Monday morning I went into the house through the back door. That door has a deadbolt I installed myself in 2004, a commercial-grade Medeco, and Sheila hadn't changed it, because you can't buy a Medeco at Lowe's and she didn't know the difference. She was at work at the dental office. Tanner's truck was gone.

I went to the spare bedroom and opened my mother's cedar chest.

Under the quilts my grandmother made, under my father's Army uniform and a shoebox of my kids' baby teeth that Sheila says is disgusting, was a blue plastic folder with a snap. I took it out to the shop without unsnapping it, put it on the seat of my truck, and drove downtown.

Marisela Cantu's office is on Austin Avenue in an old brick building with a bail bondsman on the first floor. She's about fifty, wears her hair in a long gray braid, and she's the lawyer I call when a landlord wants me to change locks on a tenant and I want to be sure I'm not about to get sued. I've done her office locks for free for nine years. She owed me one.

She opened the blue folder on her desk and went through it one page at a time.

A warranty deed, recorded with McLennan County in April of 1998. Grantee, Lyle Wayne Benford, a single man.

A marriage certificate dated October 2003.

A promissory note for the house, and a release of lien dated June 2010, stamped Paid in Full.

A copy of a check for the payoff amount, ninety-one thousand dollars, drawn on the estate account of Ruby Benford, deceased. My mother.

Marisela looked up.

"Lyle," she said, "you bought this house five years before you married her."

"I did."

"And you paid it off with your mother's inheritance."

"Her estate. I kept the paperwork. A locksmith keeps paperwork."

She leaned back in her chair and smiled for the first time.

"Okay. Here's Texas," she said. "Texas is a community property state. Anything you two earned during the marriage, the paychecks, the savings, the 401k, that's community, and it gets divided. But property you owned before the marriage is your separate property. A house you bought single and paid off with an inheritance is about as separate as it gets. A judge can't give it to her. Can't. Not in a divorce."

"She thinks it's hers because she's lived in it twenty-three years."

"She can think that. She's wrong." Marisela tapped the deed. "Now. While the divorce is pending, she has the right to keep living there. It's a homestead. You can't throw her out, either. But she can't sell it, she can't borrow against it, and she sure as hell can't give it to her pool man."

"About that."

I told her what I'd heard through the shop window. Affidavit. Memorandum. The twentieth. Two hundred eighty thousand in cash from some men in Austin.

Marisela stopped smiling. She turned to her computer and started typing.

"The McLennan County clerk has the real property records online," she said. "If he recorded something Saturday morning, it'll be indexed by now." She clicked. She clicked again. She went quiet.

"Lyle," she said. "Come look at this."

On the screen was a scanned document, filed Saturday at nine-fifty in the morning. Memorandum of Contract for Sale. Seller, Sheila Ann Benford. Buyer, Voss Property Group LLC. Property, my address on Lake Shore Drive. Sales price, two hundred eighty thousand dollars. Closing date, the twentieth of the month.

Attached behind it was an affidavit. Sheila's signature, notarized.

"I, Sheila Ann Benford, being duly sworn, state that the above property is the community property of my marriage, that I have full authority to convey it, and that no other person has any ownership interest in said property."

I read it twice. Then I sat down in Marisela's client chair, because my knee was acting up.

"She swore to that?"

"She swore to it in front of a notary and put it in the public records of McLennan County." Marisela turned to me. "Lyle, in Texas, knowingly filing a fraudulent document about real property is a crime. So is a false statement to get somebody's property. If she knew this house was yours, and Tanner knew it, they just committed a felony to sell your house out from under you for a little more than half what it's worth."

"Can they actually sell it?"

"Not legally. No title company in Texas should close on it once they see the 1998 deed. But a sloppy one might, if they're in a hurry, if the buyer's paying cash, if somebody's bought a title policy from a cousin." She shook her head. "And if they close, you'll spend two years and a lot of money undoing it."

"So what do I do?"

"Nothing," she said. "For now, nothing. I'll file your answer to her divorce when she files it, and she will. I'll record a notice that you own the property and there's a dispute. And we wait for the twentieth."

"Wait?"

"Lyle, they don't know you have this folder. They think you never read the mail. Let them keep thinking it." She closed the folder. "And keep living in your shop. Don't give that man a reason."

My phone rang on the way back to my truck. It was Mr. Odell from across the street. He's eighty-three, a retired Baylor chemistry professor, and he calls everybody Mister, including his dog.

"Mr. Benford," he said. "There's a trailer backed up to your shop. A flatbed. Some young men are carrying out a very large machine."

It was a little after two when I came around the corner onto Lake Shore Drive. A flatbed trailer with Voss Pools on the side was backed up to my shop door, and the shop door was standing wide open, which was interesting, since I had the only key around my neck.

Two kids in Voss Pools T-shirts were wrestling my Ilco key duplicator up a pair of ramps onto the trailer. That machine weighs a hundred and forty pounds and cost me nine thousand dollars in 2016. Tanner Voss was standing in the yard with his sunglasses on his head, supervising, with a cup of coffee in his hand. My coffee mug. The one that said World's Okayest Locksmith.

I parked my truck across the end of the driveway so the trailer couldn't get out, and I got out slow.

"Put it back," I said.

Tanner turned around and grinned. "Hey, old man. We're clearing the yard. Sheila wants this building gone by the twentieth. Pool's going right here."

"That's my business in there. Twelve hundred keys and every tool I own."

"Sheila says it's community property. She's letting you keep the keys. Generous, I think."

"How'd you get in?"

He held up a crowbar. The door frame was bent like a soda can.

"Put my machine back on the floor where it was," I said. "Then get your trailer off my property."

"Your property." He laughed. "Your wife sold this property, man. You just don't know it yet."

"I know it."

His grin flickered.

"I know all about Voss Property Group," I said. "I know about the men in Austin and the two-eighty and the twentieth. And I know the county clerk's office has a nice clean copy of my wife's signature on a piece of paper that's going to put somebody in jail."

He set my mug down on the hood of his truck, very carefully. Then he walked up to me until he was about a foot away. He smelled like sunscreen and cigarettes.

"You talk too much for a guy living in a shed," he said quietly.

"Move your trailer."

"Or what?"

"Or I call the sheriff and tell him you broke into my shop with a crowbar in broad daylight in front of a man with a camera."

He glanced across the street. Mr. Odell was standing at the end of his driveway in a bucket hat, holding up his phone in both hands like a tourist at the Grand Canyon, filming every second.

Tanner looked back at me. Then he shoved me.

Two hands flat on my chest, hard, like he was trying to push a car out of a ditch. My bad knee buckled and I went backwards over the tongue of the trailer and landed on my hip in the gravel, and my glasses went flying.

"Oops," Tanner said.

I got up. It took me a minute, because I'm sixty-one, and nothing gets up fast at sixty-one. I found my glasses in the gravel and put them back on. One lens was cracked.

Tanner had his fists up now, bouncing a little on his toes, like a man who'd taken two kickboxing classes at a strip-mall gym.

"Come on," he said. "Come on, old man. Swing."

I didn't swing. I took my phone out of my pocket, dialed, and put it on speaker so he could hear.

"McLennan County Sheriff's Office, what's your emergency?"

"This is Lyle Benford on Lake Shore Drive," I said. "I've been assaulted on my property by a man named Tanner Voss, who broke into my business with a crowbar and is trying to steal my equipment. There's video. He's still here."

Tanner's fists came down.

He told his boys to unload the duplicator, and they did, fast, and they set it on the gravel instead of inside, which I suppose was the best I was going to get. Then they jumped in the truck and pulled the trailer out across my side yard, through Sheila's flower bed, and out onto the road the long way around my parked truck.

Deputy Lena Porter got there eleven minutes later. She took my statement and looked at my cracked glasses and walked across the street to watch Mr. Odell's video on his kitchen table. When she came back she had a look on her face I liked.

"That's an assault, Mr. Benford. A clean one," she said. "Plus burglary of a building. I'll write it up and put out a warrant for Mr. Voss." She looked at my bent door frame. "You're the locksmith, right? You're going to want a better door."

"I've got a better door," I said. "I just didn't think I'd need it at my own house."

Three days later, I was the one who got served.

It was a deputy I didn't know, a young man with a buzz cut, at seven in the morning at the door of my shop. He handed me two things. A petition for divorce, filed by Sheila Ann Benford. And a temporary restraining order.

The TRO said I was excluded from the residence on Lake Shore Drive, including all outbuildings, pending a hearing in fourteen days. It said I had "engaged in a pattern of intimidation and physical aggression," including "a violent altercation on the property with a guest of the petitioner" on Monday afternoon.

"A guest of the petitioner," I read out loud.

"Sir, I'm going to need you to collect your personal items and vacate the property," the deputy said. "You can take what you need for two weeks."

"This is my shop. My business is in here."

"I understand, sir. The order says outbuildings."

I stood there with the paper in my hand. Twelve hundred keys on the pegboard. My duplicator still sitting out in the gravel where Tanner's boys had dumped it, with dew on it.

"Can I take the duplicator?"

He looked at it. "Does it fit in your truck?"

"With your help it does."

So a sheriff's deputy helped me load a hundred-and-forty-pound key machine into my pickup at seven in the morning, and then he watched me drive away from my own house.

I spent that night on the sofa bed at my daughter Callie's apartment by the Baylor campus, with the duplicator in her carport under a tarp and my phone on my chest.

At ten, Sheila texted me.

"Told you. Find somewhere else to live."

Callie read it over my shoulder from the kitchen, where she was pretending to wash a pan. She's twenty-eight, teaches eighth-grade science at a middle school on the south side, and has her mother's chin and my mouth, which is to say she says what she thinks about two seconds before she should.

"Dad," she said, "is that guy actually living in our house?"

"In my recliner. In my socks."

"In your socks?"

"The Cabela's ones."

She put the pan down. "Okay, that's disgusting. Mom knows those are your good socks."

"I'm aware."

She came and sat on the arm of the sofa bed. "She called me this morning. Before the deputy came. She said you'd gotten violent with a contractor and I should be careful if you showed up here."

"What'd you say?"

"I said Dad once spent twenty minutes getting a wasp out of the kitchen with a Tupperware so he wouldn't have to kill it." Callie shook her head. "She said people change. I said not that much."

"Thank you."

"Mr. Odell sent me the video, by the way. He found me on Facebook." She pulled it up on her phone. There I was, going over backward across the trailer hitch, my glasses flying, and Tanner standing over me saying oops. Callie watched it with her jaw tight. "I've watched it nine times. He shoved you, Dad. You didn't even put your hands up."

"I put my phone up."

"That's what I mean." She looked at me. "Why are you so calm? She changed the locks. She moved her boyfriend in. She got you kicked out of your own shop with a fake order. Why aren't you yelling?"

I thought about how to explain it to her. Thirty-four years of opening other people's doors teaches you a kind of patience you don't learn anywhere else. A lock's not a wall. It's a puzzle somebody else built. You don't kick it. You find the part they didn't think about.

"Because she's in a hurry," I said. "And I'm not."

The next evening, Sheila showed up at Callie's door.

I heard the knock from the sofa bed, and I heard Callie open it, and I heard my wife's voice say, "Is your father here?" in the same tone she used to use on the cable company.

Callie didn't move out of the doorway. "Hi, Mom."

"Callie, honey, let me in. I need to talk to him."

"You can talk from there."

So Sheila talked from there, over our daughter's shoulder, while I sat on the edge of the sofa bed in my sock feet.

"You called the sheriff on Tanner," she said. "There's a warrant. He can't even go to his own job sites. Do you know what you've done to his business?"

"He broke into mine with a crowbar."

"It was a misunderstanding. He thought you'd moved out."

"He shoved me over a trailer, Sheila. On video."

"You provoked him. You always provoke people. You just stand there with that face and wait for them to get mad."

"I'll work on my face."

"You think you're so smart." She leaned around Callie to see me better. "You think because you've got the old man across the street filming everything, you've won something. You haven't won anything. I've got a lawyer. I've got the house. I've got an order that says you can't come within five hundred feet of it. And by the end of the month, you're going to be living in your truck."

"Mom," Callie said quietly. "Stop."

"Stay out of this, Callie."

"No. You stop." Callie's voice shook a little, but she didn't back up an inch. "That's Dad's house. Grandma Ruby helped pay for it. I was there when he made the last payment, I was thirteen, he took me to Bush's Chicken to celebrate. You weren't even there. You were at Pilates."

Sheila stared at her daughter like she'd never seen her before.

"You don't know anything about it," she said.

"I know more than you do," Callie said, "and I'm the one who got a C in civics."

Sheila turned around and walked back to her red Lexus without another word. I'd never seen that car before in my life. It still had the paper dealer tag in the back window.

Callie closed the door and leaned her forehead against it.

"Where'd she get a Lexus?" she said.

"I have no idea," I said. "But I bet somebody's about to ask."

Marisela didn't sleep much that week either, from the sound of her.

"She's being smart," she said on the phone Friday. "A TRO gets you out of the house right before the closing on the twentieth. If you're out, nobody's there to stop the pool man from walking a title agent through the place for a final inspection. It's a move, Lyle. A good one."

"What's our move?"

"Our move is that I just sent her lawyer everything."

"Everything?"

"Everything. Your answer and counter-petition. The 1998 deed. The marriage certificate. The 2010 release of lien and your mother's estate check. The memorandum of contract and her sworn affidavit, certified copies from the county clerk. Mr. Odell's video of a thirty-nine-year-old man with a crowbar shoving a sixty-one-year-old locksmith over a trailer hitch. Deputy Porter's report number. And a copy of the warrant for Tanner Voss." She sounded almost happy. "All of it in one email with the subject line Your Client's Felony."

"You didn't."

"I wrote Your Client's Affidavit. I'm a professional." She paused. "Her lawyer's name is Brent Ivey. Young guy, good school, sharp. He's going to read it this weekend. Watch your phone."

It rang at a little after nine on Saturday morning, exactly fourteen days after I'd read Sheila's text out loud at the Elks Lodge. I was sitting on Callie's balcony with a cup of coffee and a pair of cracked glasses on my nose.

"Mr. Benford? This is Brent Ivey. I represent your wife, Sheila."

"Morning, Mr. Ivey."

"Please, Brent. Mr. Benford, I know it's unusual to call you directly, but your attorney said it would be all right, and I think, please, I think we should talk before anybody does anything they can't take back."

"What would that be?"

"Please. Look, I've gone through the documents Ms. Cantu sent. All of them. Twice." He took a breath. "Mr. Benford, my client did not tell me about the 1998 deed. She did not tell me about the memorandum of contract. She told me the house was community property and that you'd been abusive. I filed what she gave me."

"And now you've read what I gave you."

"Yes, sir." His voice dropped. "And I'd like to negotiate. Please. Today, if possible."

"Negotiate what, Brent?"

"The TRO, for one. I'm prepared to agree to dissolve it Monday. You can go back to your shop. And my client is prepared to withdraw her claim to the residence entirely, and to discuss a fair division of the community property, the accounts and the vehicles."

"That's generous of her. Since the house was never hers."

"Mr. Benford." He sounded like a young man trying very hard not to sound scared. "There's an affidavit in the public records with my client's signature on it. And there's a warrant out for the man she was going to sell your house to. If the county attorney decides to look at that affidavit, I can't protect her. I'd like to resolve this before anyone decides to look."

"You want me not to press it."

"I want you to let us fix it. We'll file a release of the memorandum. We'll cancel the contract with Voss Property Group. We'll do it all Monday. Please."

That was the fourth please. I counted.

I took a sip of coffee and looked out at the Brazos River, brown and slow behind the apartment buildings.

"Brent," I said. "Is the closing still on for the twentieth?"

There was a pause on the line.

"I'm told the buyers haven't cancelled," he said carefully. "Mr. Voss is apparently still telling them it's going forward. My client says she doesn't know where he is."

"I bet she knows where he is."

"Mr. Benford—"

"Here's my counteroffer," I said. "Tell Sheila to go to the closing. Brazos Title on Valley Mills, ten o'clock Monday. That's where she wanted to be. Tell her to bring her pen."

"Why on earth would she go to the closing?"

"Because Tanner's going to be there," I said. "Two hundred eighty thousand dollars in cash is going to be there. And I'd like her to see what happens when the title officer opens the file."

Monday, the twentieth, ten o'clock. Brazos Title has a conference room with a long walnut table, a bowl of peppermints and a framed aerial photo of Waco from 1972. I got there at nine-forty-five with Marisela and the blue folder. The title officer, Gloria Banda, was a tidy woman in her fifties with reading glasses on a beaded chain, and she'd been in the business twenty-six years. She looked at me, then at Marisela, then at the file in front of her.

"Mr. Benford," she said. "I got your attorney's notice Friday. I've pulled the chain of title back to 1971." She tapped her pen on the folder. "We'll see how this goes."

The buyers came in at ten on the dot. Two men in golf shirts and boat shoes, one older, one younger, with a briefcase between them. They introduced themselves as investors from Austin and didn't look at me twice. They thought I was somebody's uncle.

Sheila came in at five after ten, with Brent Ivey a step behind her looking like he'd swallowed a peppermint whole. She had on her good blue dress and sunglasses she didn't take off. She saw me at the end of the table and stopped.

"What is he doing here?" she said.

"He owns the house," Gloria Banda said pleasantly. "Have a seat, Mrs. Benford."

Sheila sat at the far end of the table, as far from me as the room allowed, and lined up her pen next to her coffee cup like a woman getting ready to sign the best paper of her life. She still thought she was.

Tanner Voss came in last, at twelve after ten, in a pressed white shirt and a sport coat that didn't fit his shoulders. He had a fresh haircut. He was smiling until he saw me, and then he wasn't.

"What the hell is this?" he said.

"This is your closing, Mr. Voss," Gloria said. "Sit down."

He sat. The two men from Austin looked at him. He looked at Sheila. Sheila looked at her lap.

Gloria opened the file.

"Before we sign anything," she said, "I'm required to go over the title commitment. The property is located on Lake Shore Drive. The seller listed in the contract is Sheila Ann Benford, individually. However." She lifted a page. "Our title search shows the property was conveyed by warranty deed in April of 1998 to Lyle Wayne Benford, a single man. There is no subsequent deed out of Mr. Benford. There is no deed to Mrs. Benford. The lien was released in 2010. And on Friday, a notice of claim of ownership was recorded by Mr. Benford's attorney."

The older Austin man leaned forward. "Which means what, exactly?"

"Which means Mrs. Benford doesn't own the property she's selling you," Gloria said. "Mr. Benford does. And Mr. Benford is not selling."

There was a sound in that room like somebody letting air out of a tire.

"That's not right," Tanner said. "That's not— She's his wife. It's Texas. Wives get half."

"Of community property," Marisela said. "This isn't."

"She signed an affidavit!"

"Yes, she did," Gloria said. "I have a copy. It states under oath that the property is community property and she has full authority to convey it. Our underwriter has flagged it." She took off her reading glasses. "Brazos Title will not be insuring this transaction. I'll also be notifying our underwriting counsel about the affidavit, as I'm required to."

The younger Austin man turned to Tanner very slowly.

"You said it was clean," he said. "You stood in our office and said you'd walked the title yourself. You said the husband was a nobody living in a shed."

"He is living in a shed!"

"You took twenty-eight thousand dollars of our earnest money last month."

Tanner's face went red from the collar up. "That money's in escrow."

"It's not in escrow, Tanner." The older man had his phone out now, scrolling. "Our bank says it cleared to Voss Pools operating on the third. And from there, I'd guess, to that red Lexus your girlfriend's been driving." He jerked his chin at Sheila. "Nice car, ma'am."

Every head in the room turned to Sheila.

"He said it was a gift," she said. Her voice was very small. "He said the closing would cover it. Tanner, you told me Texas splits everything down the middle. You told me you'd checked. You said all I had to do was sign."

"You told me he never read the mail!" Tanner was on his feet now. "You said he was a dumb old locksmith who'd roll over! You said the house was half yours and he'd never fight!"

"I said I didn't know whose name was on it!"

"You swore you did!"

"You told me what to swear!"

Brent Ivey put his face in his hands.

Tanner turned to me then, finally, all that gym shoulder and bad haircut, and pointed a finger across the walnut table.

"This is your fault," he said. "You set us up."

"I didn't set anything," I said. "You changed a thirty-four-dollar lock on a locksmith and recorded a lie at the county courthouse. All I did was read my mail."

He came around the table. He was faster than he looked. Marisela said "Lyle" and pushed her chair back, and the younger Austin man stood up too, and for a second I thought I was going to end up on my back on Gloria Banda's carpet the same way I'd ended up in my own gravel.

The conference room door opened.

Deputy Lena Porter stepped in, in uniform, with her hat in one hand. Behind her was a second deputy I didn't know, a big man with a mustache.

"Tanner Voss?" she said. "I've been calling your phone for two weeks. You're a hard man to find. Turns out all I had to do was come to your closing."

Tanner stopped halfway around the table. He looked at the door. He looked at the window. We were on the second floor.

"Don't," Deputy Porter said. "I've had a long morning."

She cuffed him right there by the bowl of peppermints. Assault. Burglary of a building. The deputy with the mustache read him his rights while the two men from Austin watched with their arms folded, and the older one said, loud enough for everybody, "And theft. Write down theft. Twenty-eight thousand dollars."

"Take it up with the county attorney, sir," Porter said. "He's going to have a busy week."

They walked Tanner out. At the door, he turned his head and looked back at Sheila.

"Don't call me," he said. "Don't you ever call me."

Then he was gone, and it was just the rest of us at the long walnut table, and the peppermints, and Waco from 1972.

Sheila took off her sunglasses. Her mascara was a mess. She looked about seventy.

"Lyle," she said. "Lyle, I'm sorry. I didn't know about the deed. I swear I didn't. Tanner said—"

"You lived in that house twenty-three years," I said. "You put your hand on a cedar chest every day to set your purse down. The deed was right there under my mother's quilts."

"I didn't look."

"I know. That's the whole problem."

"What happens to me now?" she said. "Brent says I could be charged. Brent says the county attorney could— Lyle, I can't go to jail over a house."

"It's not over a house. It's over an affidavit."

"Please. Twenty-three years. Please don't let them."

I looked at Brent Ivey. Brent looked at me like a man watching a coin in the air.

Gloria Banda slid a single sheet of paper across the table to me, with a pen. It was a release, prepared that morning by Marisela, to be recorded with the county clerk. It said that the memorandum of contract filed by Sheila Ann Benford was void, that the property at Lake Shore Drive belonged solely to Lyle Wayne Benford, and that the seller, by her signature below, agreed.

There were two signature lines. One for her. One for me.

I signed mine. Then I turned the paper around and set it in front of my wife with the pen on top.

"What happens to you," I said, "is up to the county attorney. Not me. But this paper's going in the record today, with your name on it or without it."

She picked up the pen. Her hand was shaking so bad she had to hold it with both.

"Lyle," she said. "Where am I supposed to live?"

I stood up and put the blue folder under my arm.

"I hear you can find somewhere else," I said. "Good luck with that."
