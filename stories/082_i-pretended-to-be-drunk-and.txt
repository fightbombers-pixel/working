"Tomorrow we take him to the psychiatric hospital. The papers are ready."

My wife whispered it to her sister in my own den, on a Friday night after the fish fry at St. Mark's, about four feet from the recliner where I was slumped with my mouth open and a can of Old Style tipped against my leg. Two people heard it. Her sister, Roxanne. And me.

Twenty-nine years of marriage, and the first time I heard my wife make a plan for my life, I was pretending to be asleep for it.

"Tomorrow?" Roxanne whispered back. "Patty, that's fast."

"Dexter says fast is better. Before he wakes up sober and starts asking questions."

I let out a snore. A long, ugly, rattling one, the kind my son says sounds like a cement mixer with a bad bearing. Then I let my right hand slide off the armrest and hang there over the carpet, loose, the way a drunk man's hand hangs.

The two of them went quiet for a second. Then Patricia laughed, very softly.

"See? He's out. Six beers and he's gone. That's the head injury, Rox. That's exactly what Dexter wrote."

Sixteen hours later, my wife was standing in the intake hallway of a hospital in East Chicago with those same papers in her hand, and she was watching a psychiatrist read them out loud to a sheriff's deputy, and her hands were shaking.

What she didn't know was that my phone was lying face down under the afghan on my lap, and it had been recording since my second beer.

My name is Vernon Kidd. I'm 57 years old, and I've been an ironworker out of Local 395 in Gary, Indiana, for thirty-four years. When you walk steel forty stories up, you learn to check every connection twice, because the one bolt you didn't check is the one that kills somebody. Two years ago, a connection I didn't set gave out on a job in Hammond, and I fell twenty-two feet onto a deck of rebar. Remember that fall. Everybody else in this story did.

"What if he won't go?" Roxanne whispered.

"He'll go. Deputies take him. It's an emergency detention. Dexter filled out the doctor part, I filled out the rest, and a judge signed it this afternoon. They just show up and take him for seventy-two hours."

"And then what?"

"And then Dexter's evaluation says what it says, and Monday my lawyer files for guardianship. And then I'm in charge of the money. All of it. The settlement, the pension, the house."

"Four hundred thousand," Roxanne said, like she was tasting it.

"Four hundred and twelve," Patricia said. "I'm sorry, but I've earned every penny of it. Two years of cutting his meat and listening to him forget where he put his keys."

I had never once forgotten where I put my keys. They go on the nail by the back door. They've gone on that nail since 1997.

"You're sure Dexter's okay signing?" Roxanne asked. "After the Joliet thing?"

"That was nothing," Patricia said quickly. "That was Illinois. Don't bring that up again."

"I'm just saying..."

"Don't."

There was a car horn outside. Roxanne's ride. I heard the two of them kiss cheeks at the front door, and Roxanne said, "Love you, Patty," and Patricia said, "Sorry for keeping you so late," because my wife apologizes for everything, even for plotting to have her husband locked up. Then the door shut.

Patricia came back into the den. I heard her stop in front of my chair. I kept my breathing slow and my mouth open, and I could feel her looking at me for a long time.

Then she took the empty beer can out from against my leg, very gently, and set it on the side table. She pulled the afghan up a little higher on my chest. She almost touched my hair.

"I'm sorry, Vern," she whispered. "It's just easier this way."

And she turned off the lamp and went upstairs.

I lay there in the dark until I heard the shower run and stop, and the bedroom door close, and the house go quiet. Then I waited another hour, because forty stories up you don't move until you're sure the beam won't swing.

Around three, I picked up my phone, slipped my boots on at the back door, took my keys off the nail, and walked out to my truck in my socks with the boots in my hand so the porch wouldn't creak.

I sat in the driver's seat with the dome light off and played the recording back with the phone pressed against my ear. Two hours and eleven minutes of the fish fry talk, the Bears, Roxanne's ex-husband, and then, at an hour and fifty-two minutes, my wife's voice saying, "Tomorrow we take him to the psychiatric hospital. The papers are ready."

I listened to it three times. Then I called my son.

Caleb is 24, and he's a second-year apprentice in my local, which means he gets up at four anyway. He picked up on the second ring.

"Dad? What's wrong? Is it your head?"

"My head's fine. That's sort of the problem."

"What?"

"I need you to listen to something. Don't say anything until it's over."

I played it for him. The whole part. When it ended, I could hear him breathing on the other end.

"That's Mom," he said.

"That's your mom."

"Who's Dexter?"

"Dr. Dexter Rowland. Family doctor. Runs Rowland Family Care over on Broadway in Merrillville. Your mother's been his office manager for three years."

"The guy with the boat? The guy who came to Grandma Agnes's Fourth of July and kept whistling?"

"That's the guy."

"Dad." His voice went flat, the way mine does. "Is she..."

"I don't know what she is with him, son. I know she's in his office forty hours a week, and I know he signed a paper saying I'm crazy, and I've never been in his office in my life."

"What do you need?"

"Two things. Meet me at the Methodist ER in Merrillville at five. And don't call your mother."

"Why the ER?"

"Because if they're going to say I'm out of my mind tomorrow," I said, "I want a real doctor who's never met any of us to say I'm not, tonight."

The emergency room at Methodist at five in the morning on a Saturday is a place where nobody's in a hurry and everybody's tired. There was a man asleep in the waiting room with his arm in a bag of ice and a woman feeding quarters into a vending machine that wasn't giving her anything.

The triage nurse looked at me over the counter.

"What brings you in, sir?"

"I'd like a psychiatric evaluation, please. And a blood alcohol test."

She stopped typing.

"Are you having thoughts of hurting yourself?"

"No, ma'am."

"No, ma'am. I'm having thoughts that somebody's going to say I'm having those thoughts, and I'd like it on paper that I'm not."

She looked at Caleb, standing behind me in his hoodie with his hands jammed in the pocket. He nodded.

"It's real," Caleb said. "We have a recording."

They took my blood. They put me in a curtained bay with a gown I didn't put on. At a little before seven, a woman in a white coat with dark hair pulled back in a clip pulled the curtain aside. Her badge said Dr. Nadia Ferrante, Psychiatry.

"Mr. Kidd. I'm told you came in asking to be evaluated. That's unusual."

"It's an unusual morning, Doctor."

"Your blood alcohol is point-zero-one. That's basically a sip of mouthwash."

"I had two beers at eight o'clock last night. I poured the other four into my wife's ficus."

She almost smiled, then caught it.

"Tell me why you're here."

So I told her. The fall in Hammond, the settlement, the fish fry, the recliner, the whisper. I played her the part of the recording with the papers. She listened without moving, and when it ended, she pulled a stool over and sat down.

"I'm going to do a full evaluation," she said. "The real one. Memory, orientation, judgment, the whole hour. If you have a deficit, I'm going to write it down, Mr. Kidd. I'm not on your side. I'm on the side of whatever's true."

"That's all I want."

It took fifty minutes. She had me count backward by sevens, draw a clock, remember three words and say them back to her twenty minutes later. Apple, table, penny. She asked me who the president was, what I'd done on my last job and what I'd do if I found a stamped envelope on the sidewalk. I told her I'd mail it, because somebody went to the trouble of putting a stamp on it.

At the end she wrote for a long time.

"You have no evidence of a cognitive disorder, Mr. Kidd. You have no signs of psychosis, no danger to yourself or others, no intoxication. Your judgment, frankly, is better than mine at this hour." She signed the page and handed me a copy. "If someone says otherwise, they're either mistaken or they're lying. That's in here too, in nicer words."

"Thank you, Doctor."

"Don't thank me. Go home. And Mr. Kidd? The physician who signed that statement. Do you know if he ever examined you?"

"Never met me in a room."

"Then that's not my problem anymore," she said. "That's somebody else's."

Indiana says what's in a joint account belongs to both of us. So I did what any lawyer would tell you to do. I moved half into a new account in my own name, $206,302, and not one dollar more.

The teller, a young guy with a goatee, looked at the amount twice.

"Big day, Mr. Kidd?"

"You have no idea," I said.

My phone buzzed against the counter. A text from Patricia.

"Where are you?? Your truck is gone. We need to go somewhere at ten. It's important. Please just come home. I'm sorry."

I didn't go home at ten. I drove to the union hall on Ripley Street, made photocopies of Dr. Ferrante's report, one for my glove box, one for my wallet and one for Caleb, and I sat in the parking lot eating a gas station breakfast sandwich while my phone buzzed itself across the dashboard.

Ten-fifteen. "Vern where ARE you."

Ten-forty. "This isn't funny. I'm worried sick."

Eleven-thirty. "Roxanne's here. We're scared for you. Please."

Patricia was standing on the front porch with Roxanne. She was wearing her church cardigan, the yellow one, and she had a manila envelope pressed against her chest with both arms. Two deputies were standing on the walk. One was young, maybe thirty, with a shaved head. The other was older, gray mustache, sergeant's stripes, and a body camera clipped to his chest with a little red light.

"There he is," Patricia said, and her voice cracked right on cue. "Oh, Vern. Thank God."

"Afternoon, officers."

"Vernon Kidd?" the sergeant said.

"That's me."

"I understand, Sergeant. I'll come."

Patricia blinked. She'd been expecting a scene. I could see it on her face. She'd probably told the deputies I'd fight, because a man who fell twenty-two feet onto rebar and hit his head must be unpredictable.

"You'll come?" she said.

"Sure. I like hospitals. They've been very nice to me today."

"Today?"

I reached into my wallet slowly, so nobody got nervous, and pulled out the folded copy of Dr. Ferrante's report.

They didn't cuff me. St. Catherine's has a behavioral health unit on the fourth floor. The intake hallway is long and pale green with chairs bolted to the wall. "Yes, sir."

"Right here."

"Off the record, Mr. Kidd," he said, "I've been doing this nineteen years. I've seen a lot of families try to put somebody away. I've never seen the somebody show up with his own psychiatrist."

"I walk steel, Sergeant. You check the connection before you put your weight on it."

"Vern," she said. "Please. Let me drive you home."

"Caleb's coming."

"Caleb knows?"

"Caleb heard it at four this morning."

"I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I don't know what I was thinking. I was scared. Your head, the way you've been since the fall, the forgetting..."

"Things."

"Who do you think?"

On Monday morning, I was sitting in the office of a lawyer named Gwen Ashcraft on Main Street in Crown Point, across from the old courthouse. She'd done my workers' comp case after the fall. She's in her fifties, wears cowboy boots with her suits and keeps a jar of root beer barrels on her desk that she won't let anybody touch.

"Doctors sleep with office managers every day in this county, and most of them don't commit felonies for it. Something else is going on."

It said Revoked. Below it was a link to a public order from the Illinois Department of Financial and Professional Regulation, four years earlier.

Dr. Rowland had certified that a 68-year-old patient named Harold Pruett was incompetent to manage his affairs. The patient's wife, who worked in Dr. Rowland's office, was appointed guardian. Within eight months, about $300,000 of Mr. Pruett's money had gone through accounts the wife controlled. That night, I was asleep upstairs when my phone buzzed on the nightstand. It wasn't a text. "Can you send this to me?" I said.

"I already sent it to you. And to the detective. Patty was screaming this morning that some woman detective showed up at Dexter's clinic, so I called the sheriff's office and asked who it was. Bustamante." Roxanne wiped her eyes with the back of her hand. "Vern, I'm not a good person. I wanted that money too. I thought about what I'd do with my cut. But I'm not going to stand in front of a judge and say you threatened my sister over a ham."

"Okay, Rox."

"There's something else."

"There's always something else."

"Saturday is Mom's 80th. At the VFW in Hobart. Seventy people. The whole family, the church ladies, everybody." She took a deep breath. "Patty's bringing Dexter. As her date. She's going to stand up and tell everybody you attacked him in your garage when he came to check on the furnace for her. She's got a speech written. She read it to me this afternoon."

"With the stitches."

"With the stitches. She says if the family's on her side before the hearing, the judge will see it."

I looked at the phone in my hand. Our anniversary. 0412.

"Rox," I said, "is your mother still mad at me about the time I fixed her garbage disposal and didn't take any money?"

Roxanne laughed through her crying.

"She says it's the only thing that works in that house."

"Then tell her I'll be there Saturday," I said. "And tell her to save me a seat."

The VFW Post in Hobart has a hall with wood paneling, a drop ceiling, a bar along one wall and a little stage where they do bingo on Tuesdays. On Saturday it had seventy people in it, a buffet of pulled pork and mostaccioli, a sheet cake that said Happy 80th Agnes in blue frosting, and a banner over the stage that somebody's grandkid had painted.

Agnes Dillon sat at the head table in a purple blouse with a corsage on her wrist, holding court. She's four foot eleven, a retired lunch lady from Lew Wallace High School, and she has been telling people exactly what she thinks of them since the Truman administration.

I came in with Caleb a little after seven. People looked up. Word gets around a family fast, and some of them had heard one version and some had heard another, and none of them knew which one to believe yet. A cousin of Patricia's turned her back on me at the bar. One of the church ladies gave me a little wave.

Agnes saw me and pointed her fork.

"Vernon. Sit. You're late."

"Sorry, Agnes. Happy birthday."

"Don't sorry me. You sound like my daughter."

I sat down next to her. Caleb stood behind my chair with his arms folded.

Patricia and Dexter came in about ten minutes later.

She was wearing a blue dress I'd never seen. He was in a sport coat and khakis, with his right hand wrapped in a big white bandage, carrying it in front of him like a trophy. The room got quieter as they crossed it. Patricia went straight to the stage, picked up the bingo microphone and tapped it. It squealed.

"Hi, everybody. Sorry. I'm sorry, I know it's Mom's day. I'll be quick." She looked at a folded piece of paper. "Most of you know Vern and I are going through a hard time. Most of you know about his fall, and his head, and how hard it's been. What you don't know is that on Wednesday night, when my friend Dexter went by our house to check on the furnace for me, Vern attacked him in the garage. With a pipe." She pointed at the bandage. "Eleven stitches. That's who I've been living with. I'm not angry. I'm scared. And I'm asking all of you to support me at the hearing, because Vern needs help, and I'm the only one trying to get it for him."

There was a murmur. Somebody said, "Oh my Lord." A man near the buffet looked over at me like he was ready to come across the room.

Dexter stood beside the stage with his bandaged hand and a sad, patient, doctor's face. Then he started to whistle under his breath, the way he does when he's nervous. Nobody else noticed. I did.

I stood up.

"Agnes," I said, "is it all right if I say something at your party?"

"Somebody'd better," Agnes said. "That one's had the microphone long enough."

I didn't go to the stage. I didn't want the microphone. I just stood at the head table, which is the advantage of being a big man with a big voice in a small hall.

"Patty's right about one thing," I said. "Dexter was in my garage on Wednesday night. At one in the morning. In a hoodie and rubber gloves."

"He was checking the furnace," Patricia said into the mic.

"My furnace is in the basement, Patty. You know that. You've been nagging me to replace it since 2016." People laughed, a few of them. "He was in the garage, in my workbench drawer, holding my laptop. And when I walked in, he swung a piece of steel conduit at my head. He cut his hand on my vise when I took it away from him. There's a camera."

"That's a lie," Dexter said. "I've already given my statement."

"I'm sure you have. You give a lot of statements." I looked at the room. "Two weeks ago, Dexter signed a paper swearing he examined me in his office on a Thursday afternoon and found me confused and dangerous. That Thursday afternoon, I was on the 38th floor of a building in Chicago. I've never been in his office. Patty used that paper to have deputies take me to the psych ward. The psychiatrist there sent me home in four hours."

"You're twisting it," Patricia said. "Vern, you're confused, you don't..."

"How did he get in the garage, Patty?"

She stopped.

"The side door wasn't broken. It's got a keypad. Four numbers." I looked at her. "Do you want to tell them the code, or should Roxanne?"

Patricia looked at her sister. Roxanne was standing by the cake table with her hands pressed together. She didn't look away this time.

"It's 0412," Roxanne said, clear enough for the whole hall. "Their anniversary. Patty sent it to Dexter on Tuesday night. I'm in the text. I'm so sorry, Mom."

Agnes put her fork down very slowly.

"You gave that man the code to your husband's garage," she said. "With your anniversary."

"Mom, you don't understand..."

"I understand fine. I'm eighty, not dead."

The back door of the hall opened. Detective Ines Bustamante came in with two uniformed deputies behind her. She didn't hurry. She walked straight across the room, past the buffet and the bar and seventy staring faces, and stopped in front of Dexter Rowland.

"Dexter Rowland?"

"Yes. Officer, this man assaulted me..."

"I've seen the video, Doctor. And the texts. I have a warrant for your arrest for residential burglary, battery with a deadly weapon and perjury. Turn around, please."

"This is a family party!"

"Then you shouldn't have brought a crime to it," Bustamante said. "Hands behind your back. Mind the stitches."

He turned. The deputy put one cuff on his good wrist and then, carefully, the other one over the bandage, and Dexter Rowland made a small whimpering sound. He looked across the hall at Patricia.

"Patty. Call my lawyer. The number's in my phone."

"Your phone's evidence, Doctor," Bustamante said. "So's hers."

She turned to Patricia, who was still standing on the bingo stage with the microphone hanging at her side.

"Mrs. Kidd, I'll need to talk with you as well. Not tonight, it's your mother's birthday. Monday morning, my office, ten o'clock. I'd bring a lawyer."

"I didn't do anything. I just... I just sent a number."

"Then you can tell me why on Monday."

They walked Dexter out through the hall. He was whistling again, under his breath, all the way to the door, and then he stopped.

Patricia came down off the stage. She walked over to the head table, and the room parted for her the way it does for somebody with a bad smell, and she stopped in front of me.

"Vern," she said. "Vern, I didn't know about Joliet. I swear to God. He told me it was the easiest way. He said you'd be taken care of. He said nobody would get hurt."

"You told your sister you earned that money cutting my meat."

"I was angry."

"You told the deputies I was confused."

"I was scared."

"And you gave a man our anniversary so he could get into my garage while I was sleeping upstairs."

She opened her mouth. Nothing came out. Her eyes filled up.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

"I know, Patty," I said. "You always are."

Caleb put his hand on my shoulder, the one that still hurt. I didn't tell him.

Agnes picked up the cake knife from the table, looked at her daughter, then looked at me, and pushed the knife across the tablecloth into my hand.

"Vernon," she said, "you cut it. You're the only one in this family I'd trust with a knife tonight."
