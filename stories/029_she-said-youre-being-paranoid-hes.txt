"You were home for four days, Wes. You don't owe anybody an apology."

"What happens now?"

"Saturday happens," I said. "Then we'll see."

"Call me Saturday night. Whatever happens. I'll be up."

"I will."

"Dad?" He paused. "You're not dumb. Mom told me that's what he calls you. You're not dumb. You're the smartest guy I know. You built me a treehouse with a working elevator when I was eight."

"It was a pulley and a bucket, Wes."

"It was an elevator," my son said. "Call me Saturday."

The Spring Bridal Showcase filled the whole Laurel Ballroom and spilled out onto the terrace. Florists, photographers, cake bakers, three different DJs who all played the same Lonestar song, and maybe four hundred brides and mothers and a few sorry-looking grooms walking from table to table with tote bags. My booth was by the big windows looking out at the Blue Ridge, with two cedar arbors and a walnut sign that said MR. & MRS. in letters I'd carved by hand over the winter.

I'd carved that sign before February. I'd thought about it a lot this week.

Rochelle Tilden came in at a quarter past eleven, in a navy suit, with a young man in a cheap blazer carrying a manila envelope. She walked past my booth and stopped.

"This is Kenny," she said. "He serves papers for our firm. He's got one for your wife. I filed Thursday."

"Frances Upchurch filed mine Friday," I said. "Leon's got Craig's."

Leon was over by the cake tables, eating a sample of red velvet, with a manila envelope of his own under one arm. He raised the fork at me.

Craig Tilden was behind the tasting tables in a white chef's coat, handing out little plates of shrimp and grits to brides, laughing too loud. He was a good-looking man, I'll give him that, tall, with a lot of dark hair that he obviously spent money on. My wife was across the room by the dance floor in a gray suit, holding a clipboard, smiling at a bride and her mother with the exact same smile she'd given me at the Ponders' cookout.

She hadn't come home since Monday.

At eleven-thirty, Victor Amato came in through the service door in a red bow tie, with a woman from corporate in a black suit, and a tall, white-haired man in a sport coat who walked like he owned every room he'd ever been in. Behind them came Deputy Lena Fuentes.

The white-haired man walked straight to the tasting tables.

"Mr. Tilden," he said. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't have to. "I'm Judge Anthony Moretti. Retired. You billed me nine hundred and forty dollars for two nights in Suite 410 after my daughter's wedding. You told my wife it was a system error."

Craig had a little plate of shrimp and grits in each hand. He looked at the judge. He looked at Victor. He looked at the deputy.

"Judge Moretti, sir, I can explain."

"I'm sure you can," the judge said. "Mr. Amato's accountants say you can explain it about forty times over."

Victor stepped up beside him.

"Craig, as of this moment you're terminated for cause. Fraud against our guests. Corporate's already frozen your access. Deputy Fuentes is here because Judge Moretti and two other families have filed complaints for obtaining property by false pretenses."

The whole tasting area went quiet. Four hundred brides is a lot of people to go quiet at once.

Craig set down the plates. Then he turned around and pointed across the room, at the dance floor.

"It wasn't me! Ask her! She's the events manager! She controls the room blocks! I didn't even know which rooms were open. She'd text me the room number. She set the whole thing up!"

Every head in the Laurel Ballroom swung toward my wife in her gray suit.

Lacey's clipboard hit the floor.

"Craig," she said. "You told me it was comped. You told me the rooms were free."

"I never told you that."

"You told me in the Tahoe! In the Ingles parking lot! You said, 'Nobody pays for those, babe, they just sit there!'"

A bride near the tasting table said "Babe?" out loud, and her mother shushed her.

Victor turned to my wife.

"Lacey," he said, and his voice was sorry. "I need your badge and your keys. You're terminated too."

She just stood there. Rochelle's boy Kenny walked up to her, very polite, the way they teach them, and held out the envelope.

"Mrs. Lacey Tolliver? You've been served."

She took it because she didn't know what else to do. She looked down at it. She looked up across the ballroom and found me by the windows, next to the walnut sign that said MR. & MRS.

Leon Mayfield was already behind the tasting table, holding out his envelope to Craig.

"Craig Tilden? Byron Tolliver versus Craig Tilden, Buncombe County Superior Court. Alienation of affection. Criminal conversation. You've been served, brother."

Craig slapped the envelope out of his hand onto the floor. Deputy Fuentes picked it up and put it gently back in his hand.

"Sir, you're going to want to keep that," she said. "Turn around for me, please."

She cuffed him right there next to the shrimp and grits. Craig's face had gone the color of a cheap paper plate. As she walked him past my booth toward the service door, he looked at me, at the arbors and the carved sign, and he stopped short so fast the deputy had to catch his elbow.

"You," he said. "You're the carpenter. You're supposed to be dumb."

I picked up the walnut sign that said MR. & MRS. and turned it face down on the table.

"I check the joints in the back, Craig," I said. "You should've opened the doors."
