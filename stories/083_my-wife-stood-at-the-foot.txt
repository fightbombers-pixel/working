"I paid for this cruise, Elton. I'm not wasting it on your heart."

My wife said it at the foot of my bed in the emergency room at Clear Lake Regional, on a Sunday morning, with the curtain pulled back and a nurse, a doctor and our grown son standing right there. Three people heard it. The nurse stopped writing on her clipboard.

I had a tube in my arm, two little stents in an artery that had been ninety percent blocked two hours earlier, and a monitor beeping next to my ear. The ship left Galveston at four o'clock. It was eleven-fifteen.

"Mom," our son Travis said. "Are you serious?"

"I'm completely serious," Charlene said. "The doctor said he's stable. Stable means fine. The tickets were forty-eight hundred dollars, nonrefundable, and I've been looking forward to this for a year."

I looked at her. Thirty-one years of marriage. Pink sundress, big sunglasses pushed up on her head, her rolling suitcase standing right behind her by the curtain. She'd brought the suitcase into the ER.

"Go," I said.

She blinked. I don't think she expected it to be that easy. Then she smiled, kissed my forehead, and grabbed the handle of her suitcase.

Before she reached the curtain, I picked my phone up off the tray table and made the first call.

Seven days later, when her ship came home to Galveston, she'd come down the gangway with three hundred other sunburned people, and she'd stop dead halfway down, with her hand on the rail, because of who was standing at the bottom waiting for her.

What she didn't know, wheeling her suitcase out through the ER doors, was that the forty-eight hundred dollars for that cruise had been charged to my credit card, and somebody in a cubicle in Miami was about to read me every change that had been made to it.

My name is Elton Spradlin. I'm fifty-eight years old, and I've been an instrument technician at the refinery in Texas City for thirty years. I calibrate the gauges and valves and pressure transmitters that keep a plant full of hot oil from blowing up. The one thing that job teaches you is that you never trust one reading. You check it against a second, and you check that against a third. When three instruments say the same thing, it's true. Hold on to that.

"Dad," Travis said. "Who are you calling?"

"You," I said. "You're call number one. Hang on."

His phone buzzed in his pocket. He looked at it, and then at me, confused.

"Travis," I said into my phone, while he stood three feet away from me. "I'm going to need you to do some things for me. Starting with going to my house and getting my laptop, and the blue folder out of my desk drawer."

"Dad, you're lying in the ER."

"I'm lying in the ER with my phone. That's two things a man needs."

The nurse, a heavy, gray-haired woman named Trudy with reading glasses on a chain, put her clipboard down.

"Mr. Spradlin," she said, "your heart rate's going up. Who was that woman?"

"My wife."

"Uh-huh," Trudy said. "Well. I've been working ER for twenty-six years, and I've seen a lot of things. That's a new one." She picked up her clipboard again. "You make your calls, honey. Just do it lying down."

The doctor, a young cardiologist named Dr. Ibarra who'd put the stents in, cleared his throat.

"Mr. Spradlin, you had a significant heart attack. You need to rest. No stress."

"Doc," I said, "I'm about to get rid of the stress. This is the plan for that."

Travis came back at one-thirty with my laptop, the blue folder, and a cheeseburger from Whataburger he wasn't supposed to give me, which Trudy confiscated on sight.

Travis is twenty-nine. He's an electrician at a plant in La Porte. He's big like my side of the family and quiet like my side of the family, and for the last hour he'd been sitting in the ER waiting room getting madder, which on my side of the family looks exactly like sitting still.

"Dad," he said, pulling a chair up to the bed. "What is going on? Mom just walked out. She walked out of the ER with a suitcase. I keep waiting for somebody to tell me I imagined it."

"You didn't imagine it."

"Who's she even going with? Aunt Lorene?"

"That's what I want to find out," I said. "Hand me the folder."

The blue folder is where I keep everything. Mortgage. Insurance. Truck title. The credit card statements, every month, because I read them, every line, every month. Charlene used to laugh at me for it. "Who reads their credit card statement?" A man who calibrates gauges for a living, that's who.

The cruise was in there. I'd printed the confirmation in March when she booked it. Seven nights, Western Caribbean, out of Galveston. Cozumel, Roatan, Costa Maya. Balcony cabin, deck nine. Two guests: Charlene Spradlin and Elton Spradlin. Forty-eight hundred and twelve dollars, charged to my Visa, the one with her name on it as an authorized user.

"I paid for this cruise," I said out loud.

"What?"

"That's what she said. 'I paid for this cruise.'" I held up the printout. "My card. My name on it. She booked it in March, on my card, as a thirtieth anniversary trip. For both of us."

Travis took the page and read it.

"Then who's using your ticket?"

That was the question.

At two o'clock, Charlene sent me a text. A picture. Her in a white deck chair on a balcony with a glass of champagne, the gray Galveston water behind her. "Made it! Feel better!!! Don't be grumpy." Three hearts.

At two-oh-five, I made call number two. To my bank, Texas First, on the 800 number. I spent nine minutes on hold and four minutes with a nice young man named Darnell, and when I hung up, my direct deposit from the refinery was going to a brand-new checking account in my name only, starting with the paycheck on Friday. The joint account stayed exactly where it was, with exactly what was in it. I didn't touch a dime of it.

At two-thirty, I made call number three. The Visa company.

"I'd like to remove an authorized user from my account," I said.

"Certainly, sir. Can I ask the reason?"

"She's on a cruise ship, and I'm in a hospital bed."

There was a little pause. "I'm so sorry, sir. Effective immediately?"

"Effective immediately."

"Done. Her card will decline on the next transaction. Is there anything else?"

"Yes," I said. "There's a cruise on this card from March. Can you tell me if anything else got charged to it later? Any changes?"

She typed for a while.

"Yes, sir. There's a charge from the cruise line on the eighth of this month. One hundred fifty dollars. It's coded as a passenger name change fee."

I sat up in the bed. Trudy, out at the nurses' station, looked over at my monitor and frowned.

"The eighth of this month," I said. "That's eighteen days ago."

"Yes, sir."

"Thank you," I said, and hung up, and looked at my son.

"Eighteen days ago," Travis said slowly. "Before your heart attack."

"Before my heart attack," I said. "She changed the name on my ticket eighteen days ago. She was never planning to take me."

Travis stood up. He walked to the window, which looked out on a parking garage, and put both hands flat on the sill.

"Dad," he said, with his back to me. "I want to go to Galveston right now and get on a speedboat."

"The ship's gone, son. It left at four."

"I don't care."

"Sit down," I said. "I need you here. I've got four more calls to make."

Call number four was to the cruise line. It took fifty-five minutes on hold, listening to steel drums, before a woman named Kiara in Miami came on the line.

I gave her the booking number. I gave her my name, my address, my date of birth, and the last four of my card.

"I see the reservation, Mr. Spradlin. Deck nine, balcony. Sailed today."

"I'm the person who paid for it," I said. "I'm the original second passenger. I'm not on the ship. I'm in a hospital. Somebody changed my name on the reservation on the eighth, and charged it to my card. I need to know who's in my cabin."

She hesitated.

"Sir, I'm not sure I'm allowed to…"

"Ma'am, I'm the card holder, and I'm the person who was removed. If you need me to dispute the charge to find out, I will. But I'd rather you just tell me."

Another pause. I could hear her typing. Then her voice changed, got quieter, the way people's voices do when they decide to be a person instead of a company.

"The second guest on the reservation as of the eighth," Kiara said, "is a Mr. Kirk Gilliam."

I knew the name.

Everybody in League City knew the name. Kirk Gilliam taught the six a.m. boot camp class at the YMCA on Butler Road, the one Charlene had started going to in January. Fifty-two years old, shaved head, tan, the kind of man who wears a headset microphone to yell at middle-aged women about burpees. His wife Jeannie ran the front desk at our dentist's office. Jeannie had been handing me appointment cards across that front desk for fifteen years.

"Thank you, Kiara," I said. "You've been a big help."

"Mr. Spradlin," she said. "I hope you feel better. I really do."

I put the phone down on the blanket.

"Who?" Travis said.

"Kirk Gilliam."

"The YMCA guy? Mom's boot camp guy?"

"The boot camp guy."

Travis sat back down. For a long minute neither of us said anything. The heart monitor beeped. Out in the hall, somebody's kid was crying.

"Three readings," I said finally.

"What?"

"The card says the name changed eighteen days ago. The cruise line says it's Kirk Gilliam. That's two." I picked up my phone. "Call five is going to be number three."

"Who's call five?"

"Your Aunt Lorene," I said. "She's not on that ship either. And I want to know why."

Lorene Bridges is Charlene's older sister by three years. She's sixty-one, a retired school cafeteria manager in Dickinson, and she's the only person in that family who ever told Charlene no and lived.

She answered on the first ring.

"Elton? Honey, I heard you were in the hospital. Charlene texted me from the car. Are you all right?"

"I've got two new stents and a cheeseburger I'm not allowed to eat. Lorene, I need to ask you something, and I need you to just tell me."

"Okay."

"In March, when Charlene booked this cruise, she told me it was for our thirtieth. Then in May she told me you might come along too, in a separate cabin, because you'd never been on a cruise. And then a couple of weeks ago she said you'd backed out. Is that true?"

The line went quiet.

"I never backed out," Lorene said slowly. "Elton, I was never going. She never asked me. She told me in June that you two were going alone for your anniversary, and I said how sweet."

"Okay."

"Why? Who's she on that ship with?"

"Who do you think, Lorene?"

Another silence. A longer one. When she spoke again, her voice was flat.

"The YMCA man."

"How'd you know?"

"Because she showed me his picture at Easter," Lorene said. "On her phone, in my kitchen. She said, 'Isn't he something?' and I said, 'Charlene, you're married,' and she laughed and said, 'Oh, Lorene, it's just exercise.'" She breathed out hard. "I told myself I was being an old prude. I'm coming up there."

"You don't have to."

"Elton Spradlin, I'm sixty-one years old and my sister just walked out of an emergency room to go on a cruise with her gym teacher. I'm coming up there, and I'm bringing a pie."

She came at six with a buttermilk pie, which Trudy also confiscated, and sat in the chair by my bed while Travis went to get coffee. She held my hand the whole time like I was her own brother, which in a way I was. I'd fixed her air conditioner every summer since 1994.

"There's more," she said, when Travis was out of the room. "I didn't want to say it in front of him. In July, after a couple of glasses of wine at the Fourth of July, she told me something. She said when she got back from this trip, she was going to see a lawyer. She said Kirk wants to open his own gym, on 646, a real one, with a pool, and he needs a partner. She said once the house is sold, her half will be enough." Lorene looked down at our hands. "She said, 'Elton won't fight. Elton's heart isn't in it.'"

"She said that? 'Elton's heart isn't in it'?"

"She thought it was funny."

I looked at the monitor beside my bed, at the little green line going up and down.

"Well," I said. "Turns out she was half right."

On Monday morning, Trudy came on shift at seven and came straight to my room up on the cardiac floor, where they'd moved me overnight. She had a cup of coffee in one hand and a folded piece of paper in the other.

"I'm not your nurse up here," she said. "I'm just visiting. Don't tell anybody."

"I won't."

"How's the ticker?"

"Still ticking."

"Good." She set the coffee down on my tray table, which I wasn't allowed to drink either, and held up the paper. "Mr. Spradlin, I've been doing this twenty-six years, and I'm going to tell you something I've never told a patient. When your wife said what she said down there yesterday, I wrote it down."

"You wrote it down?"

"Word for word. On the back of your chart sheet, with the time. Eleven-sixteen. 'I paid for this cruise, Elton. I'm not wasting it on your heart.'" She unfolded the paper. "And right next to it, your heart rate. It went from eighty-four to a hundred and twelve in about nine seconds. Dr. Ibarra saw it too. He wrote a note in your chart about 'acute emotional stressor in the presence of spouse.' That's doctor talk for what we all saw."

I didn't know what to say.

"Why?" I said finally.

"Because my first husband did something a lot like that to me in 1991," Trudy said, "when I had my gallbladder out. He went to a Garth Brooks concert in San Antonio with my best friend. And when I got divorced, nobody believed me, because nobody had written anything down." She put the paper on my blanket. "This is a copy. The original's in your chart, where it belongs. If your lawyer ever wants it, she can ask the hospital for it, and it'll be there, with my name on it."

"Trudy," I said, "I don't know how to thank you."

"Eat your oatmeal," she said. "That's how. And Mr. Spradlin?"

"Yes, ma'am?"

"Whatever you're doing with those phone calls," she said, "do it slow."

She left. I sat there with that piece of paper in my lap, her neat nurse's handwriting, the time, the words, my heart rate going up in little numbers down the side. Eighty-four. Ninety-one. A hundred and three. A hundred and twelve.

That was the fourth reading. And I hadn't even asked for it.

Travis came in at nine with my razor and a clean shirt. I handed him the paper.

He read it standing up. Then he sat down.

"She wrote down your heart rate," he said.

"She did."

"Dad, people do that? Strangers?"

"Some do," I said. "The good ones."

He folded the paper carefully and put it in the blue folder, in the front pocket, right behind the cruise confirmation.

They let me go home on Tuesday afternoon, with a paper bag of pills and a list of things I couldn't do for six weeks, which was most things. Travis drove me. He'd taken the week off work without asking me, and he moved into his old bedroom with a duffel bag, and when I told him he didn't have to, he said, "Dad, if your heart goes out again, somebody's got to be here who knows where the folder is."

On Wednesday the ship docked in Cozumel.

At a little after eleven in the morning, my phone rang. It was a strange number, a long one, with a Mexico country code in front.

"Elton!" Charlene. Furious. I could hear wind, and a crowd, and somebody playing a steel drum. "What did you do to my card?"

"Good morning."

"Don't good morning me. I'm standing at an excursion desk in Cozumel with a line of people behind me and my Visa just got declined. Twice. The girl says it's been canceled. It's been canceled, Elton!"

"It's my card," I said. "You were an authorized user. I unauthorized you."

"From a hospital bed?"

"It's a phone call. You can do it lying down."

"Elton, I'm in another country! What am I supposed to do? I've got a snorkeling trip and a lunch and I've got no way to pay for anything!"

"Doesn't the man in my cabin have a credit card?"

There was a silence on the line. A long one. The steel drum kept going in the background, plinking away like nothing was wrong.

"What," she said.

"Kirk Gilliam," I said. "Second passenger, deck nine, balcony. Changed on the eighth, a hundred and fifty dollars, charged to my card. Eighteen days before my heart attack, Charlene. You booked my anniversary on my card and gave my seat to your gym teacher before I ever got sick."

"I don't know what you're…"

"The cruise line told me. The credit card company told me. Lorene told me." I counted them off. "That's three readings. When three readings agree, it's true."

"You called Lorene?"

"Lorene came to the hospital. She brought pie. They wouldn't let me eat it."

"Elton, you listen to me." Her voice dropped, went sweet and fast. "It's not what you think. Kirk's a friend. He was going to come anyway, he had a cabin of his own, and when his fell through, I just… it seemed like a waste. Your ticket. Since you never want to go anywhere."

"You changed it eighteen days ago. I was fine eighteen days ago. I was going."

"You would've backed out! You always back out!"

"I bought the swim trunks, Charlene. They're on the bed right now. They've still got the tag on."

She started to cry then. Not real crying. The crying she uses at the DMV.

"How could you do this to me?" she said. "On my vacation. I'm stranded in Mexico with no money. You're supposed to take care of me."

"You told me you paid for this cruise," I said. "Pay for the snorkeling."

"You're being cruel. You're sick and it's making you cruel."

"Charlene," I said, "you told me to go to the ER and you told me you weren't wasting your trip on my heart. I said go. I meant it. Go. Have a good time. Just don't do it on my card."

I hung up.

Travis was standing in the kitchen doorway. He'd heard all of it.

"Dad," he said. "That's the calmest I ever heard anybody be."

"Doctor's orders," I said. "No stress."

He didn't laugh. He came over and sat down at the kitchen table across from me.

"Are you going to divorce her?"

"That's call six."

"Who's call seven?"

"You'll find out Sunday," I said.

Call number six was to a lawyer named Sondra Pike, whose office is in a little brick building on Main Street in League City, across from the old railroad depot. A man I work with at the plant had used her in 2021 and said she was "nice like a rattlesnake is nice, right up until." She came to my house Thursday morning, because I couldn't drive for a week, and she sat at my kitchen table with her briefcase open and read the blue folder from front to back.

"Mr. Spradlin," she said, when she was done, "I'll be honest with you. Texas is a community property state. That means most of what you two built over thirty-one years gets split. The house, the savings, your 401(k) from the refinery for those years. That's the law, and I can't change it."

"I don't want you to change it."

"Good. Because I can't." She tapped the credit card statement. "But Texas is also one of the few states where a judge is allowed to consider fault. Adultery is a ground. And when one spouse spends community money on an affair, a judge can give the other spouse a bigger share to make up for it. That's called a disproportionate division, which is lawyer for 'she doesn't get half.'"

"How much does she not get?"

"Depends on the judge. Depends on what we can prove." She held up the March confirmation and the page with the hundred-and-fifty-dollar name change fee. "Forty-eight hundred dollars of community money on a cruise for her and her boyfriend. A name change to prove she planned it. Your son as a witness to what she said in the ER. A nurse who wrote down your heart rate when she said it." She smiled for the first time. "And the boyfriend's name on a passenger manifest that a cruise line will produce under subpoena. Mr. Spradlin, I've had cases with less than this go very well."

"Can she come back to the house?"

"It's her house too, until a judge says otherwise. You can't change the locks on her. But you don't have to give her a key to your new bank account either."

"I already didn't."

"I noticed," Sondra said. "I'll have the petition filed by tomorrow afternoon. When does the ship come in?"

"Sunday. Seven in the morning."

"Then she'll be served when she gets home," Sondra said. "Unless you'd rather do something else."

"I'd rather go meet the ship," I said.

She looked at me over her reading glasses.

"Mr. Spradlin, you just had a heart attack."

"I know. I'll sit down for most of it."

Thursday night, a little after nine, the house phone rang. The landline. Nobody calls the landline but robots and Charlene's mother, and Charlene's mother has been dead since 2019.

Travis picked it up in the kitchen before I could get out of the recliner.

"Spradlin residence."

I heard her voice come out of the receiver from across the room. Not words. Just the pitch of it.

"Mom," Travis said. "No. No, he's resting. Because he had a heart attack, Mom. Four days ago. You were there."

He listened. His jaw worked.

"No, I'm not going to put him on. You can talk to me."

He listened some more. Then he put the phone on speaker and set it down on the kitchen counter, and folded his arms, and looked at me like, you need to hear this.

"…a sixty-dollar Wi-Fi package, Travis, I'm paying sixty dollars a day just to talk to my own family, and your father has me stranded out here like some kind of criminal. Kirk had to put the excursion on his card, and then his card got declined at dinner, at the steakhouse, in front of the waiter, because apparently he's carrying a balance, and now we're eating at the buffet like animals…"

"Kirk's card got declined?" Travis said.

"That's not the point!"

"Mom. He's your boyfriend and he can't pay for dinner?"

"He is not my… Travis, you don't understand. Kirk has big plans. He's putting everything he has into opening his own gym. Everything. He's a visionary. He just doesn't have a lot of cash right now."

"So he's using Dad's ticket," Travis said, "and your money."

"Our money! Your father's and mine! Thirty-one years, and I'm not allowed to have one thing for myself?"

"You had Dad," Travis said. "In the ER. Bleeding out of his arm. And you picked a buffet with your gym teacher."

The line went quiet. When she came back, her voice had dropped into the low, mean register she used to use when I was twelve and had tracked mud on the carpet. Travis's carpet now.

"Put your father on the phone."

"No."

"Put him on, Travis, or so help me…"

"Mom," Travis said, "I'm twenty-nine years old. You don't get to so-help-me me anymore. Dad said go. You went. Enjoy the buffet."

He hung up. Then he stood there with his hand on the receiver, breathing hard.

"Son," I said from the recliner.

"I know."

"That was a lot of stress for a man with no stents."

He laughed, finally. It came out wet. He went out to the back porch for a while, and I let him.

When he came back in, he picked up the dry-erase marker and wrote a new number on the refrigerator under the shopping list. "Calls from Mom: 9." Then he drew a little cruise ship next to it, badly, with smoke coming out.

On Friday, the ship was in Roatan. Charlene called eleven more times. I let every one go to voicemail. Travis kept the count going on the refrigerator, the way you'd keep score at a ballgame. Twenty. Then sixteen more by noon. By Saturday afternoon at sea, the number on the fridge said forty-one.

On Saturday night, one more text came in. Not from Charlene. From a number I didn't have.

"Elton, this is Kirk Gilliam. Charlene is very upset. I think we can all be adults about this. Let's talk when we get in. No reason this has to get ugly. K."

I showed it to Travis. He read it and set my phone down on the table very gently, the way you'd put down something that might go off.

"Dad," he said. "How does he have your number?"

"Same way he has my ticket," I said.

Then I made call number seven. I went out on the back porch to do it, and I shut the sliding door, and I didn't tell Travis who it was. It took about four minutes. Most of it was the other person crying.

When I came back in, Travis looked at me.

"Well?"

"Set your alarm for five," I said. "We're going to Galveston."

The cruise terminal in Galveston at six-thirty on a Sunday morning is a big gray building on the harbor, a parking lot the size of a county, and about four hundred people standing behind a metal barricade waiting for somebody they love to come down a gangway. Grandmothers with balloons. Kids with signs. Men in ball caps leaning on the hoods of their trucks, drinking coffee from Buc-ee's.

The ship was already in. It was the biggest thing I'd ever seen float, white as a refrigerator, sixteen decks tall, sitting at the pier with the sun coming up behind it. Up on deck nine, on one of the hundreds of little balconies, my wife was packing.

Travis parked as close as they'd let us and got the folding camp chair out of the bed of his truck, because Dr. Ibarra had said I could go if I sat down, and Travis takes orders literally. Lorene came in her own car. She brought coffee and no pie.

"Who's call seven?" Travis said again, setting up my chair at the end of the barricade where the gangway came down.

"Look for a woman in a blue Astros cap," I said.

At a quarter to seven, a white Camry pulled into the lot. A small woman got out, about fifty, in jeans and a blue Astros cap, with a manila envelope under her arm. She stood by the car for a minute, like she might get back in. Then she walked across the lot to us.

Jeannie Gilliam.

I'd known her fifteen years. She'd checked me in at Dr. Hargrove's dental office twice a year, every year, and asked about Travis, and told me to floss. I'd never once seen her without a smile.

She wasn't smiling now. Her eyes were swollen.

"Elton," she said.

"Jeannie. Thank you for coming."

Travis looked at her. Then at me. Then he got it.

"Oh," he said quietly. "Oh, Dad."

Jeannie sat down on the curb next to my camp chair, because there was nowhere else to sit, and held the envelope in her lap.

"He told me he was at a fitness conference in Dallas," she said. "All week. He called me Tuesday from his 'hotel.' He said the keynote was boring." She laughed, a horrible little laugh. "He called me from a cruise ship and told me the keynote was boring."

"I'm sorry, Jeannie."

"When you called me last night, I didn't believe you. I almost hung up on you." She opened the envelope. "Then I logged into our bank account. Our joint account. Kirk took eleven thousand dollars out of it in July. He told me it was for gym equipment." She held up a printout. "He put it down on a lease. On 646. For a gym. A gym with a pool. Signed by Kirk Gilliam and Charlene Spradlin, as partners."

Lorene, standing behind us, said a word I'd never heard her say.

"Charlene co-signed a commercial lease?" I said.

"Her name's right there." Jeannie pointed. "Partner. Fifty percent. A hundred and sixty-two thousand dollars over five years. And Kirk's name next to it. And our eleven thousand dollars as the deposit."

I sat back in my camp chair.

That was the plan. Not the cruise. The cruise was just the celebration. The plan was the house, sold, and Charlene's half of it going into a gym on 646 with Kirk Gilliam's name on the door.

"Jeannie," I said. "What are you going to do?"

"I already did it," she said. "I filed Friday. And I took my name off our account, and I put his clothes in Hefty bags in the garage." She looked up at the ship. "I just want to see his face."

At seven-forty, they started letting people off.

They come down in a long line, the cruise people, dragging suitcases with their sunburns and their straw hats and the little bags of duty-free rum, and the crowd behind the barricade starts waving and yelling names, and every couple of minutes somebody runs under the rope to hug somebody and a security guard yells at them. Travis stood next to my chair with his arms crossed. Lorene had her phone out. Jeannie stood up off the curb and pulled her Astros cap down low.

It took forty minutes.

Then I saw the pink sundress.

Charlene was coming down the long covered gangway with her rolling suitcase, a straw hat, and big sunglasses. Kirk Gilliam was right behind her with his shaved head, a tank top that said "No Days Off," and a backpack. He had his hand on the small of her back. They were laughing about something.

Halfway down, Charlene looked out over the crowd at the barricade, the way everybody does. Looking for nobody. Just looking.

She saw me. In a camp chair. With our son standing beside me.

She stopped dead. Right there on the gangway, with her hand on the rail. The man behind her walked into her suitcase. The people behind him bunched up. Somebody said, "Lady, keep moving."

Then Kirk looked where she was looking. And he saw the woman in the blue Astros cap.

I've seen a lot of faces in my life. I've watched a man realize a pressure valve he was standing next to was about to let go. Kirk Gilliam's face looked like that.

"Keep moving, folks," a crew member called. "Keep it moving."

They kept moving. There was nowhere else to go. You can't go back up a gangway on a cruise ship. They came down the last thirty feet like two people walking into a courtroom, and they came out through the gap in the barricade, and there we were.

Jeannie walked right past me, straight up to her husband.

"How was Dallas?" she said.

"Jeannie. Baby. Listen…"

"How was the keynote, Kirk?"

"This isn't… I can explain. It's a business trip. Charlene and I are business partners. We were working on the gym…"

"In a balcony cabin," Jeannie said. "Deck nine."

"It's not what it looks like!"

"It looks like you took eleven thousand dollars out of my account in July and signed a lease with her." She held up the printout. Half the crowd at the barricade had turned around to watch by now. A grandmother with a balloon. Two teenagers with their phones up. "Partner, Kirk. Fifty percent. Her name and yours, right under mine on our bank statement."

"That money was going to come back. The gym was going to make all of it back."

"Your clothes are in Hefty bags in the garage," Jeannie said. "The lawyer's name is on the envelope. And the code on the garage door is changed, so call me before you come get them, and I'll have my brother there." She took the manila envelope and pushed it flat against his chest, and he caught it the way you catch something you didn't want. "Have a good trip, Kirk."

She walked away across the parking lot toward her white Camry, and she didn't look back once. A woman in the crowd started clapping. Then two more.

Kirk stood there holding the envelope. Then he turned to Charlene.

"You said he wouldn't fight," he said. "You said Elton never fights for anything. You said the house was as good as sold."

"He doesn't!" Charlene said. "He never has!"

"Then why is he here?"

Charlene looked at me. She took her sunglasses off. Her face was sunburned, and her eyes were puffy, and under the sunburn, she looked about ten years older than she had in the ER a week ago.

"Elton," she said. "Why are you here?"

"Because you told me to go," I said. "So I came."

"You shouldn't be out of bed."

"I'm sitting down. Doctor said that was fine."

She took a step toward me. Travis took a step too, and she stopped.

"Travis, honey…"

"Don't, Mom," he said.

She turned back to me.

"Elton. Please. Let's just go home. Let's go home and sit down at the table and talk about this like grown-ups. I made a mistake. Kirk was a mistake. Look at him. He couldn't even pay for dinner. I don't know what I was thinking. It was the boot camp, and the music, and he made me feel young. You know I love you. You know I'd never really leave you. Thirty-one years, Elton. Please. I'll do anything. I'll go to counseling. I'll go to church. I'll go to cardiac rehab with you. Please don't do this."

Behind her, Kirk Gilliam was already walking away across the parking lot toward the rideshare pickup, pulling his backpack on, with Jeannie's envelope sticking out of his back pocket. He didn't look back either.

Charlene turned to her sister then, like Lorene might save her. Lorene had been standing behind my chair the whole time with her coffee, not saying a word.

"Lorene," Charlene said. "Tell him. Tell him I didn't mean it. You know me."

"I do know you, Charlene," Lorene said. "I've known you fifty-eight years. That's why I came." She set her coffee cup down on the barricade. "You showed me that man's picture at Easter in my kitchen. You told me on the Fourth of July that Elton's heart wasn't in it. And then you stood at the foot of his bed in the emergency room with your suitcase. I've watched you do a lot of selfish things since we were girls. I never once thought I'd watch you do that one."

"You were supposed to be on my side."

"I am on your side," Lorene said. "That's why I'm telling you the truth. Nobody else ever has."

Charlene looked at all three of us. Her husband, her son, her sister. Nobody moved.

I reached into the pocket of my windbreaker and took out the page from the blue folder. The March confirmation. Seven nights, Western Caribbean. Balcony cabin, deck nine. Two guests: Charlene Spradlin and Elton Spradlin. Forty-eight hundred and twelve dollars, charged to my Visa.

I held it out to her. She took it.

"Sondra Pike's office is going to call you this afternoon," I said. "Ask for the papers. That's for your lawyer."

"Elton…"

"You said you paid for this cruise, Charlene. You said you weren't wasting it on my heart." I got up out of the camp chair, slow, the way the doctor told me, and Travis folded it up and tucked it under his arm. "You were right about one thing. You didn't."

I turned around and walked toward Travis's truck, and my son walked beside me at my pace, which was slow, and behind us, my wife stood alone at the cruise terminal barricade with her suitcase and her straw hat and a piece of paper with my name on it.

"Dad," Travis said, when we got to the truck. "Can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

"What would you have done if she'd stayed? At the hospital. If she'd stayed."

I thought about it while he opened the passenger door for me.

"I'd have bought her a new swim suit," I said, "and we'd have gone next year."
