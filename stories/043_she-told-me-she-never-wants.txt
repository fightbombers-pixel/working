"I never want kids. Ever. Not one. Corey knows. Right, babe?"

My wife said it at my sister's gender reveal party, in my sister's backyard, in front of about forty people in folding chairs, about ten seconds after my sister cut into a cake that turned out to be blue inside. Everybody was still cheering. Somebody's aunt had asked us, the way somebody's aunt always does, "So when are you two next?"

And Brittany laughed, flipped her hair, and said it.

Seven years of marriage, and I'd heard her say that at about two hundred parties. Never kids. Ever. And every single time, I'd said the same thing back.

"Right," I said.

People laughed. The aunt said, "Well, you never know!" Brittany said, "Oh, I know," and took a sip of her White Claw.

What made it different that afternoon was the bottle. Two days earlier I'd found a bottle of prenatal vitamins in the glove box of her car, half empty, under the registration, and the receipt was still in the bag. Sixty days' supply. Twenty-two dollars at the Walgreens on Mount Rushmore Road.

I picked up the cake knife off the table, wiped the blue frosting off it with a napkin, and handed it back to my sister Jenna handle first. Then I raised my red Solo cup.

"To Jenna's kid," I said. "The only baby this family's getting."

Everybody cheered again. Jenna gave me a funny look. Brittany clinked my cup and didn't look at me at all.

Two weeks later, my wife would be standing at her parents' fortieth anniversary dinner holding a plastic stick over her head like a winning lottery ticket, and three people in that room would know something she didn't.

What she didn't know, while she drank her White Claw, was what was still on the old phone she'd asked me to wipe. The one sitting in our kitchen junk drawer, next to the takeout menus and the dead batteries.

My name is Corey Ferris. I'm forty-four, and I run the night shift at the water treatment plant in Rapid City, South Dakota. Every four hours, all night long, we pull a sample and test it, because water can look perfectly clear and still make a whole city sick. You don't believe the color. You believe the lab. Remember that.

Across the yard, by the cooler, a man laughed. Loud. A big, easy, look-at-me laugh I'd heard at about five barbecues.

Dusty Dempsey. He sells boats and RVs out on the east side of town, and my sister's husband works for him, which is the only reason he was in that backyard. He was standing by the cooler in a fishing shirt with a beer, and his wife Amber was next to him, holding somebody's baby and making a face at it.

And my wife was looking at him over the top of her White Claw, like a dog looking at a steak through a window.

Two days earlier, on a Thursday afternoon, Brittany had come home from the salon with a brand new phone in a box.

"Can you wipe my old one?" she said. "I'm trading it in at the store Saturday. They give you a hundred and forty bucks."

"Sure."

"Like, all the way. Factory reset. I don't want some kid at the store looking at my pictures."

"I'll do it tonight."

"You're the best." She kissed me on the cheek and went to take a bath.

I didn't do it that night. I was tired. I'd come off a twelve-hour shift and slept five hours. I tossed the old phone in the junk drawer and forgot about it.

On Thursday after supper, before I left for work, I took it out of the drawer to do the reset. I plugged it in. And when the screen came on, it was still logged into her account, and her messages were still syncing to it, from the new phone, live. She'd moved everything over but never signed the old one out. I didn't go looking. I want to be clear about that. I was going to hold down two buttons and erase it. But a message came in while I was holding the phone, and it popped up across the middle of the screen in a gray bubble.

"Did you take the vitamins today?"

From a contact saved as D.

I knew I shouldn't. I'm not going to pretend I didn't know. I tapped it.

There were about three months of them. Mostly D. Some her. I read for maybe four minutes, standing in my own kitchen with my work boots on, and then I sat down on a kitchen chair, because my legs had decided they were done.

From her, in May: "I want your baby. He doesn't deserve one."

From D: "You'll have one. Mine. You deserve the real thing."

From her: "He thinks I don't want kids lol. I just don't want HIS."

From D: "Keep taking the vitamins, Britt. Every day. And stop letting him near you, just in case."

From her: "Don't worry. That's been easy."

At four in the morning, my buddy Pete Navarrete found me sitting in the truck in the plant lot with the engine running. Pete's been on nights with me for nine years. He's forty-seven and has four kids and an old Harley that he spends more on than all four of them.

He knocked on the window. I rolled it down.

"You've been out here forty minutes," he said. "The chlorine log's not going to fill itself out."

"I know."

"What's going on?"

"I think it's her ex. Dusty Dempsey. They dated in high school. She's told me about him."

"The boat guy? With the commercials? 'Come on down to Dempsey's, we'll float you a deal'?"

"That's him."

Email them to yourself. Print them out. Put them in your locker here." A third finger. "And you're going to wait till you're not shaking."

"Okay," I said. "I'm shaking."

And on Saturday, at my sister's party, I said "Right" when my wife said she never wanted kids. And I watched her watch Dusty Dempsey over the top of her drink.

"You told him you want his baby," I said. "You told him I don't deserve one."

I waited for her to cry, or yell, or deny it. I'd seen her do all three, over smaller things. Over a parking ticket once.

She didn't do any of them. She leaned back in her chair, crossed her arms over her robe, and smirked at me. It was a smirk I'd never seen before. I'd been married to her for seven years, and I'd never seen it, and it was like finding out the house you live in has a basement.

"Well," she said, "he has better genes anyway."

I put my fork down.

"Better genes," I said.

"Look at him, Corey. He's six-three. He played college ball. He's got that hair. And look at you." She waved her hand at me, up and down, the whole of me. "You test water for a living. At night. You go bald on top a little more every year. I'm not saying it to be mean. It's just facts. If I'm going to do it, I want to do it right."

"So you were always going to have kids."

"I was always going to have the right kids." She shrugged. "You were just easier to say no to."

"I got a vasectomy for you," I said. "Two years ago. Because you said never. Because you said you'd lie awake at night scared of a slip-up."

"Okay," I said.

"Okay?" She laughed. "That's it? Okay?"

"That's it."

"You're not going to yell? Throw something? Cry?"

"No."

"Wow." She stood up and took her coffee. "You really are boring, Corey. I'm going to go get dressed. And you're going to stay out of my stuff from now on."

She went upstairs. I heard the shower start.

I finished my pancakes. Then I went out to the truck, got the manila folder I'd brought home from my locker, and sat in the cab with my laptop and scanned all fourteen pages with my phone.

Her parents, Hal and Debbie Vickers, live out by Ellsworth Air Force Base, where Hal was a master sergeant for twenty-two years. They go to First Baptist every Sunday and Wednesday. Debbie calls Brittany every morning at seven forty-five on her way to work. Hal has never once said a word to me that wasn't about the weather or the Broncos.

I typed their email address in. Then I opened the Rapid City school district website, found the staff directory for Knollwood Elementary, and found Amber Dempsey, fourth grade, and typed hers in too.

I wrote one line.

"I'm sorry to send this. I think you'd want to know. Corey Ferris."

I attached the fourteen pages, and the photo of the vitamins, and the photo of this morning's message on the lock screen.

My thumb sat on the send button for a long time. Long enough for the shower upstairs to shut off.

I pressed it.

Amber Dempsey didn't call. She emailed back, Monday at six forty-five in the morning, before school. One line.

"Can you meet me at the Colonial House at 3:30 today? I don't want to do this on the phone."

The Colonial House is a family restaurant on Mount Rushmore Road with the best caramel rolls in the Black Hills and booths so old the vinyl has been patched with tape. I got there at three fifteen after four hours of sleep. Amber came in at three thirty-two with a canvas tote bag full of fourth-grade spelling tests and a blue folder, and she sat down across from me and didn't take her jacket off.

"I'm sorry."

"Everybody keeps saying that." She almost smiled. "My sister said it. My principal said it, and I didn't even tell her anything, I just walked in this morning and she looked at me and said it."

She opened the blue folder and turned it around on the table.

"He knows this," I said.

"He sat in the room. He held my hand. He cried in the car." She took the letter back and put it in the folder very neatly. "And then he made me promise never to tell anybody. Not his mom. Not his brother. Nobody. He said it would kill him if people knew. So for three years I've let everybody think it was me. His mother asks me every Christmas if I've thought about adoption. Like I'm the broken one."

"Because she's the only woman in Rapid City who thinks he can." Amber wiped her eyes with a paper napkin. "Do you know what that does for a man like Dusty? To have somebody look at him like that? Better genes. Six-three. College ball." She laughed, and it hurt to hear. "He played two years at Black Hills State and got cut. He sells pontoon boats."

"So what happens when she doesn't get pregnant?"

"She'll think it's her fault. That's what he does. He did it to me." Amber closed her folder. "Or she'll think it's yours."

"I had a vasectomy two years ago."

Amber stared at me. Then she put both hands over her face and laughed into them until the man in the next booth turned around.

She'd been in a good mood all week. She thought I'd let it go. She thought "okay" meant okay. She'd hummed in the shower. She'd texted somebody at dinner Monday and smiled at her phone, and Tuesday she'd come home from the salon at nine and said she'd had a late color. I didn't say anything.

Debbie opened the door before we knocked. She didn't hug Brittany. She always hugs Brittany.

"Come in," she said. "Sit down. Supper can wait."

"I'll drive you home," she said. "Then I'm going to go find my husband."

"That who I think it is?" Pete said.

"That's him."

The truck stopped about twenty feet away with the high beams on us. The door opened, and Dusty Dempsey got out, and he was holding an aluminum baseball bat down by his leg.

He was bigger than me. That part was true. Six-three, wide shoulders, a fishing shirt untucked over a gut that college ball hadn't left him. His eyes looked wrong in the headlights, too shiny. I could smell beer from fifteen feet away.

"Ferris," he said.

"Dusty."

"You sent it to my wife."

"I did."

"She threw me out. She changed the garage code. She told her whole family. Do you know who her brother is? Her brother's the sergeant at Pennington County. Do you know what that's like? I've been carrying forty-pound chlorine cylinders since I was twenty-two. I'm not big, but I'm not slow. Pete knelt on his back. Dusty was breathing hard and swearing into the pavement.

Twelve days later, on a Saturday night, Hal and Debbie Vickers had their fortieth anniversary dinner in the fellowship hall at First Baptist.

Amber stood up.

"Debbie did." Amber took the blue folder out of her purse. "Dusty, I kept your secret for three years. I let your mother think I was the broken one. I'm not keeping it anymore. Not when you let her stand up in front of her parents' church and say this."

"Amber, don't." Dusty let go of Brittany's hand. His salesman smile was gone. "Amber. Not here."

"Black Hills Reproductive Medicine," Amber said, and opened the folder. "October 2021. Dusty Dempsey. Sperm count, zero. Not low. Zero. From the mumps, when he was nine." She held the page up. "That's why we did three rounds of IVF with a donor. That's why we spent forty-one thousand dollars. Dusty can't have children. He's known since he was thirty-six."

The whole fellowship hall was so quiet you could hear the coffee urn ticking in the kitchen.

Brittany turned to Dusty. "That's not true."

Dusty didn't say anything. He was looking at the floor.

"Dusty. Tell them that's not true."

"It's true," Dusty said, very low.

"Then..." She looked at the phone in her hand. She looked at me. "Then it's Corey's. It has to be. We were still... it was before... Corey, tell them."

"I had a vasectomy in December of 2022," I said. I didn't stand up. I didn't raise my voice. "You drove me to the appointment. You held the ice pack. You said it was the best Christmas present I ever gave you."

Somebody at Hal's buddies' table let out a long, low whistle.

Brittany's face had gone blotchy red, then pale, then red again. She held the phone up higher, like holding it higher would make it true.

"The test is real! I took it Tuesday! It's real!"

And Debbie Vickers, who had not said one word all night, reached into her own purse, took out her reading glasses and her own phone, and scrolled for a second. Then she held her phone up next to her daughter's.

"Amy from your salon posted this Tuesday morning," Debbie said. "On Facebook. 'Baby Hernandez coming this fall!' Same test. Same pink bathroom counter. Same chip in the corner of the sink. Same purple nail polish on the thumb. Amy does her nails purple, Brittany. You do yours red." She set the phone down on the tablecloth next to the ruby hearts. "You stole her picture. You stole a picture of somebody else's baby and stood up at my anniversary and called it yours."

Brittany opened her mouth. Nothing came out.

Dusty stepped back from her. One step, then two. He looked at her the way he must have looked at the zero on that lab report three years ago.

"You told me you were pregnant," he said. "You told me Tuesday. You said I had to leave Amber for good. You said it was mine."

"I was going to be! I would have been! I took the vitamins every day!"

"With who?" Dusty said. "With who, Britt?"

He turned around and walked out of the fellowship hall, past the buffet and the cake with the two little plastic people on it, and the doors swung shut behind him.

Brittany stood alone at the head table with her phone in her hand. She looked at her mother. Her mother looked back. She looked at her father.

Then she looked at me. All the way across the hall, past the round tables and the ruby hearts, to the table in the back where I was sitting with my arm in a sling next to Pete and his wife and a plate of ham I hadn't touched.

"Corey," she said. Her voice had gone small. "Corey, say something. Tell them I'm not... tell them. You always know what to say to Daddy. Please."

Forty people turned around in their folding chairs to look at me.

I thought about the pancakes. About the smirk. About better genes, and the bottle of vitamins on the kitchen table next to the syrup, and seven years of saying "right" at parties.

"Okay," I said.

That was all. She waited for more. There wasn't any. After a few seconds she understood that there wasn't going to be, and her face folded up, and she sat down hard in her chair at the head table and put her phone face down on the tablecloth.

Hal Vickers stood up slowly. He's sixty-four and he has a bad hip from twenty-two years of climbing on bombers. He picked up his glass of sweet tea and looked at the forty people in that room, his church friends and his crew chief and his wife's sisters and the man at the back with his arm in a sling.

"Forty years," Hal said. "I want to thank everybody for coming. And I want to say one thing about my daughter, since she brought it up." He turned to her. "Brittany, you never wanted kids. You wanted somebody to clap."

He raised his glass to Debbie.

"Happy anniversary, Deb," he said. "Let's cut the cake."
