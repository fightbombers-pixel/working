"Don't worry. The idiot is so happy about the pregnancy, he'll raise your son thinking it's his."

My wife said it in German, across a white tablecloth at Ludwig's Chop House on Water Street in Peoria, to her boss, with her hand resting on her stomach and a smile on her face that was meant for me. There were three of us at the table and maybe forty people in the room, and not one of them understood a word of it except the man she said it to.

And me.

For the last hour I'd smiled like a fool while the two of them talked across me in German about shipping schedules and a man from headquarters named Keller. I'd nodded and buttered my bread and asked Stefan, in slow loud English, whether they had Applebee's in Germany. He'd laughed and said, "Not yet, thank God." Valerie had patted my hand.

She was fourteen weeks along. We'd tried for a baby for nine years. I had cried in the parking lot of the OB's office six weeks earlier, and she had held my face in both hands and said, "Dennis, we did it."

I picked up the bottle of Riesling, the one Stefan had ordered because it was from his hometown, and I filled his glass first, then hers, then mine. I didn't spill a drop. Then I set the bottle down, looked her boss right in the eye, and said, in German, clean and slow so he wouldn't miss a word:

"The idiot spent four years in the Army at Grafenwöhr, Stefan. His grandmother came over from Bavaria. And the idiot would like to know whether your wife back in Stuttgart is also very happy about the pregnancy."

Stefan Vogel's fork hit his plate so hard a waiter turned around.

Twenty minutes later, he was standing in the Ludwig's parking lot with his phone to his ear, talking fast in German to somebody who kept hanging up on him.

What my wife didn't know was what was folded inside the owner's manual in my truck's glovebox. One sheet of paper from the urology clinic at OSF Saint Francis. Dated April third.

My name is Dennis Salinas. I'm fifty-two, I own Salinas Plumbing, three trucks and a shop on the south side of Peoria, Illinois, and I've been married to Valerie for sixteen years. In my trade you learn that water always finds the crack. You can paint over a leak, you can hang a picture over it, but the water's still running behind the wall, and sooner or later it comes through the ceiling. Remember that.

Valerie was staring at me like I'd grown a second head.

"You don't speak German," she said, in English.

"I do," I said, in English. "I just never had much reason to."

"Since when?"

"Since 1991. You've seen the pictures. Me in the tank in the snow."

"You said that was Kentucky."

"I said it was cold. You said Kentucky."

Stefan had his napkin pressed to his mouth. He's forty-six, tall and thin, with expensive glasses and the kind of haircut that costs sixty dollars and looks like it cost six. He's the plant director at Rheinhold Hydraulics out on the Pioneer Parkway, the German company that bought the old pump factory four years ago, and Valerie has been his executive assistant for two of those years.

"Mr. Salinas," he said in his careful English. "I think there is a misunderstanding."

"Is there?" I said, in German. "Then translate it for me. What part did I misunderstand? The idiot part, or the son part?"

He didn't answer. His face had gone the color of old dishwater.

Valerie looked from him to me and back. "What did you just say to him?"

"I asked him to explain what you said."

"It was a joke," she said. "It's a work joke. You wouldn't get it."

"I got it fine, Val. Word for word."

The waiter came over, a young guy with a little notepad. "Can I get you folks anything else? Dessert?"

"Just the check," I said, and I took out my wallet and laid my card on the tablecloth. "All of it. Mine, hers, and his."

"Dennis, you don't have to—" Valerie started.

"I'm the idiot who pays," I said. "Isn't that the plan?"

I signed the slip. I added twenty percent for the waiter, because none of this was his fault. Then I stood up, put on my jacket, and walked out through the dining room past forty people eating steak, and I didn't look back.

In the parking lot I got into my truck and opened the glovebox. The owner's manual was right where I'd left it. Inside the cover was the letter from the urology clinic.

I didn't need to read it again. I'd read it maybe a hundred times since April.

Here's the part I'd never told my wife.

Last winter, after nine years of trying, our family doctor sent me for a test, just to rule things out. They did the test in March. The urologist called me into his office in April, a quiet older man named Dr. Pruett, and he told me that the case of mumps I'd had at thirty-four, the one that put me in bed for two weeks and that I'd mostly forgotten about, had done permanent damage.

"Mr. Salinas," he said, "I'm sorry. Your count is zero. Not low. Zero. Without surgery and a lab, you won't be fathering a child."

I'd sat in my truck in that parking lot for an hour. I'd planned to tell Valerie that weekend. I'd even practiced it. And then, before I could find the words, on a Saturday morning in late May, she came out of the bathroom crying and holding a stick with two pink lines on it.

"Dennis," she'd said. "We did it."

I had stood there in the hallway in my socks and felt the floor go out from under me. Because I knew. I knew right then. And I put my arms around her, and I said, "We did," and I let her think I was crying from joy.

For six weeks, I'd smiled. I'd gone to the ultrasound. I'd painted the spare room a pale yellow because we didn't know if it was a boy or a girl yet. I'd waited for her to tell me the truth.

Tonight she'd told it. Just not to me.

Through the windshield I could see Stefan come out of Ludwig's, phone to his ear, walking in little circles by his rented Audi, saying "Anna, bitte, Anna, hör mir zu," which means "Anna, please, Anna, listen to me." Then he looked at the phone and swore and dialed again.

Valerie came out behind him. She didn't go to him. She came to my truck and pulled on the passenger door handle. It was locked.

I rolled the window down two inches.

"Let me in, Dennis."

"Ride home with Stefan."

"He's not driving me home. He's on the phone with his wife!"

"Sounds like he's trying to be."

"Dennis, please. We need to talk about this at home. Not here."

I looked at her. Sixteen years. The yellow room. Her hand on her stomach.

I unlocked the door.

She got in. And for the whole twenty-minute drive home, neither of us said one word. She kept looking at me sideways like she was trying to figure out who she was sitting next to.

When we pulled into the driveway, I reached across her, opened the glovebox, and took out the owner's manual.

"What's that?" she said.

"That," I said, "is why I'm not the idiot."

We sat at the kitchen table, under the light with the one burned-out bulb that I kept saying I'd fix. I took the letter out of the manual, unfolded it, and slid it across the table to her.

She read it. I watched her read it twice.

"April," she said.

"April third."

"You've known since April."

"I've known since April that I can't have kids. Then in May, you came out of the bathroom with a stick."

"Dennis—"

"I was going to tell you that weekend. I had it all planned. And then you said, 'We did it.'" I folded my hands on the table. "So I waited. I figured you'd tell me eventually. Six weeks, Val. I painted the room."

She pushed the letter back toward me like it was hot. "Labs make mistakes."

"Dr. Pruett did it twice. Zero both times."

"It happens. Miracles happen. I read about a man in Ohio—"

"Then you won't mind a test."

She stopped.

"There's a blood test," I said. "They can do it now, while you're pregnant. A little blood from you, a cheek swab from me. A week, ten days. If it's mine, I will get down on my knees in this kitchen and apologize for the rest of my life."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll know what the answer is, won't I?"

She stood up. She went to the sink and ran the water for no reason, and stood there with her back to me.

"You said it in German," I said. "You said it to him because you thought I'd never understand. You called me an idiot to another man's face while I was pouring your wine. And you can stand there and tell me labs make mistakes."

"He was upset," she said to the sink. "He was worried. I was just calming him down."

"Calming him down about what?"

She didn't answer.

"About whose son it is?"

She turned the water off.

"I want you to leave," she said quietly. "Tonight. I can't look at you."

"That's funny," I said. "I've been looking at you for six weeks."

I packed a bag. There's a small apartment above the shop on South Adams, with a bed and a shower and a coffeemaker, where I'd slept during the big ice storm in 2019 when every pipe in Peoria froze at once. It smelled like PVC cement and old coffee. It was fine.

I was lying on that bed a little after midnight, staring at the ceiling, when my phone buzzed with a text from a number I didn't recognize.

"Mr. Salinas. This is Stefan Vogel. Valerie gave me your number. We should talk. Man to man. Tomorrow, after work, at your business. Please do not contact my wife."

I read it three times. Then I typed back, in German.

"Six o'clock. Bring your checkbook. You'll want to offer me money. Everybody in your position does."

The next morning, before I went to a single job, I went to see a lawyer.

Ruth Calderon has an office over a bakery on Main Street, and the whole place smells like cinnamon rolls, which I think is a tactic. She's about my age, short, with gray curls and a voice like a drill sergeant who also teaches Sunday school. My buddy Rico's mother used her for her divorce and said she was the only lawyer in Peoria who ever made her cry from laughing.

I told her everything. The dinner. The German. The letter. The test.

When I was done, she sat back and tapped her pen on her teeth.

"Okay," she said. "Here's the thing nobody tells you. In Illinois, if a baby's born while you're married, the law presumes you're the father. Automatically. Your name goes on the birth certificate, and you're on the hook for support for eighteen years, unless somebody does something about it."

"So I do something about it."

"You do something about it. You file. You ask the court for a DNA test after the baby's born, or you get her to agree to one now." She held up a finger. "She has to agree to the prenatal one. It's her blood. Nobody can make her give it."

"She said no."

"Women say no on Monday and yes on Friday when their lawyer tells them a judge is going to order it anyway." Ruth wrote something down. "Now. The boss."

"Stefan Vogel. Plant director."

"And she's his executive assistant. Reports directly to him."

"Two years."

Ruth smiled the kind of smile I imagine sharks practice. "Most companies, especially the big foreign ones, have a policy. If a supervisor gets involved with somebody who reports to him, he has to disclose it to HR. Right away. If he doesn't, and they find out, it's usually the end of his job. Not hers. His."

"Should I report it?"

"You don't have to do anything," she said. "That's the beauty of it. Just don't sign anything, don't take any money, and don't record anybody. This is Illinois. Everybody in the conversation has to agree to be recorded, or you're the one committing a felony."

"He's coming to my shop tonight. To talk."

Ruth's eyebrows went up. "He's going to offer you money."

"That's what I told him."

"Don't take it. Don't hit him. And for God's sake, Dennis, make sure your shop cameras are working. Video's fine. It's the sound that gets you in trouble."

At five minutes to six, my apprentice Rico Alvarado was sweeping the shop floor when the rented Audi pulled into the lot. Rico is twenty-four, built like a refrigerator, and has the reflexes of a cat who drinks too much coffee. I'd hired him out of the trade school three years earlier, and he'd turned out to be the best I'd ever trained.

"That him?" Rico said, leaning on his broom.

"That's him."

"Want me to stay?"

"Stay inside. Watch the monitor. If anything happens, call 911."

"If anything happens," Rico said, "I'm coming out there."

"Stay inside, Rico."

He sighed like I'd taken away his birthday.

I walked out into the lot. The October light was going orange over the grain elevators by the river. Stefan got out of the Audi in a dark overcoat, holding a leather folder.

"Mr. Salinas."

"Mr. Vogel."

"Shall we speak in English or German?"

"English," I said. "You don't get to hide anymore."

He took a breath. "I want to be practical. Valerie is very upset. You are very upset. I am also very upset. But nobody needs to be destroyed by this."

"Somebody already was. Last night. In the middle of Ludwig's."

"What she said was cruel. I agree. I told her so." He opened the folder. Inside was a cashier's check. I could see the number from where I was standing. Fifty thousand dollars. "This is for you. For your trouble. And for your understanding."

"My understanding of what?"

"That you raise the child," Stefan said, like he was explaining a shipping schedule. "As yours. As you planned. Valerie stays with you. You stay married. Everyone is happy. I return to Germany in time, and this is all forgotten."

"In time," I said.

"In time."

"And your wife?"

His mouth tightened. "My wife does not need to know about any of this."

"She sounded like she already did. Last night. In the parking lot. You kept saying 'Anna, bitte.'"

"That was a different matter."

"That was the same matter, Stefan."

He held the check out. "Fifty thousand dollars, Mr. Salinas. You are a plumber. It is a great deal of money."

"I've got three trucks and a shop and twelve years left on my mortgage," I said. "I fix what's leaking for a living. I don't paint over it." I didn't take the check. "Put it away. Go home. And tell your wife the truth before somebody else does."

He didn't put it away. His face changed. The careful, polite, plant-director face slid off like a cheap decal.

"You think you are clever," he said. "Because you speak a little German. You are a man who fixes toilets. Valerie told me. Sixteen years she was bored to death. Sixteen years, you came home smelling like a sewer and fell asleep in a chair."

"She tell you that in German too?"

"She did not need to. I could see it." He stepped closer. "This child will have a real father's blood. Not some soldier who could not even—"

"Careful," I said.

He shoved me. Both hands, flat, right in the chest, hard enough that I went back a step and my heel hit the curb of the loading dock.

I didn't fall. I came back up off that curb, and when he reached for me a second time I caught his wrist and turned it the way a drill sergeant at Fort Knox showed me thirty years ago, and Stefan Vogel went down on the gravel on his hip, with his expensive glasses skittering across the lot and the cashier's check fluttering under the Audi.

I let go of his wrist and stepped back with my hands open.

"That's the only one you get," I said.

The shop door banged open behind me. Rico came out with the broom held like a baseball bat.

"You good, boss?"

"I'm good."

"I called 911 anyway."

"Of course you did."

Stefan got up on one knee, groping for his glasses. His hip was scraped through his good slacks, and he was breathing hard. He found the glasses, put them on crooked, and looked up at me with pure hate.

"You attacked me," he said. "You attacked me. I will tell the police. I will tell them you threatened me and attacked me, and Valerie will tell them you are violent, and you will lose everything, you stupid—"

The sirens were already coming down South Adams.

"Go ahead and tell them," I said. "Then smile for the camera."

I pointed up at the corner of the shop. The little black dome above the loading dock. It's been there since 2016, when somebody stole forty feet of copper pipe off my truck.

Stefan looked at it. Then he looked at me. And for the first time since I'd met him, he didn't have anything to say in any language.

The responding officer was a woman named Sergeant Holly Breen, maybe forty, who'd grown up on the south side and whose dad used to drink coffee in my waiting room. She listened to Stefan first, because he was talking loudest. Then she listened to me. Then she asked Rico to pull up the camera.

We all stood in my little office around the monitor. Rico ran it back. There was no sound, just the picture, clear as day in the orange light. Stefan walking up. The folder. The check. Me standing still with my hands at my sides. Stefan stepping in. Stefan's two hands hitting my chest. Me going back. Stefan reaching again. Me catching his wrist. Stefan going down. Me stepping back with my hands up.

Sergeant Breen watched it twice.

"Mr. Vogel," she said. "You want to tell me again who attacked who?"

Stefan straightened his overcoat. "I was provoked."

"By what? He's standing still."

"By what he said."

"What'd he say?"

Stefan opened his mouth and shut it.

"That's what I thought," Sergeant Breen said. She turned to me. "You want to press charges, Mr. Salinas? It's battery. He put hands on you first."

I thought about it. "I want it on paper. A report. With his name and mine on it."

"You'll have it." She looked at Stefan. "You'll get a copy of it in the mail, too. At whatever address you give me."

He gave her the address of the company's rental house in the Grand View Drive neighborhood. I remember thinking at the time that it didn't matter.

It turned out it mattered more than anything.

Valerie didn't take long to make her move.

Two days later, Saturday, my sister-in-law called me. Valerie's older sister, Pam, a dental hygienist in Pekin who has never liked me much and has never hidden it.

"Dennis," she said, "I need to hear you say it. Did you hit that man?"

"He shoved me. I put him on the ground. It's on camera. There's a police report."

"Valerie says you jumped him. She says you threatened him, and you threatened her, and she's scared to be in the house."

"I haven't been in the house since Thursday, Pam."

"She says you've been driving by."

"I've been driving to jobs. Half of Peoria's on the way to my house." I took a breath. "Pam, did she tell you why I left?"

Silence.

"Did she tell you what she said at Ludwig's?"

"She said you overheard a work conversation and got paranoid."

"She told her boss, in German, that I'm an idiot who'll raise his son thinking it's mine."

The silence went on so long I checked to see if the call had dropped.

"His son," Pam said finally.

"His son."

"Dennis, you can't—I mean, you two have been trying for—"

"Nine years. I know."

"Oh my God." Her voice changed. "Oh my God, she told Mom it was a miracle."

"It's a miracle, all right. Just not mine."

That afternoon, Ruth Calderon called. "Her lawyer just contacted me. Valerie's agreeing to the prenatal paternity test."

I sat down on the edge of my desk. "Why? Why would she agree?"

"I asked her lawyer that. Off the record, he said she's convinced it might be yours. She says the timing works both ways." Ruth paused. "Dennis. Does the timing work both ways?"

I thought about the spring. A few Sunday mornings. A night in April, after the doctor's letter, when I'd held her and not said anything.

"Yes," I said. "It could."

"Then she's hoping," Ruth said. "And you've got a lab report that says it can't be. Results in ten days."

"Ten days."

"Ten days. Don't do anything stupid in ten days."

I didn't do anything stupid. I didn't do anything at all. I went to work. I fixed a water heater in Bartonville and a sewer line in East Peoria, and I let Valerie's calls go to voicemail. Twelve the first day. Nineteen the second.

On the fifth day, a woman walked into my shop.

She was about forty-five, small and very straight, in a camel coat, with blond hair pulled back hard and a face that had been crying and was done with it. She was holding an envelope with the Peoria Police Department's seal on the corner.

"Mr. Dennis Salinas?" she said. Her accent was soft, careful.

"That's me."

"My name is Anna Vogel," she said. "I flew in from Stuttgart last night. On Thursday my husband called me to say I should not come to the company Oktoberfest after all, that he was too busy. I hung up on him four times. Then I bought a ticket. This was in the mailbox at the rental house this morning. Your name is on it. And your address."

She laid the police report on my counter.

"I read my husband's mail," she said. "For eighteen years, I never once read his mail. Today I did." She looked up at me. "I want to know what happened in your parking lot. And then I want to show you something my husband has not told your wife."

I locked the shop door and turned the sign to CLOSED, and Rico made her a cup of the terrible coffee from our machine, which she drank without complaining, which told me a lot about her.

I told her everything. I didn't make it softer. She'd come four thousand miles for the truth, and I figured she'd earned all of it. I told her about Ludwig's and the German at the table, and the letter from Dr. Pruett, and the fifty thousand dollars fluttering under the Audi.

When I got to "he'll raise your son thinking it's his," she closed her eyes.

"He has wanted a son for eighteen years," she said quietly. "We could not. We tried everything. Doctors in Munich, in Zurich. He said it did not matter to him." She opened her eyes. "He said it did not matter."

"I'm sorry, Mrs. Vogel."

"Anna." She set the coffee down. "And now I will show you what he has not shown Valerie."

She took a folded sheet of paper from her coat. It was on the letterhead of Rheinhold headquarters in Stuttgart, in German, and I read it slowly, the way you read a contract.

It was Stefan's transfer. Approved. Effective the first of January. Plant director in Peoria to vice president of operations in Stuttgart. A promotion. Behind it was a printed email from Stefan to the head of human resources in Germany, three weeks old. Anna had printed it from the family computer.

I read the email twice. Then I read the line that mattered out loud, translating as I went.

"Rumors regarding my assistant are unfounded. Mrs. Salinas is a married woman with emotional difficulties who has misread my professional kindness. There is no relationship to disclose."

"Emotional difficulties," Rico said from the doorway. "Man."

"He is going home in January," Anna said. "He told me last month we would buy a house near my mother. He told me the American assistant was a little crazy and he would be glad to be rid of her." She looked at me. "Your wife thinks he is leaving me for her."

"She thinks he's staying."

"He is not staying. He was never staying. He is leaving her with the baby and with you, and going home to be promoted." Her voice cracked for the first time. "And to me. He was going to come home to me and never tell me."

"Water finds the crack," I said.

"What?"

"Something we say in my trade. You can hide a leak for a while. Not forever."

She almost smiled. "In German we say, lies have short legs."

"I like yours better."

She picked up the transfer letter and the email and handed them both to me.

"Keep these," Anna said. "Saturday is the Oktoberfest. Three hundred people from the plant and their families, and Thomas Keller, the man from headquarters who signed this transfer. Stefan will give a speech." She buttoned her coat. "I think you should both be there. You and your wife."

"Why both?"

"Because I want to see his face," Anna said, "when the crazy secretary and the stupid wife are standing in the same tent."

The paternity results came in on the twelfth day, a Monday, at Ruth Calderon's office over the bakery. Valerie's lawyer was there, a soft-spoken young man named Gavin Reyes who kept straightening his tie. Valerie was there too, in a long cardigan, with one hand on her stomach. She didn't look at me when I sat down.

Ruth opened the envelope. She read it. She didn't make a face. She just slid it across the table to Gavin Reyes, and he read it, and then, very gently, he slid it to Valerie.

"Probability of paternity," Ruth said, because somebody had to say it out loud. "Zero percent."

Valerie stared at the page.

"It's wrong," she said.

"It's not wrong, Val."

"It could have been yours. The timing—"

"The timing worked," I said. "I didn't."

She put both hands over her face. Gavin Reyes looked at the ceiling. Ruth looked at me, and I gave her a little nod, and she stood up and took Gavin out into the hallway, toward the smell of the cinnamon rolls.

When it was just the two of us, Valerie lowered her hands.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Fine. It's Stefan's. You knew it. You win."

"Nobody won anything."

"He's going to take care of us," she said, wiping her face with the sleeve of her cardigan. "Me and the baby. He's leaving his wife. He told me. He's staying in Peoria, and we'll get a place out in Dunlap, and—"

"Val."

"—and you'll get the house, you can have the house, I don't even want it—"

"Valerie."

She stopped.

I took the transfer letter out of my jacket and laid it on the table in front of her. Then the email. I didn't say anything. I just let her read.

She can't read German. So she looked up at me. And I translated, line by line, slow, the way I'd done it for Anna. The first of January. Vice president of operations. Stuttgart. And then the email. Mrs. Salinas is a married woman with emotional difficulties who has misread my professional kindness.

Her lips moved on the words. Emotional difficulties.

"Where did you get this?"

"From his wife. Anna. She's in Peoria. She flew in last week."

"His wife is here?"

"She read the police report from my parking lot. It came to their rental house." I tapped the transfer letter. "He's going home, Val. He's been going home the whole time. He told his headquarters you're crazy. He told his wife you're crazy. And he told me, in my own parking lot, that he'd go back to Germany 'in time,' and you'd stay with me and I'd raise his son."

"He said that?"

"With a fifty-thousand-dollar check in his hand."

She pulled out her phone. Her hands were shaking. She dialed, and she put it on speaker and set it on the table between us, I think because she wanted me to hear it. Maybe she wanted a witness. Maybe she wanted me to hear him say it was all a lie.

It rang four times.

"Valerie." Stefan's voice, low, irritated. "I told you not to call this number in the evening."

"Is it true you're going back to Germany in January?"

A pause. Just a half-second too long.

"Who told you this?"

"Is it true?"

"Valerie, we will talk on Saturday. After the Oktoberfest. I will explain everything."

"Did you tell your company I have emotional difficulties?"

Another pause. Longer.

"Where are you? Who is with you?"

"Answer me, Stefan."

"I will fix this on Saturday," he said, and his voice had gone flat and hard. "I will make an announcement. Everything will be clear. Do not come to the tent. Do you understand? Do not come."

The line went dead.

Valerie looked at the phone for a long time.

"He's going to tell them I'm crazy," she said. "In front of everybody. That's his announcement."

"Probably."

"What happens to us?" she said. "To the baby? Are you just going to walk away from him?"

"Ruth filed this morning," I said. "Two things. The divorce. And a petition so that when he's born, my name doesn't go on his birth certificate. His father's does."

"You'd do that to him? Leave him with nothing?"

"I'm not leaving him with nothing, Val. I'm leaving him with the truth. That's more than either of us got." I put my hands flat on the table. "You'll get half of what we built, because that's the law and because it's fair. The house, the savings, all of it, right down the middle. What you won't get is me, standing at a crib for eighteen years pretending I didn't hear you at Ludwig's."

She looked at the zero on the page again. Then back at the phone.

"He's going to stand up in front of three hundred people and Thomas Keller and make me the crazy secretary." She laughed, a horrible broken little sound. "And you're going to let him."

I stood up and put the transfer letter back in my jacket.

"No," I said. "I'm going to be there. So is his wife. What you do is up to you."

The Rheinhold Hydraulics Oktoberfest was in a giant white tent in the plant's back lot off Pioneer Parkway, with long tables and a brass band from the German-American club in Peoria Heights playing polkas. Three hundred people, maybe more. Kids running around with pretzels as big as their heads. Beer in tall glass mugs that you had to give back.

I wore a clean flannel and my good jeans. Rico came with me because he said there was no way he was missing this, and because, he said, somebody had to be there to hold my beer.

Anna Vogel met us at the entrance in a dark green dress. She looked calm in a way that scared me a little.

"He does not know I am here," she said. "He thinks I am at the rental house with a migraine."

"Where's Keller?"

She nodded toward the front table. A tall gray-haired man in a tweed jacket, laughing with the plant's managers. "Thomas Keller. He speaks perfect English. He went to university in Michigan."

"Good," I said. "That'll save time."

Rico handed me a mug of beer from the line and leaned in close.

"Boss, real talk. If this goes bad, what's the plan?"

"There's no plan, Rico. I'm just going to stand here and understand German."

"That's the worst plan I ever heard."

"It's worked twice so far."

He thought about that, took a long pull of his own beer, and said, "Okay. Fair."

Stefan got up on the little stage at half past seven. He tapped the microphone and the band stopped. He was smooth again, the glasses polished, the hip apparently healed. He thanked everybody for coming. He thanked headquarters. He thanked Thomas Keller by name. Then he said, "I also want to address some unpleasant rumors."

I saw Valerie then. She was standing near the back of the tent, by the beer line, in her long cardigan, with one hand on her stomach. She'd come anyway.

"There are those who have spread stories about me and a member of my staff," Stefan said. "A woman who, I am sad to say, has been struggling. Personally. Emotionally. I have tried to be kind to her. Perhaps too kind." He looked down at his notes with a sad, wise face. "I have asked that she be given some leave, for her own good."

People were murmuring now. Some of them were looking at Valerie.

Then he leaned away from the microphone, toward Thomas Keller at the front table, and said, quietly, in German, the way you'd mutter to a friend:

"The hysterical cow is here. Get security to walk her out before she makes a scene."

He thought nobody in that tent but Keller understood him.

I put my beer down on the table, and I said, loud, in English, so the whole tent could hear it:

"He just told Mr. Keller, in German, that the hysterical cow is here, and to get security to walk her out before she makes a scene."

Three hundred heads turned toward me.

Stefan froze on the stage. Thomas Keller turned around slowly in his chair.

"Who is this?" Stefan said into the microphone. "Security, this man is—"

"I'm the idiot," I said, walking up the aisle between the tables. "The one who's supposed to raise your son thinking it's his. That's what Valerie told you at Ludwig's two weeks ago, in German, while I was pouring your Riesling. You remember. You dropped your fork."

The tent went dead silent. Even the kids stopped.

"This man is unstable," Stefan said. "There is a police report, he attacked me—"

"There's a police report," I said. "It says you attacked me. On camera. With a fifty-thousand-dollar check in your other hand." I stopped at the front table and laid the transfer letter and the email down in front of Thomas Keller. "And there's this. Your transfer to Stuttgart, effective the first of January. And this. Your email to headquarters saying there's no relationship with your assistant to disclose."

Keller picked up the email. He read it. Then he looked up at the stage.

"Stefan," he said, in perfect, flat Midwestern English. "Is Mrs. Salinas pregnant with your child?"

"Thomas, this is not the place—"

"I asked you a question."

"It's his," Valerie said.

Everyone turned. She'd come up the aisle behind me. Her face was wet, but her voice was steady.

"It's his," she said again, louder, pointing at the stage. "The DNA test says my husband's not the father. Stefan is. He's been sleeping with me for a year and a half. He told me he was leaving his wife. He told headquarters I was crazy." She took a breath. "I'm not crazy. I'm stupid. There's a difference."

Stefan grabbed the microphone stand. "She's lying, she is a liar, she—"

"Stefan."

That was Anna. She walked out from behind the beer line and up the aisle in her green dress, past me, past Valerie, all the way to the edge of the stage. She looked up at her husband. Then she took off her wedding ring and set it on the stage at his feet, right next to his polished shoes.

"Lies have short legs," she said in English. Then she said something in German, very quietly, that I'm not going to translate, because some things belong to the people they were said to.

Stefan stood there on the stage looking down at the ring.

Thomas Keller stood up. He buttoned his tweed jacket.

"Stefan," he said, loud enough for every table to hear. "Our conduct policy requires a supervisor to disclose any relationship with a direct report. You were asked directly. You lied in writing to headquarters." He picked up the transfer letter, folded it once, and tore it in half. "The transfer is rescinded. You are suspended, effective now, pending termination. Security will walk you to your car."

Two security guards in yellow jackets came up the steps. Stefan didn't fight them. He looked out over the tent, over three hundred people holding their giant pretzels and their glass mugs, and his eyes found me.

"You," he said. "Of all people. A plumber."

I picked my beer back up off the table and raised it to him, and I said it in German, slow and clean, so he wouldn't miss a word.

"Water always finds the crack, Stefan. Even in Stuttgart."
