The clerk stopped it.

Nobody in the courtroom said anything. I heard Mateo let out a breath behind me. I heard Blake uncross his legs.

Patrice was staring at the monitor. At the woman in the sundress. At the pen.

"Mrs. Nunez," Harriet said gently. "Were you crying in that video?"

Patrice didn't answer.

"Mrs. Nunez?"

"No," she said.

"Thank you. Nothing further."

The magistrate didn't even call a recess. She took her half-glasses off and looked straight at Patrice, and then, for a long moment, straight past her, at the man in the second row.

"The motion to set aside the postnuptial agreement is denied," she said. "The court finds Mrs. Nunez was represented by independent counsel, read the agreement by her own account, and signed it voluntarily, as she said at the time in her own words. The agreement stands. Nunez Heating and Cooling is the separate property of Mr. Nunez." She turned a page. "Mr. Pryor, the court has also received a letter this morning from Ms. Lomax regarding how your client came to retain you, and a 2022 case in Fayette County, Kentucky. You'll want to read it before you file anything else in my courtroom."

Pryor had gone a color I've only seen on refrigerant lines that are about to freeze.

"We're adjourned," the magistrate said.

Out in the hallway, people were already standing around the way people stand around a car wreck on the interstate, slowing down so they can look. A couple of lawyers. A bailiff. A man waiting for a traffic case.

Blake walked out of the courtroom first, fast, phone already at his ear, and he got exactly four steps.

"Blake Haley?"

A short man in a windbreaker stepped in front of him and held out a thick envelope.

"You've been served."

Blake took it without thinking, the way people do. Behind the process server stood Candace, in her navy dress again, with her purse over her shoulder and her phone in her hand.

"That's the divorce, Blake," she said. "And a civil suit for the money you took from three women out of our joint account. They're all named in it. Toledo, Lexington. And Dayton."

"Candace, come on. Not here."

"You did it here." She looked over his shoulder at Patrice, who'd just come out of the courtroom with Pryor. "Mrs. Nunez. You should hear this. I'm sorry. Nobody played it for me, either, and I wish somebody had."

She held up her phone and pressed play, right there in the hallway of the fourth floor.

"...Patrice is the best one yet. The best one yet, man. This one's sitting on a million-four business, and once Lionel gets that postnup tossed, she's walking out with half of it. And she'll hand it right to me. She hands me everything."

Patrice stood in the hallway and listened to the whole thing. She didn't look at Blake. She looked at the phone, as if it were somebody she used to know.

"Patrice, babe," Blake said. "That's out of context. That's..."

"Don't," Patrice said. Very quietly. "Don't call me babe."

Blake looked at Pryor. Pryor was already walking toward the elevator without him. Blake stood there alone with the envelope in his hand and his sunglasses sticking out of his blazer pocket, and nobody in that hallway would look at him, not one person.

He went down the stairs. Nobody followed him.

Candace opened her purse and took out a cashier's check and handed it to me, not to Patrice.

"Thirty-one thousand, two hundred," she said. "That's what's left of the money from your joint savings. The rest went on golf clubs. It was your marital money, both of yours. I figured the court can sort out who gets which half."

I took it. I didn't look at the number. I handed it straight to Harriet.

"Put it in the joint account," I said. "Half of it's hers."

Patrice was looking at me now. Her mascara had started to run, for real this time, no tissue folded just right.

"Vince," she said. "Vince, I didn't know. I swear to God I didn't know. Can we just... can we go home and talk? Like adults? Please. I'll call Lionel off. I'll take the post down. We can fix this. Twenty-five years. We can fix it."

I looked at her. At the silver earrings I'd bought her for our twentieth. At the woman who'd walked into our party on another man's arm and told a hundred and twenty people that I couldn't keep up.

"You keep the house money and half the savings, Patrice. That's what you signed. It was always yours." I buttoned my gray suit jacket. "The shop is mine. The trucks are mine. And I'm not coming home to talk."

"Vince, please."

"Try to keep up," I said, and walked to the elevator.

Behind me, I heard my son say something to his mother, low, and I heard her start to cry. And then, loud enough for the bailiff and two lawyers and the man waiting for his traffic case to hear it, I heard my sister-in-law Gwen say the last thing anybody said in that hallway.

"Well, Patty. Looks like he kept up."
