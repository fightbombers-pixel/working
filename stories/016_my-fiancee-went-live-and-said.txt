"Can't wait to marry this fool and take half!"

My fiancée said it on a Facebook Live from her own bridal shower, with thirty women in the room behind her and a little counter in the corner of the screen that said 2,312 people were watching. The women behind her screamed laughing. Somebody blew one of those paper party horns.

I was watching it on my phone at the kitchen table of Fire Station 6, with a plate of chili going cold in front of me. Six weeks before the wedding. Thirty-one thousand dollars in deposits, every one of them on my credit card.

I didn't throw the phone. I didn't say a word. I put my spoon down, tapped the little comment box at the bottom of her live, and typed with one thumb.

"Joke's on you, the wedding's canceled."

I hit send, and I watched my own words float up the side of her screen.

Four minutes later the counter said 6,140, Maureen was jabbing at her phone trying to find the button to end it, and her maid of honor was holding a pink gift bag over the camera.

What Maureen didn't know was who had sent me the link to her live in the first place. It came from a number she knew very well. Her own sister's.

I'm Norman Steele. I'm 59, I'm a captain with the Erie Fire Department, and I've been on the job twenty-six years. If the job teaches you one thing, it's that a fire always tells you where it started, if you're patient enough to let it cool before you go poking. You don't charge in yelling. You read the burn pattern.

Hold on to that.

Lou Ferraro, my engineer, was across the table from me eating a grilled cheese. Lou's 46, built like a refrigerator, and he laughs at his own jokes before he finishes telling them. He leaned over to look at my screen just as Maureen read my comment out loud.

You could hear it clear as day through my phone speaker. She was still smiling when she started reading.

"Joke's on you, the wedding's..."

And then she stopped.

Her face dropped mid-stream. I mean it dropped, the way a cake drops when somebody slams the oven door. The smile stayed on her mouth for about two more seconds, like it didn't know it was supposed to leave, and the rest of her face had already left. Behind her, thirty women went quiet one at a time, like somebody was turning down a dial.

"Norm," she said to the camera. "Norm, that's not funny."

Comments were flying up the side of the screen faster than I could read them. "OMG." "Is that the fiancé??" "He's watching lol." "Somebody call the fire department." That last one got about two hundred hearts.

"It was a joke," Maureen said, to the phone, to the room, to nobody. "Everybody knows it was a joke. Right? Right, girls?"

Nobody answered her. Then the pink gift bag came down over the lens, and the live ended.

Lou put down his grilled cheese.

"Cap," he said. "Did you just cancel your wedding in the comment section?"

"Looks like it."

"On Facebook."

"She picked the venue."

Lou stared at me, and then he started laughing, that big helpless Lou laugh, and he had to put his forehead on the table.

"I'm sorry," he said, wheezing. "I'm sorry, it's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It's a lot funny. Thirty women, Cap."

My phone started ringing before he'd finished. Maureen. I let it ring. It rang again. And again. By the time the chili was cold all the way through, the screen said fourteen missed calls.

The text that mattered came in at 3:22. It was from Colleen, Maureen's younger sister.

"She's driving to the station. Rae's with her. I'm so sorry, Norm. Call me tomorrow. I have more."

I read the last three words twice. I have more.

You're probably wondering how a fire captain who's supposed to read burn patterns for a living ended up engaged to a woman who'd call him a fool to two thousand strangers. Fair question.

My wife Ellen passed in 2017. Cancer, fast and mean. We'd been married thirty years and I didn't date for six after, and my daughter Kara, who's 31 and lives in Pittsburgh and worries about me too much, kept telling me to get out of the house. I met Maureen the old way. She was the realtor showing the house next door to mine, and she knocked to ask if the basement on our street flooded.

She was 46, sharp, funny, and she sold more houses than anybody at Lakeshore Realty. She did those Facebook Lives every week, walking through kitchens, saying "Look at this backsplash, people!" in that big bright voice. I thought it was cute. I bought her a ring for $7,400 at a jeweler on State Street, and she showed it to the camera before she showed it to her mother.

There were things. There are always things. She kept score of who paid for what, and she'd bring it up in front of people months later. "Norm's paying, he still owes me for that dinner in Pittsburgh." She called me "the old man" at her office Christmas party, and everybody laughed, and I laughed, because what are you going to do. When we picked the wedding venue, she said in front of the events manager, "Put it on his card, he's got that city pension."

Small things. You put them in a drawer. Then one Saturday afternoon the drawer won't close.

At 3:51, a red Lexus I knew very well came around the corner on West 26th Street too fast and pulled right up onto the apron in front of our bay doors, which is the one place in Erie you're absolutely not allowed to park.

Lou looked out the window.

"Want me to tell her she's in a fire lane?"

"I'll tell her."

I went out through the bay. The engine was parked inside behind me, the doors were up, and three of my guys were washing Ladder 6 in the driveway. They all stopped washing. Rae Castellano got out of the passenger side first. Rae was Maureen's best friend from the office, the one who'd been holding the camera at the shower. She was still wearing a paper crown that said BRIDE SQUAD.

Maureen got out of the driver's side and came at me in her shower dress and heels, holding her phone in front of her like it was evidence.

"Do you have any idea what you just did?" she said.

"I answered you."

"In front of six thousand people!"

"You went live in front of two thousand first. I figured the rest were your fault."

"It was a joke, Norman."

"Okay."

"It was a joke! Everybody does bits at a shower. Rae, tell him."

Rae opened her mouth, then shut it, then opened it again. "Everybody was kind of doing bits," she said, without much behind it.

"See?"

"What part was the joke?" I asked. "The fool part or the half part?"

Maureen blinked. "What?"

"You said two things. Marry this fool. Take half. Which one was the joke?"

"Both! Neither! Oh my God, you're doing that thing."

"What thing?"

"That calm thing! That fire-captain thing where you talk slow like I'm a cat in a tree!"

Behind me one of my guys, a kid named Danny Reyes, made a noise that turned into a cough.

"You need to delete that comment," Maureen said. "Right now. And you need to post that you were kidding."

"I wasn't kidding."

"Norm."

"The wedding's canceled, Maureen. That part's not a bit."

"You can't cancel a wedding over one joke."

"I'm not canceling it over one joke. I'm canceling it because you said out loud what you meant, and I happened to be listening."

She laughed, short and mean. "Oh, so now you're a mind reader."

"No. I'm a fiancé who watched the live. Your sister sent me the link."

That stopped her cold. Her eyes went sideways to Rae, then back to me.

"Colleen sent you that?"

"She did."

"That little..." She caught herself, looked at my guys, put her smile back on. "Fine. Fine. You want to do this? You know what you lose if you cancel? The Yacht Club keeps your deposit. The band keeps theirs. The florist. That's your money, Norman, not mine. You're about to light thirty-one thousand dollars on fire because you can't take a joke."

"It's my money," I said. "I'll decide what to light."

"And you're not getting the ring back."

"We'll see."

"It was a gift."

"Then you should've treated the guy who gave it to you a little better."

Danny Reyes put his sponge down and walked very quickly into the station. I heard him laughing in the hose tower.

Maureen's face went red from the neck up. She pointed her phone at me.

"You'll be begging by Wednesday," she said. "You're 59 years old. You think somebody else is going to put up with you? You'll be eating chili out of a can in this firehouse until you die."

"The chili's homemade," I said. "Lou makes it."

She got back in the Lexus and slammed the door so hard the side mirror shook. Rae stood there on the apron in her paper crown, looking at me like she'd never really seen me before.

"Norm," Rae said quietly. "I... she said it was a bit. Before we went live. She said it'd be funny."

"Was it funny?"

Rae took off the paper crown and looked at it.

"Not anymore," she said, and she got in the car.

The Lexus backed off the apron and went up 26th Street, and I stood in the bay door with my hands in my pockets.

Lou came out and stood next to me.

"Cap," he said, "is that true? About the deposits?"

"Some of it."

"How much is some?"

"I'll find out Monday at nine," I said. "Right now I want to know what Colleen meant by I have more."

Colleen Dwyer met me Sunday morning at the Sunrise Diner on West 12th, the kind of place where the waitress calls you hon and the coffee could strip paint off a hydrant. Colleen is 43, three years younger than Maureen, and she runs the front desk at an orthodontist's office on Peach Street. She'd spent her whole life being the little sister who cleaned up after Maureen, and she looked like she hadn't slept.

She ordered wheat toast and didn't touch it.

"Before you say anything," she said, "I know I should've told you months ago."

"Told me what?"

"That she's done this before."

I put my cup down.

"The live?"

"The plan." Colleen pulled her purse into her lap and held it like a life jacket. "You know Ray? Her first husband?"

"She said he was a drunk who cheated."

"Ray was a dentist in Harborcreek who married her when she was 29. She stayed two years and three months. That's exactly how long she needed, because her lawyer told her anything under two years looks bad to a judge. She walked out with half the house and a car." Colleen's mouth twisted. "She used to call him 'the fool' too. At Thanksgiving. In front of Ray."

The waitress came by and topped me off. I waited until she left.

"Why are you telling me now?"

"Because I heard her say it about you at Mom's in August. Out on the porch with Rae. She said, 'Two years with the old man and I'm set. He's got the house paid off and a city pension and he doesn't even know what he's worth.'" Colleen's eyes filled. "And I didn't say anything. I went inside and did the dishes."

"And yesterday?"

"Yesterday she told the whole room she was going to do a bit for the live. She told us the line ahead of time. We all thought it was hilarious, because, I don't know, it was a shower and there was a lot of prosecco." Colleen wiped her eyes with a diner napkin. "And then I looked at your face in the ring photo she had taped to the cake table, and I went in the bathroom and sent you the link. I didn't think you'd actually be watching."

"I was on shift. We watch everything on shift."

She laughed a little, wet. "God, she's going to kill me."

"Is she going to kill you over the link, or over this?"

"Over this." She opened her purse and slid her phone across to me. "I'm not supposed to have this. She sent it to me by mistake in September. It was supposed to go to Rae."

It was a screenshot of a text. Maureen to Rae, with Colleen's name accidentally on top.

"Running numbers on the old man. House is worth about 260, paid off. Pension is 4,100 a month. After two years I get half of everything that went up, plus whatever I can argue. Wedding is on his card so I'm not out a dime."

I read it, and then I read it again, and then I slid the phone back across the table.

"I'm not going to use that," I said.

Colleen looked surprised. "Why not?"

"I don't need to. I'm not married to her. There's nothing to take half of."

She stared at me.

"She thought you'd fold," she said finally. "She told Rae last night in the car, 'He'll call by Monday. He always folds.'"

"I've folded a lot of laundry," I said. "I'm out of the folding business."

Colleen laughed for real that time. Then her phone buzzed on the table, and she glanced down, and her laugh went away.

"Oh no," she said.

"What?"

She turned the screen toward me. It was a group text from Maureen to her bridesmaids, sent four minutes earlier.

"Called the Yacht Club this morning. Added 40 more guests and the champagne tower. All on Norm's card. If he wants to cancel, let him eat it."

I looked at my watch. Sunday, 9:40 in the morning. The Yacht Club events office was closed until Monday.

"Well," I said, "that's a choice."

"What are you going to do?"

"Be there when they open."

I was standing outside the Erie Yacht Club events office at 8:55 Monday morning, with my reading glasses in my shirt pocket and a folder under my arm, wearing a shirt I'd ironed the night before. I iron everything. My father was a Navy man, and he ironed his jeans, and I picked it up and never put it down. Maureen used to say it made me look like I was going to court. I suppose this morning I sort of was.

The events manager was a woman named Janelle Ruggiero, about 50, with half-glasses on a beaded chain. She'd booked the wedding with us back in March. She let me in, looked at my face, and sat down slowly.

"I saw the live," she said.

"Everybody saw the live."

"Don't be. I'd like to cancel."

"Thank you."

"The refund will be twenty-two thousand to your card. Five to seven business days."

She printed the cancellation and slid it across. I signed it. My hand was steady, which surprised me a little.

"Yes, ma'am."

"In twenty years of doing weddings, I've had three people cancel. You're the only one who ironed his shirt."

"You weren't married," she said. "So this is short."

"Okay."

"The ring. Pennsylvania treats an engagement ring as a conditional gift. The condition is the wedding. No wedding, the ring goes back to the man who bought it. The Supreme Court here said fault doesn't matter. Doesn't matter who broke it off or why."

"Everybody saw the live."

"Change the locks. You don't need a reason. It's your house."

We met Tuesday at seven in the morning at a Tim Hortons on Peach Street, because Phil Marchetti said that was the only hour he had before showings. He was 64, with a white mustache and a Lakeshore Realty fleece vest zipped to the chin. "Okay."

Wednesday night, I found out what Maureen's smile was about.

I'm a fire captain. I've been on more chest pain calls than I could count, and I've learned you never, ever ignore those words. I also learned you don't drive over yourself, you send the right people.

The Station 6 fall pancake breakfast is a big deal on the west side. We roll the engine out onto the apron, set up folding tables in the bay, and four of us work two big griddles while the whole neighborhood comes through for six dollars a plate, kids get to sit in the driver's seat, and the money goes to the burn unit at Hamot. By nine-thirty there were close to two hundred people in the bay and a line out to the street.

I was at the griddle in my apron, sleeves rolled, when Rae Castellano came through the line.

She got her plate. Then instead of going to a table, she stood at the end of the griddle and waited until I looked up.

"Norm," she said, loud enough that the people in line could hear, "I was the one holding the camera. I laughed when she said it. I'm sorry. That's all. I'm sorry."

People turned around. An old man in a Steelers hat said, "That the live lady?" and his wife hit him on the arm.

"Thank you, Rae," I said. "Get some syrup. Lou made the good kind."

She went and sat down with Colleen, who'd come in quietly ten minutes earlier and was sitting with Dolores in the back. Kara was right up front, where she said she'd be, with a plate she hadn't touched. Dolores lifted her coffee cup at me, about an inch. That was a lot, coming from Dolores.

Lou slid in next to me at the griddle with a fresh bucket of batter and bumped me with his hip.

"Cap. You know she's coming."

"Rae called."

"Danny's got a pool going in the bay. Whether she shows before ten or after." He poured four perfect circles. "I took before ten. I need the money."

"You're betting on my love life?"

"I'm betting on your ex-love life. Totally different odds." He flipped a pancake without looking. "What are you gonna say?"

"I don't know yet."

"You always know."

"Not this time. This time I'm going to listen first and read the burn pattern."

Lou nodded slowly, like I'd said something deep, and then he ruined it.

"Cap, you say 'burn pattern' one more time, I'm gonna put it on a T-shirt and sell it at this breakfast for twenty bucks a pop."

"Burn unit gets the money?"

"Burn unit gets half," Lou said. "I'm taking half. Joke's on you."

Over at the front table, Kara heard that and laughed so hard she had to put her coffee down.

Then the red Lexus pulled up across the street. Not on the apron this time. She'd learned that much.

Maureen came walking up 26th Street in a camel coat with her hair done like she was going to a closing. She didn't get in line. She walked past two hundred people eating pancakes, straight to the griddle, and every head in the bay followed her like a tennis ball.

She stopped across the griddle from me. Her eyes were red, and I believe that part was real.

"Norm," she said. "Can we talk?"

"I'm working."

"Then I'll say it here." She took a breath and looked around, and her voice got bigger, the way it does on her lives. "I made a mistake. I said a stupid thing at a party, and I've lost my job, my friends won't call me back, and I've lost you. I'm asking you, in front of all these people, to give me one more chance."

The bay went quiet. Lou stopped flipping. Somebody's kid in the engine honked the horn by accident, and his mother hissed at him.

Maureen opened her hand. The ring was sitting in her palm. Seventy-four hundred dollars, catching the light from the bay door.

"Put it back on me," she said softly. "Right here. And we forget the whole thing."

I flipped two pancakes. They were done. I slid them onto a plate for the next guy in line, a little kid in a plastic fire helmet.

"Maureen," I said, "I'm not going to put it back on you."

"Norm, please. Everybody's watching."

"You wanted everybody watching. Last time too."

"I said I was sorry."

"You said you lost your job and your friends. That's not sorry. That's a list."

She flinched. Somewhere behind me Danny Reyes said, very quietly, "Oh, Cap."

"You're really going to do this to me?" Maureen's voice cracked. "Here? In front of the whole neighborhood?"

"I'm not doing anything to you. I'm making pancakes. You walked up to my griddle."

She looked at the ring in her hand for a long time. Then she looked over at the back table, where her sister and her mother were sitting with Rae, and at the front table, where my daughter was sitting with her arms crossed, and none of them moved to help her.

Her mouth trembled. She put the ring down on the steel edge of the griddle table, next to the box of paper plates, very carefully, like it might go off.

"Then keep it," she said. "Keep your stupid ring."

"Thank you," I said. "My lawyer will tell you that's the law in Pennsylvania anyway."

A few people laughed out loud. Maureen's face went red, and she turned and walked back out through two hundred people, and nobody stopped eating to watch her go, which I think was worse for her than if they had.

At the bay door she turned around one more time.

"You'll die alone in this firehouse, Norman," she called. "You're a fool."

Every head swung back to me. The little kid in the plastic helmet was still standing there with his plate.

I picked up the ring and put it in my apron pocket. Then I poured four more circles of batter on the griddle and handed the kid a bottle of syrup.

"Maybe so," I said. "But this fool's got two hundred people to feed. Next."
