"Sign it, Nelson, or I will turn this into a nightmare."

My wife pushed a stack of papers across our dining room table at ten o'clock on a Friday morning, in front of her lawyer, a notary public with a stamp in her lap, and the tennis pro from our country club, who was leaning in the doorway of my dining room like he already owned the doorframe. Four witnesses. Thirty-one years of marriage. One blue pen she'd set on top of the stack, uncapped, so I wouldn't have to bother.

The table was black walnut. My granddaddy built it in 1952 in the shop that still has our name on it, and I'd refinished it twice. I ran my thumb along the edge of it while she talked, the way I'd check a joint on the line.

"The house is mine," Marlene said. "Four hundred thousand in cash, by the first of the month. No alimony. You keep your precious factory. And you sign it today, or on Monday morning I start telling everyone in Catawba County what kind of man you really are."

"What kind of man am I?"

"Whatever kind I say you are," she said. "Try me."

I picked up the pen.

"Marlene, did you read all of it?"

"I don't need to read your factory crap," she said. "Warren read it. I want the house and I want out. Sign."

So I signed. Every page where the little yellow flags were. The notary stamped them. Then I reached in my pocket, took out my ring of keys, worked the two brass ones for the front door and the boathouse off the ring, and laid them on the walnut, right on top of the papers.

"It's all yours," I said. And I walked out of the house I'd paid for with my own money, and got in my truck, and drove away.

Four hours later, her own lawyer was shouting in my ear over a speakerphone, and he wasn't shouting at me.

What none of them knew was what was behind tab fourteen of the white binder I'd had delivered to that lawyer's office sixteen days before.

My name is Nelson Greene. I'm 57 years old, and I run Greene Furniture Works in Hickory, North Carolina, the company my granddaddy started with two lathes and a mule. We build solid wood case goods, dressers and sideboards and dining tables, and we employ one hundred and forty people. If there's one thing three generations of furniture men taught me, it's this: you check the joints, not the finish. Any fool can make a piece shine. What holds it together is underneath, where nobody looks. Hold on to that.

The house was on Lake Hickory, six bedrooms, a boathouse, a dock with a lift for a pontoon we never used. I'd bought the lot in 2004 with money from the business and built that house the way I build furniture, mortise and tenon, the whole way through. It was worth about two point one million. Marlene called it the mansion when she talked to her friends, like it was something she'd married into.

I drove three miles down the road to the little two-bedroom cabin on the cove that I'd rented two weeks earlier, when she first told me she wanted a divorce. I made myself a pimento cheese sandwich. I ate half of it standing at the sink.

Then I drove into town to see my lawyer.

Constance Mabry has been practicing family law in Hickory for thirty-five years. She's 63, wears her silver hair in a short cut like a man's, and keeps a jar of butterscotch candies on her desk that she never offers anybody. She looked up when I walked in and said, "Well?"

"I signed."

"All of it?"

"Every yellow flag."

She leaned back in her chair. "She didn't change a word? Paragraph eleven is still in there?"

"She's the one who wrote paragraph eleven, Connie. Her lawyer told her to take it out and she put it back in herself. He told me so in the hallway like he wanted credit for trying."

Constance took a butterscotch out of the jar, unwrapped it, and put it in her mouth. That was the first time in fifteen years I'd seen her eat one.

"Read it to me," she said.

I had my copy. I read it. "'Wife acknowledges receipt of Husband's complete financial disclosure and knowingly waives any further discovery. Wife waives any and all interest in Greene Furniture Works, Inc., its assets, its future earnings and any proceeds from its sale or transfer, now or in the future.'"

"Any proceeds from its sale," Constance said. "Her words."

"Her words."

"And the binder?"

"Delivered to Warren Pike's office sixteen days ago. His paralegal signed the receipt. Four hundred and eighty pages. Tab fourteen, page two hundred and twelve."

Constance smiled. It's a thing she does maybe twice a year, and you don't want to be on the other side of it. "So in plain words, Nelson, so you know what you've got. Your wife just signed a binding separation agreement, with her own lawyer present and a notary stamping it, that gives her a two-million-dollar house and four hundred thousand dollars. In return, she gave up every claim to a company that a firm in Grand Rapids has offered to buy for thirty-eight million dollars, and the offer letter was sitting in her own lawyer's file the whole time."

"That's about the size of it."

"And she never read it."

"She said she didn't need to read my factory crap."

"Did anybody else hear her say that?"

"A notary, her lawyer and her boyfriend."

Constance crunched the butterscotch. "Lord have mercy."

It was a little after two o'clock when my phone rang. Marlene. I looked at Constance. She nodded at the phone and pointed to the speaker button.

I answered and set it on her desk.

"What did you do?" Marlene said. She wasn't yelling yet. Her voice was high and thin, like a band saw just before the blade jumps. "What did you do, Nelson?"

"I signed your papers."

"Warren says... Warren's here, he says—" She broke off. "There's something about Grand Rapids. What's in Grand Rapids?"

Before I could answer, a man's voice came through the phone from somewhere behind her, loud enough that Constance's secretary stuck her head in the door.

"Do you even know what he just did to you?" Warren Pike was shouting. "Marlene! Do you understand what you just signed? It was in the binder! It was in my office the whole time and you told me you didn't want to hear about the company! You told me to just get you the house!"

"You're my lawyer! You were supposed to tell me!"

"I tried to tell you! I told you to take out paragraph eleven! You put it back in! You said, and I wrote this down, 'I don't want one board foot of that dusty factory, I want the lake house!'"

There was a sound like a chair hitting the floor.

"Thirty-eight million dollars, Marlene," Pike said, and now his voice cracked. "Thirty-eight million."

Then the line went quiet except for breathing. Marlene's breathing.

"Nelson," she said. "Nelson, is it true?"

"There's an offer from a company in Grand Rapids," I said. "It's been in your lawyer's office for sixteen days. Page two hundred and twelve."

"You hid it."

"I put it in a binder and had it hand-delivered to your lawyer and he signed for it. That's the opposite of hiding."

"You knew I wouldn't read it."

"I asked you at the table if you'd read all of it. You told me you didn't need to read my factory crap."

There was a long silence. When she spoke again, the high, thin sound was gone. What was left was flat and cold, and I'd heard it before, but never pointed at me.

"Fine," Marlene said. "Then I'll make it a nightmare. Starting Monday. At your plant. In front of every one of your hundred and forty people."

She hung up.

Constance looked at the phone, then at me. She held out the jar of butterscotch.

"Have one," she said. "You're going to need it."

Monday morning, first shift at Greene Furniture Works starts at seven. I'm usually there by six-fifteen, and that Monday I was there by six, sitting in my office with a cup of coffee and the blinds open so I could see the employee lot.

Marlene's white Range Rover pulled in at six-forty. Royce Tolbert's Audi pulled in right behind it.

Royce is 46, the head tennis pro at Lake Hickory Country Club, with a tan in February and a laugh he uses on rich women the way a fisherman uses a spinner bait. He'd been giving Marlene private lessons on Tuesdays and Thursdays for two years. I'd paid for every one of them. A hundred and twenty dollars an hour. I'd joked once that for that kind of money she ought to be playing at Wimbledon. She hadn't laughed.

Royce got out holding his phone up in front of him, already recording. Marlene got out in a camel coat and big sunglasses, and walked straight to the gate where the first-shift guys were lining up to clock in, holding their lunch boxes and their thermoses.

I went down.

By the time I got to the gate, she'd already started. There were maybe forty people standing around her, sanders and finishers and the boys from the rough mill. Dewey Hollins, my plant foreman, was at the front with his arms crossed. Dewey is 61 and has worked for my family since he was eighteen. He once lost the end of a finger to a jointer and drove himself to the hospital so he wouldn't hold up the line.

"...selling you out!" Marlene was saying. "He's selling this whole place to a company in Michigan for thirty-eight million dollars, and do you know what you're going to get? Pink slips! They'll move it all to Mexico in a year! He's been lying to every one of you!"

Royce panned the phone across the crowd for effect.

"And that's not all he lies about," Marlene said, louder. "You don't know what he's like at home. You don't know what I've lived with for thirty-one years. The control. The coldness. He's a—"

"Mrs. Greene," Dewey said.

She stopped.

"Ma'am, with respect," Dewey said, "we know about the sale."

Marlene's sunglasses turned toward him. "What?"

"He told us. Two weeks ago Friday. Called the whole plant into the finishing room, all three shifts, and stood up on a pallet and told us." Dewey uncrossed his arms. "The company in Grand Rapids has to keep every job for five years. It's in the contract. He made them put it in, or no deal. And everybody here who's been on the payroll a year or more gets a check when it closes. Ten thousand dollars a head. Out of his end."

The crowd shifted. Somebody in the back said, "That's right."

"He didn't tell you that?" Dewey said, not unkindly.

Marlene opened her mouth. Nothing came out.

"And as for what he's like," said a woman's voice, and Bonnie Ruff from the finishing line pushed her way to the front. She's 58 and has the strongest hands I've ever seen on a human being. "When my husband had his stroke, Nelson drove me to Baptist in Winston-Salem himself at two in the morning and sat in the waiting room till six. Didn't say a word about it to anybody. So you go on and tell us what he's like, Mrs. Greene. I'll wait."

Royce lowered his phone.

"Royce," I said. "You can keep filming. I'd actually like a copy."

He put it in his pocket.

Marlene turned and looked at me for the first time. Her face was red under the sunglasses.

"You told them," she said. "You told your workers about the sale two weeks ago. And you didn't tell me."

"It was in the binder, Marlene. Tab fourteen."

"You knew I wouldn't read it!"

"I asked you at the table."

She turned around and walked back to the Range Rover with Royce trotting behind her. The crowd parted for her without anybody saying a word. It was the quietest I've ever heard that gate at six forty-five on a Monday.

Dewey came and stood beside me while they pulled out.

"Boss," he said, "I've seen a lot of things in forty-three years. That's the first time I ever saw somebody try to start a strike against a ten-thousand-dollar bonus."

The whistle blew for first shift. People went in. I stood there by the gate a minute longer, and my phone buzzed in my pocket.

It was a text from my daughter, Lily. She's 21, a senior at App State up in Boone, studying accounting, which she says she got from me and which I say she got from her grandmother.

"Dad. Royce just posted a video of Mom yelling at the plant gate. Then deleted it. Then I watched it four times. Are you okay?"

Before I could answer, the phone rang. Constance.

"Warren Pike filed a motion this morning," she said. "He's asking the court to throw out the separation agreement. Says it was signed through fraud and concealment. Says you tricked her."

"Did he say how?"

"He says you hid the sale." She paused. "His own office signed for it, Nelson. He's either very desperate or very scared."

"Which one?"

"Both," Constance said. "He's scared she'll sue him for malpractice. So he's going after you to save his own neck. Thursday, ten a.m., his conference room. He wants a sit-down."

Warren Pike's office is on the fourth floor of the old bank building downtown, with a conference room that has a view of the railroad tracks and a long glass table you can see everybody's shoes under. He was fifty-something, pink in the face, with a gold tie bar and the look of a man who'd been sleeping very badly since Friday.

Marlene sat beside him. No sunglasses this time. She looked like she hadn't slept either.

Royce wasn't in the room. Through the glass wall, I could see him in the waiting area, scrolling his phone.

"Let's be direct," Pike said. "My client signed that agreement under duress and without full knowledge of a material fact. Namely, a pending sale worth thirty-eight million dollars. We're asking you to rescind the agreement voluntarily, and we'll start over in good faith. Otherwise we take it to a judge, and we let the judge decide whether a husband hiding a thirty-eight-million-dollar deal from his wife looks like good faith."

Constance didn't say anything. She opened a manila folder and slid one sheet of paper across the glass.

"What's this?" Pike said.

"Receipt of delivery," Constance said. "Four hundred and eighty pages of financial disclosure, delivered to this office, this floor, on the second of the month. Signed by your paralegal, Kristen Burch. Time-stamped two-fourteen p.m."

Pike looked at it. He didn't pick it up.

"I'm aware of the receipt."

"Then you're aware of tab fourteen."

"The letter of intent was buried in four hundred and eighty pages—"

"It was the first page of a tab labeled 'Pending Transactions,'" Constance said. "In a twenty-four-point font. My paralegal made the tabs. She's very proud of them."

Marlene spoke up for the first time. "I didn't know. I didn't know about any of it. He lied to me for thirty-one years about that company. He never told me what it was worth."

"Did you ever ask?" I said.

She turned on me. "It was a furniture factory, Nelson. Sawdust and old men. Nobody buys furniture factories."

"Somebody from Grand Rapids does."

"You planned this! You planned this whole thing! You waited until I wanted out and then you—"

"Marlene," I said. "I've been talking to those people in Grand Rapids for eleven months. You told me you wanted a divorce three weeks ago. You told me you wanted it signed by Friday. You wrote the paper. You put in the paragraph about the company. I didn't plan your divorce. I planned to sell my company. You planned to take my house."

"Our house!"

"I signed it over to you. It's your house now. Just like you wanted."

Pike cleared his throat. "Duress. My client was under enormous emotional pressure to sign—"

"Your client," Constance said pleasantly, "was the one making the threats. 'Sign it, Nelson, or I will turn this into a nightmare.'" She slid a second sheet across the glass. "That's the notary's sworn statement, Warren. Mrs. Vera Dunning. Twenty-two years as a notary public. She heard every word. She also heard your client say, 'I don't need to read your factory crap.' She wrote that down, too. It's a habit of hers when a signer says something she thinks a judge might want to hear."

Pike read the notary's statement. He read it twice. Then he took off his gold tie bar, set it on the table, and rubbed his eyes.

"Marlene," he said, very quietly. "Could I speak with you outside?"

"No! Say it here!"

"Fine." Pike didn't look at her. "If we go to court, the judge reads that statement. Then the judge reads paragraph eleven, which you wrote yourself, after I advised you against it in writing. Then the judge looks at the receipt my own office signed. Then the judge finds the agreement valid, and you pay his attorney's fees for bringing a motion with no merit." He picked the tie bar back up. "That's what happens if we go to court."

"So sue him for something else!"

"There's nothing else," Pike said. "You have the house. You have four hundred thousand dollars. You signed away the rest."

Marlene stood up so fast her chair rolled backward and bumped the glass wall. On the other side of it, Royce looked up from his phone.

She walked out of the conference room. Through the glass, I watched her go to him. I watched her talk fast, hands moving, and I watched Royce stand up, very slowly, and put his phone in his pocket.

He said something to her. Just one sentence. I couldn't hear it through the glass, but I've thought about it since, and I'd bet my granddaddy's lathe it was a question with a number in it.

Marlene stood there like he'd slapped her.

Royce picked up his jacket off the waiting room chair, put it on, and walked to the elevator without her.

Two nights later, Saturday night, there was a knock on the door of my rented cabin on the cove. It was Lily, in a hoodie and pajama pants, with her car keys in one hand and a bag of Bojangles in the other.

"I drove down from Boone," she said. "I brought chicken. Don't argue."

I didn't argue. We sat at the little table by the window with the lake going dark outside, and she ate a Cajun filet biscuit in about four bites, and then she put her hands flat on the table the way I do, and I knew she'd been practicing whatever she was about to say for the whole hour and a half drive.

"Mom called me Thursday night," she said. "After the meeting. She was crying. She said you destroyed her. She said you hid money and lied and tricked her into signing away everything."

"What did you say?"

"I said, 'Mom, did you read it?'" Lily picked at the biscuit wrapper. "She hung up on me."

"I'm sorry, Lil."

"Don't be. I'm an accounting major, Dad. I asked Mr. Pike's paralegal for a copy of the agreement on Friday. She sent it to me because I'm Mom's emergency contact and she was confused." She almost smiled. "I read paragraph eleven. Mom wrote it, didn't she? It sounds just like her. 'Not one board foot.'"

"She wrote it."

"And she didn't read tab fourteen."

"She didn't read any of it."

Lily nodded slowly. "Royce broke up with her. Did you know that?"

"I saw him leave."

"He asked her how much of the thirty-eight million was hers. She said 'none.' He said, 'Then what was the point of all this?' Right in front of the elevator. She told me that part herself. She said it like she couldn't believe it." Lily's voice went hard. "I could believe it. I told her two years ago Royce was a creep. She said I was jealous of her happiness."

We sat there for a minute. A boat went by on the lake with its running lights on.

"There's something else," Lily said. "Grandma Opal's eightieth is next Saturday. At the club."

"I know. She mailed me an invitation. Handwritten."

"She did?" Lily's eyebrows went up. "Mom doesn't know that."

"I figured."

"Dad, Mom's telling people she's still going to announce her engagement there. To Royce. She says he's just 'cooling off' and he'll come around once the lawyers fix the money. She told Aunt Peggy she wants to stand up at Grandma's party and announce it in front of the whole family so everybody knows she's 'moved on and happy.'"

"At her mother's eightieth."

"At her mother's eightieth." Lily leaned forward. "And Grandma called me this afternoon. She told me to make sure you came. She said, and I'm quoting her exactly, 'Tell your father I want him there, and tell him to bring whatever it is he's been carrying around in that white binder.'"

I looked at my daughter for a long time.

"Lil," I said. "The closing's next Saturday too. Ten in the morning. In Charlotte."

"Same day as the party?"

"Same day."

She sat back. Then she grinned, and it was her grandmother's grin, and it was a little bit frightening.

"Then I guess you'd better wear a tie," she said.

Wednesday night, a little after nine, headlights swept across the front window of the cabin. I knew the Range Rover by the sound of the door.

Marlene stood on the little porch in a sweater I'd bought her in Blowing Rock fifteen years ago. She'd brought a bottle of the red wine we used to drink on the dock. She didn't ask to come in. She held the bottle up like a ticket.

"I think we should talk," she said. "Like adults."

"We're talking."

"Nelson, this whole thing got out of hand. The lawyers, the plant, all of it. I was hurt and I was angry, and Royce was in my ear, and I did things I shouldn't have." She tried a smile. It was the one she used on waiters. "Royce is gone. It's over. I made a mistake. Thirty-one years, Nelson. That has to count for something."

"It counts for a lot."

"Then let's tear it up. The agreement. Both of us. We tell the lawyers it was a mistake, and we go back to how it was. I'll even come to the plant Christmas party. I'll wear a hairnet if you want."

"You want to tear up the agreement," I said, "the week before the closing."

Her smile held, but only just. "That's not what this is about."

"Marlene, three weeks ago you told me you'd never loved the factory or the man who ran it. A week ago you told my workers I was selling them out. Monday your boyfriend asked you how much of thirty-eight million was yours. Tonight you're on my porch with a bottle of wine." I put my hand on the door. "I read the whole binder. Every time."

The smile went. What came after it was the face from the dining room.

"You'll be sorry you didn't take this," she said. "I gave you a chance."

"I know you did. Good night, Marlene."

She walked back to the Range Rover with the wine still in her hand. She didn't slam the door. She sat in the driveway for a long time with the dome light on, typing on something that glowed in her lap, and then she backed out and drove toward the lake house.

The week did not go the way I'd planned it.

Thursday afternoon, two days before the closing, I was on the finishing floor watching Bonnie Ruff hand-rub a cherry sideboard when my cell phone rang with a 616 number. Grand Rapids. It was Clark Weston, the vice president who'd been running the deal for their side for eleven months. Clark is a careful man in his sixties who wears bow ties and says "golly" without irony, and I'd come to like him.

He wasn't saying golly.

"Nelson," he said. "I've got a problem, and I need you to be straight with me."

"I'm always straight with you, Clark."

"I know. That's why I'm calling instead of our lawyers." He took a breath. "This morning our CEO got an email. From a Marlene Greene. She says your books are padded. She says your plant is about to walk out on strike, and she says there's a lawsuit coming that'll tie up the company for years. She attached a video of her talking to your workers at the gate."

I closed my eyes. Royce had deleted that video from his page, but not from his phone.

"Clark, that video's ten minutes long. Did your CEO watch the whole thing?"

"He watched about forty seconds and called me. Nelson, he's nervous. We're pausing the closing until we can look into it. I'm sorry. It's not personal."

That was the closest I came, in that whole business, to losing my temper. Eleven months of work. A hundred and forty jobs written into a contract. Ten thousand dollars a head for people like Bonnie and Dewey, money that would pay off their mortgages and their kids' student loans. And my wife had sent one email from the lake house that I'd just signed over to her.

"Clark," I said. "Can you be on a plane tonight?"

"Why?"

"Because I want you to walk my plant tomorrow at seven a.m. at shift change. Talk to anybody you want. Open any drawer you want. Pull any file you want. If you find one thing wrong, walk away and I'll pay for your ticket."

There was a long pause on the line.

"I'll be there at six-thirty," Clark said.

He was there at six-twenty. He stood at the same gate where Marlene had stood four days before, with a cup of my break-room coffee in his hand and his bow tie a little crooked from the red-eye. Dewey met him first. They shook hands, and Clark said, "Mr. Hollins, I'm told your people are about to strike."

Dewey laughed so loud the first-shift line turned around to look.

"Mister," he said, "these people are about to get ten thousand dollars apiece and five years' guaranteed work. The only strike around here's going to be at the bowling alley when they cash those checks."

Clark walked the floor for three hours. He talked to Bonnie. He talked to the boys in the rough mill. He talked to a nineteen-year-old kid named Elias who'd been with us eight months and who told him, without being asked, that Mr. Greene had paid for his welding class at the community college. He spent an hour in accounting with our bookkeeper, going through receivables like a man looking for a splinter.

Then he went to the showroom and did something I'll never forget. He walked up to a walnut dresser we'd built for a client in Asheville, pulled the top drawer all the way out, turned it over in his hands and ran his thumb down the corner.

"Hand-cut dovetails," he said.

"On the front," I said. "Machine on the back. My granddaddy's rule. Where it shows, it's done by hand. Where it holds, it's done right."

Clark put the drawer back. It slid in like it was on ice.

"Nelson," he said, "our CEO's going to ask me what I found. I'm going to tell him I found a man whose wife is mad at him." He straightened his bow tie. "We close tomorrow at ten. Charlotte. I'll be the one with the pen."

He shook my hand. Then he paused at the door.

"One more thing," he said. "That email came from her personal account, sent from an iPad registered to a home address on Lake Hickory. Our lawyers noticed that. They're going to want to send her a letter about interfering with a contract."

"Do what you have to do, Clark."

"I always do," he said.

The closing was in a law office on the thirty-first floor of a tower in uptown Charlotte. It took forty minutes and nineteen signatures. Clark signed for Grand Rapids. I signed for three generations of Greenes. At ten-forty, a lawyer I'd never met slid a single sheet across the table that confirmed the wire, and Clark shook my hand and said, "Golly, Nelson. That's done."

I sat in my truck in the parking deck afterward for a while. I called Dewey and told him. I heard him put the phone against his chest and holler it across the finishing room, and I heard the room holler back.

Then I drove back to Hickory and put on a tie.

Lake Hickory Country Club holds about two hundred people in the big ballroom. Opal Hensley's eightieth birthday party had about ninety, which in Opal's family counts as small. There were round tables with white cloths and hydrangeas, a three-tier cake with buttercream roses, and a jazz trio playing Glenn Miller for the old folks and Earth, Wind and Fire for everybody else.

Opal was at the head table in a navy dress with a corsage the size of a cabbage. She'd been a bookkeeper at a textile mill for forty-four years, outlived the mill and her husband, and she still did her own taxes by hand at the kitchen table every March with a sharpened pencil.

When I walked in with Lily on my arm, conversation at three tables stopped. Marlene's sister Peggy actually put her hand over her mouth.

Marlene was standing near the cake in a red dress I'd never seen. She saw me, and her fork stopped halfway to her mouth.

She crossed the room in about four strides.

"What are you doing here?"

"Your mother invited me."

"She did not."

"She wrote it by hand," I said. "Want to see it?"

"Get out. This is my family."

"Marlene." Opal's voice came from the head table. It wasn't loud. It didn't have to be. "I invited him. Sit him at my table. Lily too."

Marlene turned around and stared at her mother. Opal just looked back at her and picked up her iced tea.

We sat at Opal's table. She patted my hand and said, "Did you bring the binder?"

"It's in the truck."

"Good. Go get it after the cake."

Dinner was chicken cordon bleu and green beans almondine. Marlene sat two tables away and didn't eat. I watched her check her phone eleven times. On the twelfth, her face did something complicated and she put the phone face down on the table.

Lily leaned over. "She keeps checking Royce's Instagram," she whispered. "He posted an hour ago."

"What'd he post?"

Lily showed me her own phone. It was a picture of Royce on the tennis court at the club, at that very moment about three hundred yards from where we sat, with his arm around a blond woman in her fifties I recognized as Phyllis Monroe, whose husband had owned half the car lots in Catawba County until he died last spring. The caption said: "New doubles partner. Life's good."

"Oh, Lord," I said.

"Yeah," Lily said. "She's going to do it anyway. Watch."

After the cake came out and everybody sang and Opal blew out eight candles, one for each decade, Marlene stood up and tapped her wine glass with a fork.

The room went quiet.

"I'd like to say a few words," Marlene said. "About my mother."

She talked for about two minutes about Opal. It was a good toast, honestly. Opal raising three girls alone after Daddy died. Opal's pound cake. Opal driving the church van until she was seventy-five. People laughed in the right places.

Then Marlene's voice changed.

"And I want to share some news with my family, because Mama always taught us there's no shame in a fresh start." She lifted her glass higher. "This has been a hard year. Most of you know Nelson and I are divorcing. But I want you all to know that I've moved on. I'm happy. And I'm engaged, to a wonderful man who appreciates me, Royce Tolbert."

There was a scattered sound in the room that wasn't quite applause. A few people clapped. More people looked at their phones. I watched it happen. At table after table, somebody leaned over and showed somebody else the picture of Royce and Phyllis Monroe on the tennis court.

Peggy said, out loud, "Marlene, honey. He's outside right now with Phyllis Monroe."

Marlene's glass was still in the air. "He's... we're just taking a break."

"He posted a picture an hour ago," somebody said from the back.

"That's not, that's just tennis—"

"Marlene," Opal said. "Sit down, sugar."

Marlene didn't sit down. She turned and looked at me, and everything she'd been holding in for two weeks came up all at once.

"This is your fault," she said. "All of it. You tricked me. You hid thirty-eight million dollars from me and let me sign it away. Thirty-one years I gave you, and you cheated me out of my half!"

Now the room was really quiet. The jazz trio had stopped.

"Your half of what, Marlene?" I said.

"Of the company! Of the sale! Royce said if I'd gotten my half of the sale we'd have—" She stopped. Her hand went to her mouth.

"Would have what?" Opal said.

Marlene didn't answer. She didn't have to. Ninety people had heard it.

Opal set down her fork, very carefully, on the edge of her plate. "Nelson," she said. "Would you get that binder for me now, please?"

I went out to the truck and got it. When I came back in, nobody had moved. I set the white binder down in front of Opal, and she opened it to the tab that said fourteen in my bookkeeper's neat handwriting, and she put on her reading glasses.

"Did you give this to her lawyer?" Opal asked me.

"Sixteen days before she signed. His office signed for it."

"And this page? Page two hundred and twelve?"

"First page of the tab."

Opal read it. She read it the way she'd read ledgers for forty-four years, with her finger moving under each line. Then she looked up at her daughter over the top of her glasses.

"It says right here," Opal said. "Letter of intent to purchase Greene Furniture Works for thirty-eight million dollars. Plain as day. First page." She turned the binder around so Marlene could see. "Did you read this, Marlene?"

"Mama—"

"Did you read it?"

"No."

"Did your lawyer tell you to read it?"

Marlene's voice was barely there. "He told me to take out the part about the company. I put it back."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted the house. Because I didn't want one board foot of that dusty factory. Because I wanted it done by today so I could stand up here and..." She stopped.

"So you could stand up at my birthday," Opal said, "and announce you were marrying a man who's out on the tennis court with Phyllis Monroe."

Marlene sat down. Not because she'd been told to. Her knees just quit on her.

"You have a two-million-dollar house on the lake," Opal said. "With taxes and insurance and a boathouse that leaks. You have four hundred thousand dollars. That's more than your daddy and I ever saw in our lives put together. You threw away the rest because you wouldn't turn a page." She closed the binder. "I told you when you were twelve, when you signed up for that record club and it took you six months of babysitting money to get out of it. Read before you sign. You rolled your eyes at me then too."

Somebody at the back table laughed, then stopped.

"Nelson," Opal said, turning to me. "You signed what she put in front of you."

"Yes ma'am."

"And you left her the keys."

"Yes ma'am."

"And you've been honest with her the whole time?"

"I asked her at the table if she'd read it," I said. "She said she didn't need to read my factory crap."

Opal nodded slowly. She took off her reading glasses and folded them.

"Well," she said. "It's my birthday, and I'd like my present now."

Lily reached under the table and handed me the box I'd brought in that afternoon. It was a jewelry chest, walnut, about the size of a shoebox, with three small drawers. I'd built it in the evenings over the last two weeks, in the shop after the second shift went home. It was the first thing I'd made with my own hands since the divorce started.

Opal opened the top drawer. Then she did what every old bookkeeper and every old furniture man does. She pulled it all the way out, turned it over and ran her thumb down the corner.

"Hand-cut," she said.

"Where it shows," I said. "Where it holds, it's done right."

Opal slid the drawer back into the chest. It went in like it was on ice. She looked across the table at her daughter, who was sitting in her red dress with her face in her hands and her phone face down by her plate.

"Marlene, honey," Opal said, loud enough for the whole ballroom. "That's how you hold something together. You should have checked the joints."
