"We've decided it's time to divorce you."

My wife said it from the middle of our own sectional on a Sunday afternoon, with three of her best friends sitting around her and a bottle of pinot grigio sweating on a coaster I bought at Menards for $4.99.

Diane said "we." That's the part I noticed first. Not "I've decided." We. And the three women on either side of her smirked at me like a jury that had already been to lunch.

There was a stack of papers on the coffee table. A divorce petition from a lawyer in De Pere, with a yellow sticky note on the top page that said "sign here, here and here." Twenty-seven years of marriage, and somebody had bought the sticky notes in bulk.

"We," I said.

"The girls and I talked about it," Diane said. "They agree with me. You're not a bad man, Karl. You're just not enough. You're never home in tax season, you fall asleep at nine, and you haven't surprised me since the first Bush administration."

Trish Gallo laughed into her wine glass. Marla Stokes crossed her legs and looked at her nails. Gwen Ferraro, who's been divorced four years and has had nothing nice to say about men since, nodded like she was at church.

I put my coffee mug down on the end table. Then I reached into my back pocket, took out a folded sheet of paper, opened it up, and laid it on top of the divorce petition, right on the sticky note.

It was our phone bill. Highlighted in yellow.

"So none of you care," I asked, "that she's been seeing your husbands?"

Forty minutes later, Trish Gallo would be standing in her own driveway on Cherry Lane, throwing her husband's golf clubs into the street one at a time, while half the block watched from their porches.

What none of them knew was what was in the manila envelope on the front seat of my truck. It had one word written on it in my handwriting. Gwen.

My name is Karl Daniels. I'm 56, and I'm a CPA with my own small firm on Main Street in Green Bay, Wisconsin. Two employees, about four hundred clients, and a coffee maker older than most of my clients' kids. Thirty years of other people's books teaches you one thing above all others. People lie. Statements don't. Hold on to that.

For about five seconds, nobody in my living room moved. Then Trish leaned forward and picked up the phone bill with two fingers, like it might bite her.

"What is this?" she said.

"That's Diane's line," I said. "On our family plan. I pay it every month. Those highlighted calls are to one number. Six-two-zero, four-four-one, eighteen-oh-seven."

Trish's face changed. You know the look a person gets when they recognize their own phone number, except it isn't theirs? It's their husband's.

"That's Dom's cell," she said.

"Two hundred and twelve calls since March," I said. "Some of them at two in the morning. Some of them forty minutes long. Dom doesn't talk to me for forty minutes, and we've been in the same Packers pool for nine years."

"Karl," Diane said. "Stop it. Right now."

"You said it was time. I agree."

Marla hadn't picked anything up. She was still looking at her nails. That's what I noticed, and I want you to notice it too. She wasn't surprised. Remember that.

"There's a yellow one and a green one," Gwen said, leaning over. "What's the green?"

"The green is from two years ago," I said. "Different number. Six-two-zero, five-five-two, thirty-one-ten."

Marla finally looked up.

"That's Kenny," she said, very quietly.

"Ninety-four calls," I said. "January through June of 2024. They stop the week Kenny had his hernia surgery."

Trish stood up so fast her wine sloshed onto my carpet. She looked at Diane, and Diane looked at the coffee table, and that was all the answer anybody in that room needed.

"You told me he was helping you with your car insurance," Trish said. "You said Dom was helping you shop for a car."

"Trish, honey, sit down, he's twisting..."

"Two in the morning, Diane? Who shops for a car at two in the morning?"

"He's an accountant! He makes everything look like something!"

"It's a phone bill," I said. "It looks like a phone bill."

Trish grabbed her purse off the floor. She didn't say goodbye. She walked straight out my front door and left it open, and a second later her Tahoe started in the driveway, and she backed out so hard she clipped my recycling bin.

Diane turned to me with her face going dark red from the neck up.

"Are you happy?" she said.

"Not really."

"You planned this."

"You planned this," I said, and I tapped the sticky note on the petition. "You bought the sticky notes."

Gwen was still sitting on the end of the sectional. She hadn't moved. She was looking at the bill, at the yellow lines and the green lines, and then she looked up at me, and her voice was very small.

"Karl," she said. "Is there one for me?"

Diane's head snapped around.

"Gwen, don't be ridiculous. You don't even have a husband."

"I had one," Gwen said. "Until four years ago."

She looked at me, and I didn't say anything, and that was the worst thing I could have done, because it told her everything a yes would have.

"It's not on that bill," I said. "It's in my truck."

Gwen stood up. She walked to the front window and looked out at my Silverado in the driveway, where the manila envelope with her name on it was sitting on the passenger seat.

"Don't you dare," Diane said to me. "Don't you dare give her that."

That's when I knew she knew exactly what was in it.

Gwen didn't go out to the truck that day. She said she couldn't. She said she wanted to read it alone, and she'd call me, and she left with her hands shaking so badly she couldn't get her keys out of her coat pocket. Marla helped her. Then Marla turned around in my doorway and looked back at me, very calm.

"I'll call you, Karl," she said. "Not today. But I'll call."

Then it was just me and Diane and an open bottle of pinot grigio.

"You've ruined my life," she said.

"I read a phone bill out loud."

"In front of my friends!"

"You called them in to watch me get divorced. I figured they'd want the whole show."

She grabbed the petition off the coffee table and held it against her chest.

"Okay, fine. Yes. Dom and I have been seeing each other. Are you happy? He listens to me. He takes me places. He doesn't fall asleep during Jeopardy."

"And Kenny?"

"Kenny was a mistake."

"And Russ Ferraro?"

She went quiet. That was a long quiet. In the kitchen, the dishwasher clicked over to the dry cycle.

"That was a long time ago," she finally said.

"Four years."

"You don't know anything about that."

"I know you told me Russ was in our driveway at three in the afternoon in October of 2021 because he was dropping off a casserole dish from Gwen."

"He was!"

"Gwen was in Florida that week. At her sister's. She sent us a postcard from Clearwater."

Diane stared at me like she was seeing me for the first time. Like a piece of furniture had started talking.

"You kept the postcard," she said.

"I keep everything."

She threw the divorce papers at me. They didn't make it very far. Paper never does. They fluttered down across the coffee table and onto the floor, sticky notes and all.

"Dom's going to leave Trish," she said. "We've already talked about it. We're getting married. And I'm getting this house and half of that little firm of yours, and you're going to pay me maintenance until you're dead, because that's how it works in Wisconsin. My lawyer said so."

"Your lawyer said a lot," I said.

Her phone started ringing on the arm of the sectional. She looked at the screen. It said Dom. She picked it up and walked into the kitchen, and I could hear him through the phone from twenty feet away. Not words. Just a man yelling.

"Baby, calm down," Diane said. "Calm down. I know. I know she did. No, don't. Dom, don't come over here. Dom, do not..."

She came back into the living room holding the phone against her chest.

"He's coming over," she said. "And Karl? He's really, really mad at you."

It was a little after eleven that night when the headlights came up the driveway.

Diane had gone to her sister's in Howard with a suitcase around eight. I'd made a grilled cheese, eaten half of it, and sat in my recliner with the TV on and the sound off. The game was a replay. The Packers lost it the first time too.

The truck was a white F-250 with "Gallo Plumbing and Heating" on the door. Dom Gallo is fifty-one, built like a water heater, and has been the guy at the neighborhood barbecue who wants to arm-wrestle everybody since about 2003.

He didn't knock. He pounded.

I opened the inside door but not the storm door. I'm a CPA, not an idiot. Above my head, the little Ring camera I'd put in two summers ago blinked its blue light.

"You son of a witch," Dom said. "You had no right."

"Evening, Dom."

"You told my wife. In front of everybody."

"I told my wife's friends what was on my own phone bill. Your wife happened to be one of them."

"Trish threw me out! She threw my clubs in the street! Mrs. Castellano across the road called the cops on a seven iron!"

"Sounds like a tough night for the seven iron."

He grabbed the handle of the storm door and yanked. It was locked. He yanked again.

"Open this door."

"No."

"Open the door, Karl, so we can talk like men."

"We are talking. You're doing great. The camera loves you."

He looked up at the blue light, and for a second I thought he'd go home. Then he did what guys like Dom always do. He decided the camera didn't count. He put his fist straight through the glass panel of my storm door.

It didn't break all the way. It's tempered. It turned milky and spidered and then fell out of the frame in a sheet of little cubes, all over my welcome mat, and Dom's hand came through bleeding, reaching for the inside latch.

I'm not a fighter. I'll say that up front. The last fight I was in, I was fourteen and lost to a kid named Wally Burns over a Topps baseball card. But I'd spent thirty years carrying file boxes up and down stairs, and I was standing on the inside of my own house.

I grabbed his wrist with both hands and pulled.

Dom came through the doorframe with his arm first and the rest of him following, and he caught his knee on the metal kickplate, and his weight did the rest. He went face first onto my hallway rug. He tried to get up and swing, and he did swing, and he caught me on the left side of the jaw hard enough that I saw a little white light. Then I put my knee on his back and my forearm across his shoulders, and I held him there while he yelled into my rug.

"Get off me!"

"No."

"I'll kill you!"

"Ring camera, Dom. Say that again a little louder for the deputies."

Across the street, a porch light came on. Then another. Then Mrs. Ortega next door came out in her bathrobe holding her phone up, and I heard her say, "Yes, hello, I'm calling about the man at the Danielses' house."

Dom stopped struggling. He lay there breathing hard, his cut hand bleeding onto my rug, and he started to laugh. It wasn't a nice laugh.

"You think you're so smart," he said into the floor. "You think it's about me? You don't even know about the money."

"What money?"

"Ask her about Kenny's money." He turned his head so one eye could see me. "Forty thousand dollars, Karl. Ask your precious wife where Kenny's forty grand went. Because I sure as hell never saw it."

Then the red and blue lights came around the corner onto our street.

The deputy who came was a young guy named Ruiz with a crew cut and the patience of a man who'd already been to two domestic calls that night. He watched the Ring video on my phone twice, standing in my hallway in a pile of tempered glass, while his partner sat Dom on my porch steps with a towel around his hand.

"He put his fist through your door," Deputy Ruiz said.

"Yes, sir."

"Then he swung at you."

"Yes, sir."

"And you held him down until we got here."

"I'm an accountant. Holding things down is most of the job."

He almost smiled. He didn't quite let himself.

"Mr. Gallo's going to get a ride with us tonight," he said. "Criminal damage and battery, probably. The DA decides the rest. You want to press charges?"

"Yes."

Out on the porch, Dom heard that. He twisted around on the steps.

"You're going to regret this, Karl! You hear me? Diane's going to take everything you've got!"

"Sir, stop talking," the other deputy said, and put him in the back of the cruiser.

Mrs. Ortega was still standing at the edge of my lawn in her bathrobe. She waited until the car pulled away, and then she walked over and looked at my storm door.

"I always thought he was a jerk," she said. "Ever since he backed over my mailbox in 2019 and told me it was already leaning."

"It wasn't leaning."

"I know it wasn't leaning, Karl." She looked at my jaw. "Put some frozen peas on that. And whatever that man said about forty thousand dollars, you go find out."

Hilary Shields has been my lawyer for fifteen years, mostly for clients of mine who suddenly need one. She does divorce work the way a good surgeon takes out an appendix. Quick, quiet, and nothing gets left inside that's going to rot.

She looked at my jaw when I came into her office Monday morning and didn't say a word about it. She just pushed a box of tissues toward me, which I didn't need, and picked up the divorce petition Diane had thrown at me.

"Who drafted this?" she said.

"A lawyer in De Pere. Name on the bottom."

She read it the way I read a client's shoebox of receipts. Fast and with a little bit of contempt.

"She wants the house, half of your firm, and maintenance for life," Hilary said. "Wisconsin is a marital property state. That means almost everything you earned during the marriage is presumed to be half hers, and half yours. So she'll get half of most things. Not the house and half. Half."

"And the maintenance?"

"Maintenance is what Wisconsin calls alimony. She works, Karl. She's an office manager at a dental practice. She makes fifty-eight thousand a year. Nobody's paying her for life." Hilary set the petition down. "Now. Infidelity."

"Does it matter?"

"Wisconsin is no-fault. A judge doesn't care who slept with whom." She held up one finger. "But a judge cares very much if marital money was spent on it. Every motel room, every dinner, every gift she bought another man out of joint funds, we can ask to have counted against her share. You told me on the phone there are motel charges."

I handed her my folder. The joint Visa statements. The Bay Motor Inn, out on Military Avenue, eleven charges since March, mostly on Tuesday afternoons when Diane told me she was at the dentist's continuing education class. Eighty-nine dollars a night.

"Nine hundred and seventy-nine dollars," I said. "Plus two dinners at the Wild Goose. Plus a set of golf grips from Dick's Sporting Goods, which is a funny thing for my wife to buy, since she doesn't golf."

"Dom golfs."

"Dom golfs badly."

Hilary wrote something down.

"There's one more thing," I said. "Dom said something on my hallway floor Saturday night. About Kenny Stokes and forty thousand dollars."

She stopped writing.

"Do you know what he meant?"

"No. But the green calls on the phone bill stopped two years ago, so whatever it was, it's been sitting a while."

Hilary took her glasses off and looked at me.

"Karl. If your wife took forty thousand dollars from another man and it went into an account in her name, or your joint account, or a business, I need to know where it is before her lawyer does. Because if it shows up as a debt you're both on, it's half yours."

"I'm on top of it."

"Are you?"

My phone buzzed in my coat. I looked at the screen.

It was Marla Stokes.

"Karl, it's Marla. I said I'd call. I've known since August. Meet me at the Pancake Place on Main at seven tomorrow. Come alone. Bring that phone bill."

Marla Stokes is fifty-two, teaches seventh-grade math at Franklin Middle School, and wears reading glasses on a beaded chain around her neck like a librarian in a movie. She was already in a back booth when I got to the Pancake Place, with a cup of coffee, a short stack she wasn't eating, and a blue binder thick enough to stop a door.

"You knew," I said, sliding in across from her.

"I told you I knew."

"You sat there Sunday and let her do that whole speech."

"I wanted to see how far she'd go." She took a sip of coffee. "And I wanted to see what you had. Because I didn't know about Trish's husband, Karl. I only knew about mine."

She opened the binder.

"In August I found a HELOC statement in Kenny's truck," she said. "A home equity line of credit. On our house. In both our names, which is funny, because I never signed for anything. He forged my signature on the draw request, and he pulled forty thousand dollars out of our house in May of 2024."

"And it went to Diane."

"It went to a business account. Bloom and Thread, LLC." She turned the page. "Does that name mean anything to you?"

It did. Two years ago, Diane told me she wanted to open a little gift boutique downtown. Candles and baby clothes and those wooden signs that say Live Laugh Love. I'd run the numbers on her lease and told her she'd lose money in eighteen months. We had a fight about it that lasted a week. Then she stopped talking about it.

"She told me she dropped it," I said.

"She didn't drop it. She registered it. Then she got the money from Kenny." Marla slid a sheet across the table. "I hired a private investigator in September. His name is Pete Lyons, and he used to be a Brown County detective, and he's very boring and very good. He found the account. Forty thousand in. Thirty-one thousand out, to a deposit on a retail space on Broadway, and a down payment on a lease for a 2025 Lexus RX."

"The Lexus she told me her sister lent her."

"Her sister drives a Corolla, Karl."

I laughed out loud. I couldn't help it. Two years of my wife parking a Lexus in my driveway and telling me it was Becky's, and I'd believed it because Diane said it with a straight face.

"And here's the rest," Marla said, and she didn't laugh. "The boutique fell through. The space on Broadway went to a vape shop. And Kenny never got his money back. He's been making payments on that HELOC out of his own paycheck for two years so I wouldn't notice. Nine thousand left in that account. That's all."

"Why didn't you go to Kenny with this?"

"Because Kenny lies the way other people breathe." She closed the binder. "And because I wanted to do this right. I've got a lawyer. I've got the PI. I've got the forged signature. And now you've got the phone bill that puts the calls right in the middle of the money." She looked at me over her glasses. "And Dom thinks Diane's still got that forty thousand. Doesn't he?"

"He said he never saw it."

"So he thinks she's sitting on it. And he thinks if he marries her, he gets it." Marla smiled for the first time. It wasn't a warm smile. "Dom's business is in Trish's name, you know. Gallo Plumbing and Heating. Her daddy set it up that way in 1998 so Dom couldn't drink it away. If Trish files, Dom walks out with his truck and his tools and not one dime of the company. He needs Diane's money a lot more than she needs his."

I sat back in the booth.

"So Dom's counting on Kenny's forty grand," I said. "And Kenny doesn't know Dom exists."

"Kenny thinks Diane dumped him because she felt guilty about you."

"And Diane thinks Dom's leaving Trish for love."

"Diane thinks a lot of things."

We looked at each other across the syrup bottles. You know that moment when two people who never had much to say to each other suddenly realize they're on the same side of something? It was like that.

"I'm not going to lie to anybody," I said. "I'm not going to plant anything, or fake anything, or call anybody's boss."

"I'm not asking you to."

"But if Kenny happened to learn one true thing about Dom," I said, "and Dom happened to learn one true thing about that money..."

"Then they'd do the rest themselves," Marla said. "Men like that always do."

She slid the binder across the table to me.

"It's my 50th birthday Saturday," she said. "At the VFW hall on Riverside. Trish booked it in June, before any of this. Seventy people, a DJ, a sheet cake. Everyone on the block is coming. Diane RSVP'd yes."

"Diane's still going to your birthday?"

"Diane thinks she still has friends." Marla stood up and put a ten on the table. "Read the binder. And Karl? Open Gwen's envelope with her. She can't do it alone."

My phone buzzed before I got to my truck. It was Gwen.

"I'm ready. Bring the envelope. Please come now."

Gwen Ferraro lives in a little ranch house on Sunnyview Drive with a vegetable garden she keeps going into November out of pure stubbornness. She opened the door before I knocked. Her eyes were red. She'd already made coffee for two.

We sat at her kitchen table, and I set the envelope down between us, and for a long time neither of us touched it.

"I blamed myself," Gwen said. "For four years. Russ said I'd gotten boring. He said I'd let myself go after my mother died. He said there wasn't anybody else, he just didn't love me anymore. And I believed him, Karl. I went to a counselor for a year and a half to figure out what I'd done."

"You didn't do anything."

"Then show me."

I opened the envelope. There wasn't much in it. That's the thing about the truth. It doesn't usually need a lot of paper.

There was the joint Visa statement from October of 2021. Three charges at the Bay Motor Inn, eighty-one dollars each, on Tuesday afternoons. There was the postcard Gwen had mailed us from Clearwater, Florida, with the postmark on it. And there was a photo I took on my phone and printed out at Walgreens four years ago, because something about it bothered me and I didn't know what yet.

It was Russ Ferraro's blue Ram, parked in my driveway at 3:14 on a Tuesday afternoon, the week Gwen was in Florida.

Gwen picked up the photo. She held it for a long time.

"Why didn't you tell me?" she said.

"Because when I asked Diane, she said Russ dropped off your casserole dish. And I wanted to believe her more than I wanted to know." I looked at the table. "And then you two split up a month later, and I told myself it was over, and it was none of my business. That was a coward's choice. I'm sorry, Gwen."

She set the photo down. Then she did something I didn't expect. She reached across the table and squeezed my hand, hard, once.

"It's not on you," she said. "It's on her. And it's on him." She wiped her eyes with a paper napkin. "Russ lives in Appleton now. Remarried. He tells everybody I was the one who ruined it."

"Not anymore."

"No," Gwen said. "Not anymore."

When I pulled into my own driveway an hour later, Diane's Lexus was there. Becky's Lexus, I mean. She was sitting at our kitchen island with a glass of wine, her suitcase by the back door, and a look on her face like she'd been rehearsing.

"Dom's out on bond," she said. "Because of you."

"Because of his fist."

"You set him up. You knew he'd come over."

"I asked him not to come over. You did too. I heard you."

She stood up. Round two, I figured. Round one was denial. Round two is always blame.

"Do you know what it's like being married to you?" she said. "It's like being married to a spreadsheet. Every January to April, you vanish. You come home at ten, you eat standing up, you fall asleep. For twenty-seven years I've been alone half the year."

"Tax season paid for the hot tub."

"I don't care about the hot tub!"

"You cared enough to put it on the deck where the neighbors could see it."

"You're cold, Karl. You've always been cold. You couldn't even get angry when I told you about Dom. You just stood there with your little phone bill."

"I'm angry now."

"Then show it!"

"I am," I said. "This is what it looks like."

She threw her wine glass in the sink. It didn't break. It was one of the plastic ones from the deck. It bounced and spun and rattled to a stop, and somehow that made her angrier.

"I'll tell you what's going to happen," she said. "Dom's going to leave Trish. We're going to get married. And when the judge sees what kind of man you are, with your cameras and your phone bills and your little notes, I'm going to walk away with this house."

"Where's Kenny's forty thousand dollars, Diane?"

That stopped her. She stood at the kitchen island with her hand on her suitcase and her mouth a little open, and I watched her try to figure out how much I knew.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

"Bloom and Thread, LLC."

Diane went very still. Then she picked up her suitcase, walked out the back door, and got into the Lexus that wasn't Becky's.

Thirty seconds later my phone buzzed. A text. Not from Diane.

From a number I'd seen on my phone bill in green highlighter, ninety-four times.

"This is Kenny Stokes. Marla gave me your number. She says you know about the money. I need to talk to you before Dom does. Tomorrow. Please."

Kenny Stokes runs a State Farm agency in a strip of offices on Lineville Road, between a nail salon and a chiropractor. He's fifty-four, soft around the middle, and has the kind of handshake that apologizes for itself. He locked the front door behind me and flipped the sign to Closed, even though it was ten in the morning on a Wednesday.

"Marla told me everything last night," he said. "The PI. The binder. My signature on the draw."

"Her signature."

"Yeah." He rubbed his face. "Her signature. That I wrote."

We sat in his office with a framed photo of him and Marla on a cruise ship on the desk between us. He kept looking at it.

"I want to say I'm sorry, Karl," he said. "About Diane. It was stupid. It was six months, and I knew it was stupid the whole time."

"Why'd you give her forty thousand dollars?"

"Because she cried." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "She said you'd crushed her dream. She said you sat at the kitchen table with your calculator and told her the boutique would fail. She said if she just had a little seed money, she could prove you wrong, and then she'd leave you, and we'd..." He stopped. "And then the week I gave it to her, she ended it. She said the guilt was eating her alive. She said she couldn't do that to you."

"She ended it the week you had your hernia surgery."

"Yeah. How'd you know that?"

"The phone bill."

Kenny looked at me for a while.

"So it wasn't guilt," he said.

"I'm going to tell you one thing, Kenny. Just one. It's true, and you can check it, and then I'm going to leave and you can do whatever you want with it." I leaned forward. "Diane's been with Dom Gallo since March. And Dom's planning to marry her, because he thinks she's still sitting on your forty thousand dollars."

Kenny's face went through about four different things. Surprise, then hurt, then something mean that I hadn't thought he had in him.

"Dom Gallo," he said. "That loudmouth? He sold me a water heater in 2020 that leaked in a month and he wouldn't take it back."

"That's the one."

"He thinks he's getting my money."

"I didn't say that. I said that's what he thinks."

Kenny stood up and went to the window and looked out at the parking lot for a long time. When he turned around, his face was red.

"Is he going to be at Marla's party Saturday?"

"Trish booked the hall. Diane said she's coming. I don't know about Dom. He's got a no-contact order with me. Judge set it at his bond hearing."

"Marla's still letting me come," Kenny said. "Did you know that? She hasn't filed yet. She said she wants me there." He picked up the cruise photo. "I think she's going to do something."

"I think so too."

"Do you know what?"

"No," I said, and that was the truth. "But I'll be there."

The VFW hall on Riverside Drive smelled like fryer oil and floor wax, the way every VFW in Wisconsin has smelled since 1946. Somebody had hung a banner that said "Fabulous at 50, Marla!" in pink glitter, and there was a sheet cake on the long table by the bar big enough to feed a softball team, and the DJ was playing "Don't Stop Believin'" for at least the second time that night.

Seventy people, give or take. Half the block. Marla's whole teaching staff from Franklin Middle School. Gwen, in a green dress, standing by the wall with a glass of white wine and the Clearwater postcard in her purse. Trish, working the door like she owned the place, which, since she was the one who booked it, she kind of did.

I came in a little after eight. A dozen heads turned. In a town the size of Green Bay, a phone bill gets around.

"Karl," Trish said at the door. She touched my jaw with one finger where the bruise was still yellow. "Dom did that?"

"He did."

"He's done worse to the drywall in our basement." She handed me a drink ticket. "He's not supposed to come within five hundred feet of you, right?"

"That's what the judge said."

"Good," Trish said. "Because he's in the parking lot."

Before I could ask what she meant, the front doors opened again, and Diane walked in on Dom Gallo's arm.

She was wearing a red dress I'd never seen before, and Dom had a bandage on his right hand and a sport coat that didn't close over his stomach. They walked in like a couple arriving at the Oscars. Every conversation in the room died one table at a time, like somebody was flipping off lights.

"Oh, she did not," Gwen said, loud enough for half the hall.

Diane looked around and found me by the bar. She smiled. It was a smile I'd seen on her a thousand times, mostly when she was about to win an argument with a waitress.

"Karl," she said, walking right up. "I didn't think you'd have the nerve."

"Marla invited me."

"Marla invited everybody." She tilted her head toward Dom, who had stopped about thirty feet back, near the DJ table, which was about four hundred and seventy feet closer to me than a judge had told him to be. "Dom and I are going to tell everybody tonight. About us. We're not going to hide anymore."

"At Marla's birthday."

"Everybody's here. Why not?"

That's when Kenny Stokes came out of the men's room.

He saw Dom. Dom saw him. Neither of them had ever been anything more than two guys who nodded at each other at the Fourth of July block party, and now they stood there in the middle of the VFW dance floor looking at each other like two roosters over one hen.

"Gallo," Kenny said.

"Stokes," Dom said. "Heard your wife's finally getting rid of you."

"Heard yours already did." Kenny walked straight across the floor. The DJ, who could read a room, turned the music down. "Heard you're marrying Diane for my forty grand."

Dom laughed. "Your forty grand?"

"Bloom and Thread, LLC," Kenny said. "That was my money. Out of my house. And there's nine thousand dollars left of it, genius. She spent the rest on a vape shop that never opened and that Lexus out in the parking lot."

Dom stopped laughing. He turned around slowly and looked at Diane.

"You told me there was sixty in that account," he said.

"Dom..."

"You told me sixty. You said Karl doesn't know about it. You said once we're married..."

"Honey, can we talk about this outside?"

"Nine thousand dollars?" Dom's voice went up so high it cracked. "I left my wife for nine thousand dollars?"

"I threw you out," Trish called from the door. "Let's keep the record straight, Dominic."

That got a laugh from the teachers' table. Dom spun toward the door.

"And you can keep your stupid company!"

"Oh, I'm keeping it." Trish walked toward him across the dance floor, holding up a folded sheet of paper like it was a winning lottery ticket. "Gallo Plumbing and Heating, Incorporated. Owned one hundred percent by Patricia Gallo since 1998. My daddy set it up that way because he knew you'd drink it away. I filed Monday, Dom. You get your truck. The van's mine. The shop's mine. The customers are mine."

"You can't..."

"My lawyer says I can. And my lawyer's very good."

Dom looked from Trish to Diane to Kenny, and I watched him do the math, and for once in his life Dom Gallo got a number right. He had nothing. No company, no money, no wife, and the woman on his arm had nine thousand dollars and a leased Lexus.

So he did what Dom always does when the numbers don't come out his way. He swung.

He swung at Kenny, because Kenny was closest. Kenny ducked, which surprised everybody, Kenny most of all, and Dom's fist hit nothing but air and the momentum carried him straight into the cake table. Seventy servings of buttercream went over sideways onto the VFW linoleum. "Fabulous at 50" landed face down.

Kenny jumped on his back. They went down together into the frosting, rolling and grunting and calling each other names I can't repeat. Somebody screamed. Somebody else started filming. The DJ, God bless him, put on "Another One Bites the Dust."

Then Dom shoved Kenny off, got to his knees covered in pink frosting, and saw me.

He came at me across the dance floor with his bandaged hand up and murder on his face, and I'll be honest, I took one step back. That's all I had time for.

Then Walt Brennan, who's tended bar at that VFW for twenty-two years and did two tours in Vietnam, came over the top of the bar like a man half his age and wrapped Dom in a bear hug from behind.

"Not in my hall, son," Walt said. "Not in my hall."

Dom thrashed. Walt held on. Two of Marla's teacher friends, a gym teacher named Coach Alvarado and a shop teacher whose name I never learned, came over and sat on Dom's legs. And three minutes later, the doors opened, and Deputy Ruiz walked in with his hand on his belt.

He looked at the frosting. He looked at Dom. Then he looked at me, standing by the bar, about twelve feet from the man with the no-contact order.

"Mr. Daniels," he said. "We meet again."

"Deputy."

Ruiz turned to Dom, who was still pinned on the floor with buttercream in his hair.

"Mr. Gallo," he said, "your bond conditions say five hundred feet from Mr. Daniels. You want to tell me how far you are right now?"

"He came to my party!" Dom yelled. "He set me up!"

"It's not your party, Dom," Marla said. "It's mine."

She'd been standing by the head table the whole time. She hadn't moved through any of it. Now she walked over in her pink dress with a manila envelope in her hand, stepped around the cake, and stopped in front of her husband, who was still sitting on the floor, his shirt torn, breathing hard.

"Happy birthday to me," Marla said. She dropped the envelope in Kenny's lap. "Those are divorce papers, Kenny. And underneath those is a copy of the police report I filed this afternoon, about the signature on our HELOC. Pete says the detective will call you Monday."

Kenny looked up at her.

"Marla. Babe. Not here."

"Here," Marla said. "You signed my name where everybody could see it. I'm just returning the favor."

Behind me, Deputy Ruiz had Dom on his feet, cuffed, and was walking him toward the door. Dom twisted around one more time and found Diane in the crowd.

"This is your fault!" he shouted. "You and your nine thousand dollars! I'm not marrying you! You hear me? I wouldn't marry you if you were the last woman in Brown County!"

Then the doors shut behind him.

Seventy people turned to look at Diane.

She was standing alone by the DJ table in her red dress. Nobody was near her. Not Trish, not Marla, not her sister, who'd come with Gil and was suddenly very interested in the coat rack.

Gwen walked over to her. Calmly. She opened her purse and took out a postcard.

"Clearwater, Florida," Gwen said. "October, 2021. I wrote it to you and Karl. You know what's funny, Diane? I wrote, 'Wish you were here.' And all that time, Russ was at your house." She laid the postcard on the DJ table in front of Diane. "Four years I paid a counselor to find out what was wrong with me. You could've just told me."

"Gwen, it wasn't like that..."

"It was exactly like that. Karl had a picture."

Diane looked at the postcard. Then she looked across the room at me. Her mascara was starting to go, and I'll admit, for a second, I felt something. Twenty-seven years is twenty-seven years.

Then she walked over to me, through the frosting, and she stopped about two feet away, close enough that I could smell her perfume. The good one. Ninety-two dollars at Macy's. Page eleven of the last Visa statement.

"You did this, Karl," she said, loud, so the room could hear. "You did all of this. You and your stupid phone bill. You wanted to ruin me in front of everyone."

The room waited. Seventy people and a DJ. Walt behind the bar with a bar rag over his shoulder. Marla and Trish and Gwen.

I reached into my back pocket, took out the folded phone bill, and laid it on the bar between us, yellow lines and green lines and all.

"No, Diane," I said. "I just paid it."
