"Oh my Lord, y'all, what is all this?"

My girlfriend said it walking into the back room of Rocky's Seafood on her forty-seventh birthday, with a gold paper crown on her head that the hostess had put there, and thirty-one people in that room went dead quiet at once.

Then she saw us.

Five men, standing shoulder to shoulder in front of the buffet table, in a row, like a police lineup at a church picnic. Me. Curtis. Bobby. Pete. And Jody on the end.

Five men she'd been dating at the same time for the better part of a year.

I watched her eyes go down the line. Left to right. One face at a time. Her smile stayed exactly where it was, frozen on, but her eyes went from happy to confused to something else, like a woman counting the change in her hand and coming up short.

"Happy birthday, Michelle," I said.

I picked up the cake from the table behind me and held it out in front of me with both hands. Sheet cake, white frosting, pink roses at the corners. Across the middle, in big pink cursive letters, it said:

HAPPY BIRTHDAY TO OUR GIRLFRIEND.

Somebody in the room laughed, one high nervous laugh, and then stopped.

Curtis Abshire, beside me, folded his arms. Pete Savoie took off his reading glasses and put them in his shirt pocket. Bobby Duhon, who's a big man, just grinned.

Michelle's paper crown slid an inch to the left. She didn't fix it.

The cake was the small thing. I'd paid thirty-eight dollars for it at the Market Basket bakery. The big thing was the gray-haired woman sitting by herself at the little table in the back corner of that room, with a plate of boiled crawfish and a glass of sweet tea, who Michelle hadn't noticed yet.

My name is Antonio Serrano. I'm fifty-four years old, and I own Serrano Jewelry on Ryan Street in Lake Charles, Louisiana. My daddy opened it in 1971, and I've been at that bench since I was sixteen, sizing rings, fixing clasps, putting new batteries in watches for people who could've done it themselves with a butter knife. Forty years of looking at gold through a loupe teaches you one thing above all others. Everybody thinks the inside of a ring is private. It isn't. People write the truth on the inside of jewelry, where they think nobody will ever look.

Remember that.

Because about a month before that party, Michelle LeBlanc walked into my shop and handed me the inside of a bracelet.

It was a Monday in April, a little before closing. My niece Gabby was at the front counter doing something on her phone that she swore was "the store's social media." I was in the back at the bench, putting a new crystal on a Bulova.

"Uncle Tony," Gabby called. "Your girlfriend's here."

Michelle came around the counter and kissed me on the cheek. She smelled like the gardenia lotion I'd bought her for Christmas. She was forty-six then, a medical receptionist at a clinic over on Lake Street, with long dark hair and a big laugh, and she hummed when she ate, little happy sounds, like the food was telling her a joke. I'd been seeing her for eleven months. I thought I was going to marry her.

"Baby, I need a favor," she said, and set a gold bracelet on my bench. "This was my grandmother's. The clasp's loose, and it slides off my wrist. Can you take a link out?"

"For you? I'll take two out."

She laughed and kissed me again, and said she'd come back Wednesday, because Monday and Wednesday nights were our nights. Tuesdays and Thursdays she worked late at the clinic, Fridays she was always worn out, and every weekend she drove over to Sulphur to take care of her mama, who was sick with kidney failure and on dialysis three times a week.

I'd given her four thousand dollars toward her mama's medical bills in February. I didn't mind. That's what you do.

After she left, I picked up the bracelet and turned it under the lamp.

It was nice. Fourteen-karat yellow gold, a curb link, maybe two years old by the wear. Not a grandmother's bracelet. A grandmother's bracelet has worn spots in different places, rubbed smooth over decades. This one barely had a scratch on it.

I didn't think much of that. People get confused about where jewelry came from.

Then I took my loupe and looked at the inside of the clasp plate, because that's what I always do, out of habit, the way a mechanic always listens to the engine before he opens the hood.

There was an engraving. Very small, very neat, done by machine.

M. You're my Tuesday and Thursday. Love, C.

I set the bracelet down on the felt.

I sat there long enough that Gabby came to the door of the back room.

"Uncle Tony? You okay? You look like you saw a ghost."

"Gabby," I said. "Who does machine engraving in this town? The small script, like this?"

She came over and leaned down and squinted at the clasp under the lamp. Then she read it. Then she straightened up very slowly.

"Uncle Tony."

"I know."

"Tuesday and Thursday."

"I know, Gabby."

She chewed her lip. Gabby is twenty-four, my sister Rosalinda's girl, and she's been working my counter since she dropped out of McNeese, and she's smarter than both of us put together.

"Gautreaux's," she said. "Gautreaux Trophy and Engraving, over on Broad Street. That's their font. They did the plaques for my softball team. It's the only place in town that has that little curly C."

The engraving machine on that bracelet had a little curly C.

I called Leroy Gautreaux the next morning. I've known Leroy for thirty years. His daddy and my daddy used to play bourre on Friday nights in the back of my shop, and we'd sit under the table and steal pralines.

"Antonio! What can I do for you, hoss?"

"I need a favor, and it's not a normal one."

I told him what was on the bracelet. I told him it might have come through his shop maybe two years back. I asked him if he kept records.

"I keep everything," Leroy said. "My wife says I'm a hoarder. Hang on." I heard him put the phone down. I heard drawers open and shut. Five minutes went by.

"Got it," he said, coming back. "March, two years ago. Bracelet engraving, script font, 'You're my Tuesday and Thursday, love C.' Customer paid cash, but he left a phone number for pickup." He paused. "Antonio, what's this about?"

"I think the bracelet was a gift to my girlfriend from another man."

The line went quiet for a second.

"Well, that's rough, hoss," Leroy said. "His name's Curtis Abshire. You want the number?"

I met Curtis Abshire on Wednesday morning at the Pujo Street Cafe downtown. I'd called him the night before and told him only that I had a piece of jewelry of his and some questions about it, and he said, in a deep, careful voice, "I'll be there at nine."

He was already in a booth when I walked in. Fifty-eight, bald, broad as a refrigerator, with a gray mustache and a Westlake Rams cap and forearms covered in old burn scars. Thirty-one years at the Citgo refinery, he told me later. Retired now.

I sat down across from him. I set the bracelet on the table between us on a white handkerchief.

He looked at it for a long time.

"Where'd you get that?" he said.

"She brought it to me Monday to have a link taken out. She said it was her grandmother's."

His jaw worked under the mustache.

"I gave her that bracelet two years ago, on a Thursday," he said. "Her birthday. At the Olive Garden on Nelson Road. She cried."

"She cries good," I said.

He looked up at me. And then, God help us both, he laughed. One short laugh, like a cough. And I laughed too. Two grown men laughing in a booth at nine in the morning over a fourteen-karat bracelet, while the waitress came over and poured coffee and looked at us like we'd lost our minds.

"How long you been with her?" Curtis asked.

"Eleven months. Mondays and Wednesdays."

"Two years," he said. "Tuesdays and Thursdays." He rubbed his face with both hands. "She told me Mondays and Wednesdays she does Bible study and her nails."

"She told me Tuesdays and Thursdays she works late at the clinic."

"And weekends?"

"Her mama's. In Sulphur. Kidney failure."

Curtis set his coffee cup down very carefully.

"How much did you give her for her mama?" he asked.

"Four thousand. In February."

"I gave her nine." He said it flat, the way you'd read a number off a gauge. "Three thousand last summer for dialysis. Six thousand at Christmas, for a kidney transplant deposit. She showed me a letter from the hospital in Houston. On letterhead."

"Did you call the hospital?"

"Why would I call the hospital? She's my girlfriend. Her mama's dying."

We sat there and looked at the bracelet.

"So she's got two of us," Curtis said finally.

I looked at the calendar in my head. Monday, Wednesday, me. Tuesday, Thursday, Curtis. That left Friday, when she was always "worn out." And every single weekend, when she was always "in Sulphur."

"Curtis," I said. "I don't think it's two."

It took us one week to find the other two.

Bobby Duhon was Gabby's doing. She went through every photo on Michelle's Facebook page, which was mostly sunsets and Bible verses and pictures of crawfish, and in a photo from last Mardi Gras she found Michelle at a parade on Ryan Street with her arm around a big sunburned man in a captain's hat. The caption said, "Me and my cousin Bobby!" with three hearts.

"Uncle Tony," Gabby said, holding her phone up across the counter, "nobody puts three hearts on their cousin."

Gabby clicked his name. Bobby Duhon. Captain, Gulf Coast Marine Transport. Two weeks on a crew boat out of Cameron, two weeks home. Single. His profile picture was him holding a redfish nearly as long as his leg.

"Two weeks on, two weeks off," Curtis said, when I called him. "That's her weekends. Every other one, anyway."

"And the other weekends?"

That one, I found myself.

Michelle's car was a white Nissan Altima with a pink rosary hanging from the mirror and a dent in the back bumper from where she'd backed into a mailbox at Christmas. Sunday morning, instead of going to Mass, I drove around the churches. I'm not proud of it. It's not a nice way to spend a Sunday. But Lake Charles is not a big town, and there are only so many churches with big parking lots, and at First Methodist on Broad Street, a little after ten, I found a white Altima with a pink rosary and a dent in the back.

I sat in my truck across the street until the service let out.

She came down the front steps holding the arm of a tall, thin, white-haired man in a gray suit. He was laughing at something she said. She was humming. I could tell from across the street, from the way her shoulders moved.

He opened the passenger door of a Buick for her, and she got in, and he drove her away, and her Altima sat there in the church lot like she'd be back for it later.

The church bulletin was in a rack by the door, and I took one. On the back page, under "Thank Yous," it said: "The Savoie family thanks Deacon Pete Savoie for his generous gift to the building fund in memory of his late wife, Arlette."

Deacon Pete Savoie. Sixty-one. Widower. His wife had passed three years before.

I went home and put the bulletin on my kitchen table and looked at it for a long time.

On Tuesday night, the eighth day after the bracelet, four men sat around the workbench in the back of Serrano Jewelry after closing, with the blinds down and a box of boudin from Hackett's going cold in the middle of the bench.

Me. Curtis. Bobby, who'd come in off the boat two days early and still smelled like diesel. And Pete, in a pressed white shirt, who'd driven over straight from Tuesday night choir practice and sat with his hands folded on his knees like he was waiting for a hymn to start.

Pete had been the hardest call to make. When I told him on the phone who I was and why I was calling, he didn't say anything for so long that I thought he'd hung up.

Then he said, "Mr. Serrano, I've been praying for an answer about that woman for a month. I just didn't expect it to call me."

Now Gabby sat at the end of the bench with a legal pad, because she said if we were going to do this, somebody had to take notes, and it wasn't going to be four men eating boudin.

"Okay," she said. "Let's do the money."

We went around the bench.

"Four thousand," I said. "February. For dialysis."

"Nine," Curtis said. "Three last summer, six at Christmas for the transplant deposit. Houston Methodist letter."

Bobby rubbed the back of his sunburned neck. "Twelve," he said. "Eight thousand in October for the transplant. Four more in January because the insurance fell through." He looked around at us. "She showed me a letter from Houston Methodist too."

"Same letter?" Curtis asked.

Bobby pulled out his phone and found the picture. Curtis pulled out his. They held them side by side. Same letterhead. Same doctor's name. Same patient number. Only the dollar amounts were different.

"Son of a witch," Bobby said.

Pete cleared his throat.

"I wrote her a check for sixteen thousand seven hundred dollars," he said quietly. "In March. For the transplant. She said her mother was second on the list. She said she was afraid of losing her." He looked down at his hands. "My Arlette died of kidney disease. I told Michelle about that on our first date. I told her everything about it."

Nobody said anything for a minute. Gabby put down her pen.

"Forty-one thousand seven hundred dollars," Gabby said finally. "Between the four of you. For one kidney."

"She'd better have a good kidney," Bobby said, and Curtis snorted, and even Pete smiled a little.

That's when somebody knocked on the back door of the shop.

We all froze. Gabby looked at me. I got up and went to the door and looked through the peephole.

A man about forty-five stood in the alley under the light. Medium height, sandy hair, a nice polo shirt, khakis, boat shoes. He had his hands in his pockets.

I opened the door a crack.

"Help you?"

"Antonio Serrano?" He had a friendly, easy voice. "I'm Jody Reeves. I'm sorry to just show up like this. Curtis Abshire told me to come by."

I looked back at Curtis. Curtis stood up.

"That's right," Curtis said. "He called me this afternoon. I forgot to tell y'all. He said he found my number written on a slip of paper in Michelle's purse."

"Right," Jody said. "In her purse. When she was in the bathroom at Cotton's." He came in and stood by the bench, looking around at all of us. "Wow. Okay. So it's true. I'm Fridays. And sometimes Saturdays, when she's not 'at her mama's.'" He laughed a little. "Guess I'm not that special."

"Nobody here is," Bobby said.

We pulled up a stool for Jody. He sat down, and took a boudin link, and told his story. He sold boats out at a marina on Prien Lake. He'd been seeing Michelle about eight months. She'd told him Mondays through Thursdays she worked late shifts at the clinic. He'd found Curtis's number on a slip in her purse with "C, Tue/Thu" written on it, and called it, and Curtis told him everything.

"How much did you give her?" Gabby asked, pen ready.

"Seven thousand," Jody said. "For her mama's kidney."

Gabby wrote it down. Forty-eight thousand seven hundred.

"So," Jody said, leaning forward with his elbows on the bench. "What are we going to do?"

"Go to the police," Pete said right away. "This is theft. It's fraud. She lied about a dying woman to get money out of grieving men."

"Whoa, whoa." Jody held up both hands. "Pastor—"

"Deacon."

"Deacon. With respect. You go to the police with this, you know what happens? She lawyers up. They tell you it was gifts. You gave a grown woman money because you loved her, that's not a crime, that's a bad decision. You never see a dime." He looked around at all of us. "And all five of us end up in the Lake Charles American Press as the old fools who got taken by the same woman. You want that? You want your congregation reading that?"

Pete's mouth tightened. He didn't answer.

"So what do you want to do?" Curtis asked.

Jody grinned. "Embarrass her. Big. Her birthday's in three weeks, right? The twenty-third? We throw her a surprise party. All five of us, standing there together when she walks in. Her friends, her coworkers. We let her see us. Then, real calm, we tell her she's got thirty days to pay us back, or we tell everybody in town. She'll pay. Women like that always pay to keep it quiet."

Bobby slapped the bench. "I like it."

"Cake," Gabby said, writing. "There has to be a cake."

"Happy birthday to our girlfriend," Curtis said, deadpan, and everybody laughed.

Everybody but me. I was looking at Jody Reeves.

Because about ten minutes earlier, when he'd come in, he'd said she told him she was "at her mama's" on Saturdays. And a minute after that, while he was talking about the boats, he'd said something about how it was a long drive to Sulphur every weekend for a sick woman.

Nobody at that bench had said the word Sulphur since he walked in. Curtis knew Sulphur, because I'd said it to him myself at the Pujo Street Cafe a week before. But Curtis said Jody had called him that afternoon and they'd talked about the bracelet and the money. Not where her mama lived.

Maybe Michelle told him. Maybe Michelle told everybody the same lie. She was a woman with a system.

I let it go. For then.

"Okay," I said. "A party. My shop pays for the cake."

Jody reached across the bench to shake on it, and when he did, his left hand came up into the light of my work lamp. There was a pale stripe of skin on his ring finger. Faint. The kind of stripe a man gets when he's worn a ring every day for years in the Louisiana sun and only takes it off sometimes.

I've been looking at people's hands for forty years.

Remember that.

On Saturday afternoon, four days later, Michelle came into the shop to pick up her bracelet.

She came in through the front door with the bell ringing, wearing a yellow sundress and sunglasses on top of her head, and Gabby at the counter went stiff as a board. Gabby has never in her life been able to lie with her face.

"Hey, sweetie!" Michelle said.

"Hey, Miss Michelle," Gabby said, in a voice like a robot.

I came out of the back fast, wiping my hands on a rag.

"There's my girl."

"There's my man." She kissed me over the counter. She was humming a little, some Zydeco tune. "Is my grandmother's bracelet ready?"

"All done." I got it out of the drawer in its little envelope, and laid it on a velvet pad, and fastened it on her wrist myself. My fingers didn't shake. I'd practiced. "One link out. Should sit right now."

She held her arm up and turned it in the light. The engraving was on the inside of the clasp, against her skin, where nobody would ever see it.

"It's perfect. You're perfect." She leaned on the counter. "Baby, can I ask you something? And you can say no."

"Ask."

"The hospital called. Mama's moved up on the list. She's first now. They need a deposit before they'll schedule her. Twenty-five hundred. By Friday." Her eyes filled up, right on cue. "I hate asking. I hate it. You've already done so much."

"Of course," I said.

She threw her arms around my neck over the counter.

"But this time," I said, into her hair, "let me pay the hospital directly. I'll call Houston Methodist on Monday, get the billing office, and put it on my card. That way it goes right to her account and you don't have to worry about a thing."

She pulled back. Just a few inches. Just for a second. Her face went still, the way a cat goes still when it hears a can opener in the wrong room.

Then she smiled.

"Oh, baby, that's sweet, but you can't," she said. "They won't talk to you. HIPAA. You're not family. They'll only talk to me." She squeezed my hand. "Just give it to me and I'll take it straight over. Cash is easiest. They give me a receipt."

"Cash it is," I said. "I'll have it for you Wednesday."

"You're my angel," she said, and kissed me, and went to the door.

Then she stopped in the doorway, with the bell over her head, and looked out at the street.

Curtis Abshire's blue Silverado was parked right across Ryan Street, in front of the bakery, where he'd parked that morning to bring me the copy of his Houston Methodist letter. Westlake Rams sticker on the back window. You couldn't miss it.

Michelle stood there with the door half open, looking at that truck.

"That's funny," she said slowly. "I know somebody with a truck just like that."

"Lot of blue Silverados in Calcasieu Parish," I said.

"Mm-hmm." She turned and looked at me over her shoulder. For a long second, she didn't smile at all. "See you Monday, baby."

The bell rang, and the door shut behind her.

Gabby let out a breath like she'd been underwater.

"Uncle Tony," she said. "I almost peed."

"I know, Gabby."

"She looked right at it."

"I know."

Gabby sat down on her stool, and picked up her phone, and started scrolling with her thumb, fast, the way she does when she's upset and doesn't know what else to do with her hands.

Then her thumb stopped.

"Uncle Tony," she said, in a different voice. "Come look at this."

"What?"

"Her mama." She turned the phone around. "I found her mama's Facebook. Yvette LeBlanc, Sulphur, Louisiana. She posted a video yesterday." Gabby swallowed. "She's line dancing."

Yvette LeBlanc taught a beginner line dancing class every Tuesday and Thursday morning at the VFW hall on Napoleon Street in Sulphur, eighteen miles west of my shop. Gabby found that on Facebook too. So on Tuesday morning, the fifteenth day since the bracelet, I put a closed sign on my door for the first time in eleven years and drove out there.

The hall smelled like floor wax and old coffee. A boom box on a folding table was playing "Boot Scootin' Boogie," and fourteen women in their sixties and seventies were going grapevine left, grapevine right, in a ragged line across the floor.

The woman in front calling the steps was small and wiry, with short silver hair, pink sneakers, and a voice like a drill sergeant at a church social.

"Kick, ball change! Come on, Darlene, you're dragging! Turn! And turn!"

She did not look like a woman on dialysis three times a week. She looked like a woman who could outrun me.

I waited by the coffee urn until the song ended and the ladies went to get water. Then I walked over.

"Mrs. LeBlanc?"

She looked me up and down. "If you're selling something, I already have a Medicare plan, and my Lord is Jesus."

"No, ma'am. My name's Antonio Serrano. I have a jewelry store in Lake Charles. I'm here about your daughter. Michelle."

Her face changed. Not much. Just a tightening around the mouth.

"What's she done now?"

"Ma'am, how are your kidneys?"

She stared at me like I'd asked about her underwear.

"My kidneys? My kidneys are fine. I had a checkup in January. Dr. Fruge says I've got the blood work of a fifty-year-old." She narrowed her eyes. "Why?"

I told her. I told her the whole thing, standing by the coffee urn in the VFW hall while the ladies drank their water and pretended not to listen. Dialysis three times a week. A transplant in Houston. Five men. Forty-eight thousand seven hundred dollars.

When I finished, Yvette LeBlanc sat down on a folding chair, slowly, and put her hand on her chest.

"I haven't spoken to my daughter in two years," she said. "Not since Christmas before last. She asked me to co-sign a car loan and I said no, and she said I was dead to her. Those were her words. 'You're dead to me, Mama.'" She laughed, short and bitter. "I guess she meant it. She's been killing me off a little at a time for money ever since."

"I'm sorry, ma'am."

"Don't you be sorry. You didn't raise her." She shook her head. "Her and that husband of hers. They'll take the paint off your house if you stand still."

The VFW hall got very quiet in my ears.

"Her husband," I said.

"Jody." She said it like a cuss word. "Jody Reeves. They've been married since 2011. Sells boats, or says he does. Out at a marina on Prien Lake. He's the brains of the two of them, which isn't saying much."

I didn't say anything for a while. One of the ladies came over and handed Yvette a paper cup of water, and Yvette drank half of it without taking her eyes off me.

"You've met him," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Last Tuesday," I said. "He came to a meeting of the boyfriends. He told us he was boyfriend number five. He's helping us plan a surprise party for her birthday."

Yvette LeBlanc looked at me for a long moment.

Then she threw her head back and laughed until she had to wipe her eyes with the hem of her T-shirt, and fourteen line-dancing ladies turned around to see what was so funny.

"Oh, he's good," she said, when she could breathe. "Oh, Lord, he's good. He's not planning a party, Mr. Serrano. He's planning a getaway. He's in your little club so he knows every move you make before you make it." She grabbed my wrist with a grip like a mechanic. "When's this party?"

"Her birthday. The twenty-third. Rocky's Seafood, the back room."

"Good. I'll be there."

"Ma'am, you don't have to—"

"Honey," she said, "I've been dead for two years. I'd like to go to a party."

Detective Toni Lejeune worked financial crimes for the Calcasieu Parish Sheriff's Office. She was fifty, with reading glasses pushed up in her hair and a desk covered in file folders, and when I sat down across from her on Thursday morning, with Curtis on one side of me and Pete on the other, she looked at the three of us like she'd seen this exact picture a hundred times.

"Let me guess," she said. "Her mother's sick."

"Her mother's line dancing," I said.

That got her attention.

We laid it all out. The bracelet. The four Houston Methodist letters, all with the same patient number and four different dollar amounts. Pete's canceled check for sixteen thousand seven hundred, with "Mama's transplant" in the memo line in Michelle's handwriting. Bobby's bank transfers, which he'd sent from his phone on the crew boat. Gabby's legal pad. And Yvette LeBlanc's phone number, with a note: Healthy. Willing to say so under oath.

Detective Lejeune read the letters twice. She called Houston Methodist while we sat there. She asked for the billing office, then for the transplant office, and she gave them the patient number from the letter, and then she listened for a while, and said "Thank you, ma'am," and hung up.

"That patient number doesn't exist," she said. "Neither does that doctor. Somebody built that letterhead on a home computer." She took her glasses out of her hair and put them on. "In Louisiana, Mr. Serrano, if you get money from somebody by lying to them about what it's for, that's theft by fraud. Over twenty-five thousand dollars, all told, that's a felony. And if you've got five victims who'll each sign a statement—"

"Four," I said. "We've got four."

She looked up. "You said five."

So I told her about Jody Reeves.

When I finished, she sat back in her chair and laughed, a short tired laugh, the same laugh Yvette had laughed, the laugh of women who've seen too much.

"The husband," she said. "Joined the victims' club. To steer it." She shook her head. "I've been doing this nineteen years and I've never had one do that. That's bold. That's real bold."

"He told us not to go to the police," Pete said. "He said it'd embarrass us."

"I bet he did." She pulled a fresh legal pad toward her. "Here's what I need. Statements from all four of you, today. Copies of every check and transfer. Mrs. LeBlanc's statement, which I'll take myself. And then I need the two of them in the same room, talking. Because if she says the wrong thing in front of witnesses, or he does, that's my case wrapped in a bow."

"How about a birthday party?" Curtis said. "With a cake?"

Detective Lejeune looked at the three of us.

"What time's the party?"

"Seven o'clock," I said.

"Then I'll be there at a quarter till. In plain clothes. I like crawfish." She pointed her pen at me. "And Mr. Serrano. Don't tell Jody Reeves I'm coming. Don't tell him anything you don't want her to know by supper."

That night, I called Jody.

"Hey, brother," he said, friendly as a puppy. "What's the word?"

"Party's set. Rocky's back room, Saturday. Gabby's handling the invitations. Her coworkers, her friends from the clinic, everybody."

"Beautiful. What time?"

"Eight o'clock," I said. "We want her to walk in at eight-fifteen, so get there by eight."

"Eight," he said. "Got it."

"And Jody? We decided you're right. No police. We're just going to embarrass her and give her thirty days to pay it back."

"Smart man," Jody said. "That's the smart play, Antonio. That's what I'd do."

I'm sure it was.

I hung up and looked at Gabby, who'd been listening at the end of the counter with her hand over her mouth.

"Seven o'clock," she whispered. "The real time's seven."

"The real time's seven," I said. "Tell everybody else seven. Tell Mrs. LeBlanc six-thirty, so she can get her a crawfish plate and a good seat."

So that's how we got to the back room of Rocky's Seafood on Saturday evening, the twenty-third of May, at a little after seven. Thirty-one people. Her coworkers from the clinic, her girlfriends from the gym, a few people from First Methodist that Pete had invited. A table full of boiled crawfish and corn and potatoes. A gray-haired woman in pink sneakers in the back corner. A plainclothes detective at the bar end with a Diet Coke, eating a boudin ball.

Four boyfriends in a row in front of the buffet. And a space at the end where Jody was supposed to be standing, because he'd show up at eight, an hour late, if he showed up at all.

Except at ten till seven, Jody walked in the door.

He came straight over to me with a big smile and slapped my shoulder.

"Hey, brother. Thought I'd come early, help y'all set up." His eyes were moving around the room the whole time he talked. Counting heads. "Guess you started without me, huh?"

"Guess we did," I said.

He'd come an hour early to a party he thought was at eight. A man only does that if he's planning to get to somebody before the party starts. I don't know where he was going to stand to wave her off, the parking lot, the hostess stand. But the hostess had already walked Michelle in the side door, because Gabby had arranged that too.

Jody's eyes swept the back corner. And stopped.

On a gray-haired woman in pink sneakers, eating crawfish.

He didn't move. Didn't say anything. He just stood there next to me with his mouth slightly open, the way a man stands when he steps on a rake in the dark.

Then the door to the back room opened, and Michelle LeBlanc walked in wearing a gold paper crown, and said, "Oh my Lord, y'all, what is all this?"

Jody stepped into the empty space at the end of our line, because there was nowhere else in that room for him to go.

And that's where we were when I held out the cake.

HAPPY BIRTHDAY TO OUR GIRLFRIEND.

Michelle looked at the cake. She looked down the row of us again. Me. Curtis. Bobby. Pete. Jody.

Then she did something I'll give her credit for. She laughed. A big, bright, party laugh, and she put her hands on her hips and turned to the room.

"Okay, y'all got me," she said. "Who put this together? Is this a roast? Is this one of those roasts?" She pointed at me. "Antonio, you're a mess."

Nobody laughed with her. Thirty-one people stood there holding plates of crawfish.

"It's not a roast, Michelle," I said. "It's a calendar."

I set the cake down on the table. Then I nodded to Curtis.

"Tuesdays and Thursdays," Curtis said, in that deep slow voice. "Two years. Nine thousand dollars for your mama's dialysis and transplant."

"Every other weekend," Bobby said. "Fourteen months. Twelve thousand. For the transplant."

Pete cleared his throat. He was holding his hat in both hands.

"Sundays," he said. "After church. Since last summer. Sixteen thousand seven hundred dollars." His voice shook a little. "I told you about Arlette. I told you how she died. And you came to me two months later with a letter about your mother's kidneys."

A woman in the crowd, one of the ladies from First Methodist, said "Oh, Michelle," out loud, like a mother catching a child with her hand in the collection plate.

"Mondays and Wednesdays," I said. "Eleven months. Four thousand."

Michelle's party smile was still there, but it had gone stiff, like frosting left out in the sun.

"Those were gifts," she said. "Every one of those was a gift. Y'all gave me that money because you wanted to. Nobody made you. That's not a crime. You can't take a gift back."

"Gifts for what?" I asked.

"For my mama. You know that. My mama's sick. She's—"

"Show me your wrist," I said.

She stopped.

"The bracelet," I said. "Your grandmother's bracelet. The one I took a link out of. You're wearing it right now."

She looked down at her left wrist. She was. She'd worn it to the party because it was the nicest thing she had.

"Turn the clasp over and read the inside to everybody," I said. "Out loud. Or I can. I've got the words memorized."

She put her right hand over the bracelet.

So I said it for her.

"M. You're my Tuesday and Thursday. Love, C."

Curtis raised one big scarred hand, like a man in a classroom.

"That's me," he said. "I'm C. I bought it at Leroy Gautreaux's on Broad Street. Her grandmother's never seen it."

Somebody laughed out loud at that, a real laugh this time, and somebody else shushed them.

Michelle's face was getting red from the neck up.

"Okay," she said. "Okay, so I dated more than one man. So what? I'm single. I never married any of y'all. That's my business. And my mama is sick. Whatever you think you know—"

"Hey, baby."

The voice came from the back corner. Small and dry and carrying, like a drill sergeant at a church social.

Every head in the room turned.

Yvette LeBlanc stood up from her little table, wiped her fingers on a napkin, and came walking across the room in her pink sneakers with a paper plate of crawfish shells in one hand. The crowd parted for her like she was the bride.

"Happy birthday, Michelle," she said. "I'm feeling real good, thank you for asking. Did line dancing Thursday. Dr. Fruge says I've got the kidneys of a fifty-year-old."

Michelle didn't make a sound. Her mouth opened, but nothing came out. Her paper crown slid all the way down over one ear.

"Mama," she said finally, like a little girl.

"Dialysis," Yvette said. "Three times a week. In Houston. Lord, I must be tired." She looked around the room at the coworkers, the gym friends, the church people. "I haven't seen my daughter in two years. Not since she told me I was dead to her. I guess she figured she'd make some money off the funeral."

Pete Savoie put his hat over his heart.

And that's when Michelle turned around. Not to me. Not to her mother. She turned around and looked at the end of the line, at Jody Reeves, standing there in his polo shirt and boat shoes, and she said, loud enough for every person in that room to hear it:

"You said they'd never find her. You said the old witch didn't even have Facebook!"

The room went so still I could hear the crawfish boiler bubbling through the kitchen wall.

Jody's face went the color of the cake frosting.

"Michelle," he said, very low. "Shut your mouth."

"You said—"

"Shut your mouth, Michelle!"

"Jody Reeves," Yvette said pleasantly. "You still owe me for the transmission on that Altima, son-in-law."

There it was. Son-in-law. I watched it go across the room like a wave at a ballgame. Curtis turned his head slowly. Bobby turned his whole body. Pete just closed his eyes.

"Son-in-law," Bobby said. "You're her husband?"

"Since 2011," Yvette said. "I've got the wedding pictures. He wore a white tuxedo. It was awful."

"You sat in that shop," Curtis said, "and ate our boudin."

Jody didn't answer him. He was already moving. He went sideways, fast, past the end of the buffet table toward the side door of the back room, the one that let out to the parking lot.

He made it three steps.

Bobby Duhon is six-foot-four and has been throwing chain and tying off crew boats in the Gulf of Mexico for twenty years. He didn't hit Jody. He didn't have to. He just stepped into the doorway sideways, like a man closing a gate, and when Jody tried to shove past him, Bobby reached out and took a handful of that nice polo shirt and pinned him gently against the wall, the way you'd hold a toddler trying to climb out of a grocery cart.

"Easy, boyfriend number five," Bobby said. "Party's not over."

"Get your hands off me! That's assault!"

"Sir, that's not assault. That's a citizen holding a door."

Detective Toni Lejeune set down her Diet Coke at the bar, walked over, and took a badge wallet out of her back pocket, and held it up in Jody's face.

"Calcasieu Parish Sheriff's Office, financial crimes," she said. "Mr. Reeves, Mr. Duhon is going to let go of your shirt now, and you're going to turn around and put your hands on that wall for me. Mrs. Reeves, you stay right where you are."

"It's LeBlanc," Michelle said, out of habit.

"Not on your marriage license, it isn't," the detective said. "I pulled it Thursday."

Two uniformed deputies came in through the kitchen. I hadn't even known they were back there. They took Jody first, cuffed, with his face against the paneling of Rocky's back room, still talking, saying something about lawyers and gifts and a misunderstanding.

Then one of them went to Michelle.

She looked at me. She'd stopped crying. She'd never really started. Her eyes were dry and clear and calculating, even then, even with the cuffs coming, like she was still looking for the angle.

"Antonio," she said. "Baby. Please. Tell them it was a gift. You can do that. You can tell them. I loved you. Mondays and Wednesdays, that was real. That was the only real part."

I held out my hand, palm up.

"The bracelet, Michelle," I said. "The detective's going to need it for evidence."

She looked at my hand for a long moment. Then she unfastened the clasp with shaking fingers, the clasp I'd fixed myself, and dropped the bracelet into my palm. I handed it straight to Detective Lejeune, who slid it into a little plastic bag and sealed it.

The deputy turned Michelle around and put the cuffs on her, right there in front of the buffet, with her gold paper crown still hanging off one ear.

They walked her out through the crowd. Her coworkers. Her gym friends. The church ladies from First Methodist. Nobody said a word.

At the door, she stopped and looked back at the woman in the pink sneakers.

"Mama," she said.

Yvette LeBlanc picked a crawfish off her plate, broke the tail off, and looked her daughter right in the face.

"Happy birthday, baby," she said. "Your mama never felt better in her life."
