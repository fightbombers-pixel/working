My wife texted me that at ten minutes to seven on a Thursday night, while I was sitting in the fourth row of the Kennedy High School auditorium in Cedar Rapids, Iowa, with about three hundred other parents, holding a bouquet of grocery-store carnations for our daughter's first solo. I read the text twice. Then I looked out the auditorium doors at the parking lot, where it was a clear October evening and not one single car was moving on First Avenue.

There is no such thing as hours of traffic in Cedar Rapids. There's the train on Eighth Street. That's twelve minutes on a bad night.

Our daughter Josie is sixteen, and she'd been practicing "Over the Rainbow" in the shower for six weeks. Her mother had promised her, in our kitchen, that morning, with her hand on Josie's cheek, that she'd be in the front rows.

I texted back with one thumb.

No problem. Drive safe.

Then I opened the app.

Robin drives a 2021 Acura that's on our insurance policy, and our insurance has one of those safe-driver programs where the car reports how you drive and they give you a discount. The app has a map. I nodded once.

She stood up, picked up her husband's jacket, and walked up the aisle and out the side door of the auditorium while the eighth-grade show choir was singing "Don't Stop Believin'."

Eleven minutes later, Joy Bowman would be standing in her own backyard in the dark with a flashlight, shining it on a woman in a good wool coat who was halfway out of her downstairs bathroom window.

What my wife didn't know, sitting in another man's house while her daughter waited backstage, was what was in the glove box of her own Acura. One page, folded in thirds, with her own signature at the bottom. She'd signed it in May to save two hundred and twelve dollars a year.

My name is Jesse Harwell. I'm 52 years old, and I'm the fleet maintenance manager for a regional trucking company out by the Eastern Iowa Airport. I keep a hundred and forty tractor-trailers running. Every one of them has a box under the dash that reports where it is, how fast it's going, and how long it's been sitting still. Drivers lie to me all the time. Eighteen years of that job taught me one thing: people tell stories, and the box tells the truth. I believe the box.

Hold on to that.

Robin didn't answer. The courtroom was so quiet I could hear the radiator ticking.

"Mrs. Harwell. How many times?"

"Forty-seven."

Somebody in the back row let out a breath. Judge Beck took her glasses off.

"Forty-seven," Marcy said. "Mostly between four-thirty and six in the evening, on weeknights. Why were you checking where your husband's truck was, forty-seven times, at the time of day he usually drove home from work?"

"I wanted to know when he'd be home."

"Why?"

Robin looked at the second row on the left. At Darren Bowman, in his camel coat. She looked at him for a long time, like she was waiting for him to stand up and tell her what to say.

He looked at his shoes.

"No further questions," Marcy said.

She called Joy Bowman next. Joy walked up in her Hawkeyes sweatshirt, because she said later she wasn't going to dress up for Darren, and she sat in the witness chair and held a printed photograph of an iPad screen with both hands.

"Mrs. Bowman, what is this?"

"Text messages between my husband, Darren Bowman, and Robin Harwell. From the family iPad. Dated September eighteenth."

"Would you read it to the court?"

Joy read it. All of it. In her librarian's voice, clear enough for the back row.

"'What if he ever figures it out.' 'Then you file first. Say he's been tracking you. Say you're scared. Iowa judges throw the guy out of the house same day. He's the bad guy, you keep the house, the kid stays with you, and we get time to figure out the rest.' 'That's kind of evil, lol.' 'That's kind of smart. Love you.'"

Neal Hargis stood up again, then didn't say anything, then sat down.

In the second row behind him, Darren Bowman stood up and started edging toward the aisle.

"Sir," Judge Beck said, without looking up. "Sit down. I may have questions for you."

He sat down.

Judge Beck read the screenshot for herself. Then she read Exhibit Three again. Then she took off her glasses and looked at my wife for a long time.

"Mrs. Harwell," she said. "This court exists to protect people who are genuinely afraid. Those people need these orders. Every false petition makes it harder for a judge to believe the next woman who walks in here with a real bruise." She set the screenshot down. "You consented in writing to your husband seeing your car's location. He looked exactly once, on the night you lied to him about where you were. You looked at his forty-seven times. And three weeks before any of this, you and the man in the second row planned to file this petition if you got caught. I find no basis for a protective order. The petition is dismissed. The temporary order is vacated, effective immediately. Mr. Harwell may return to his residence today."

She looked at Robin over her glasses.

"And I'm going to send a copy of this transcript to the county attorney's office. They can decide what to do about the statements in your petition. I'd suggest you talk to Mr. Hargis about that."

She banged the gavel. That was it. Two weeks on my brother's couch, and it took eleven minutes.

Out in the hallway, Robin caught up to Darren by the elevators. She grabbed his coat sleeve. I could hear her from twenty feet away. Everyone could.

"You said it would work. You said the judge throws them out the same day. You said your buddy Ken."

"Robin, not here."

"You said 'file first'! It was your idea!"

He pulled his sleeve out of her hand. He did it the way you'd pull a sleeve off a nail.

"I never told you to lie under oath," Darren said, loud, for the hallway. "That's on you."

The elevator came. Joy Bowman stepped into it first, and turned around, and held the door, and looked at her husband.

"Darren," she said. "You can take the stairs. And when you get home, your things are in the garage. Riley packed them."

The doors closed. Darren stood there for a second. Then he took the stairs.

Robin turned around. She was standing in the hallway in her funeral dress with her Kleenex balled up in her hand, and she looked at me, and I saw her try to find the smile she'd used on me at the kitchen table that morning before the concert, when she'd promised our daughter the front row.

"Jesse," she said. "I was scared. I swear to God I was scared. I didn't know what else to do. I've lost everything. Josie won't even talk to me. What am I supposed to do now?"

"Go home and open the glove box, Robin," I said. "Read what you signed."
