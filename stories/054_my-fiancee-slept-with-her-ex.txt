"We're not married yet, Luther, so forget it."

My fiancée said it at three o'clock in the morning, thirteen hours before our wedding, standing in the doorway of the bridal cabin at Pine Cove Lodge in a white satin robe that said BRIDE across the back in rhinestones. Her sister was standing on the porch of the next cabin over in her pajamas, and her ex-husband was standing six feet behind me on the gravel path with his shirt buttoned wrong, and both of them heard every word.

She didn't cry. She didn't stammer. She smirked. One corner of her mouth went up, the way it did when a customer at her salon asked for a discount.

I was holding a lace handkerchief in my hand. Her grandmother's. Something old. She'd left it on the table at the rehearsal dinner, and I'd been walking it down to her cabin because I couldn't sleep and I thought, she'll want this first thing in the morning.

Thirty-eight thousand dollars. That's what I'd paid for the wedding. The lodge, the chapel, the barbecue, the band, the dress she was going to wear at four o'clock that afternoon. Every dime.

I looked down. On the bottom porch step, half in the shadow, something gold caught the light from the porch lamp. A man's wristwatch. Heavy. The kind they hand you in Victory Lane.

I bent down, picked it up, and put it in my jacket pocket.

Then I handed her the handkerchief.

"See you at four," I said.

Thirteen hours later, my bride would be standing at the altar of the Pine Cove Chapel, saying those same eight words again, into a microphone this time, and a hundred and forty people in their Sunday clothes would hear every one of them.

What she didn't know was whose watch was in my pocket, and what was engraved on the back of it.

My name is Luther Brooks. I'm fifty-five years old, and I build racing engines for a NASCAR shop in Mooresville, North Carolina, and I've been doing it for thirty-one years. When a race car comes back to the shop on Monday, the first thing you do isn't look at the body or the tires. You pull the spark plugs. A plug tells you exactly what that engine did the night before. Too hot, too lean, too rich. An engine can't lie to you about Saturday night. People can. Engines can't.

Hold on to that.

Rochelle closed the cabin door in my face. Not hard. Just closed it, the way you close a door on a salesman.

I turned around.

Chance Avery was still standing on the gravel path, six feet away, under a pine tree, with his sunglasses pushed up on top of his head at three in the morning. He's forty-eight. He drove the Cup Series for nine years, won at Talladega in 2006, and now he does pit reports on television in a nice blazer and talks about tire wear. Back in 2006, I built the engine he won with.

"Luther," he said. "Look, man."

"Go home, Chance."

"I didn't mean for you to—"

"Go home."

He looked at me for a second. Then he looked at my jacket pocket. He patted his own left wrist, where the watch should have been, and I watched him figure it out.

"That's mine," he said. "That's my Talladega watch."

"I know whose it is. I was standing there when they gave it to you."

"Give it back."

"See you at four, Chance," I said. "You're on the guest list. Rochelle insisted. Row three."

He opened his mouth. Then he turned around and went fast down the path toward the parking lot, and a minute later I heard an engine start, a big V8, and tires on gravel.

On the porch of the next cabin, Leah Vance, Rochelle's younger sister and her maid of honor, was standing with both hands over her mouth.

I walked past her without saying anything, back up the path toward the groom's cabin.

I heard the screen door behind me. Bare feet on the gravel.

"Luther. Luther, wait. Please."

Leah caught up with me at the little bench by the lake, where the path turned. Lake Norman was black and flat, with the lights of somebody's dock way out on the far shore. She was forty-one, a kindergarten teacher in Huntersville, quiet where her sister was loud, and she was shaking.

"Sit down a minute," she said. "Please."

I sat down. She sat at the other end of the bench, hugging her knees.

"I heard him come in," she said. "Around eleven. The walls in these cabins are like paper. I heard them laughing. I heard—" She stopped. "I should have come and got you. I should've walked right up to your cabin and knocked on your door. I didn't. I'm sorry."

"It's not your fault."

"It's a little bit my fault." She wiped her face with the sleeve of her pajamas. "Because I knew, Luther. Not this. Not tonight. But I knew she was texting him. Since August. Since right after you two sent out the invitations."

I looked out at the lake.

"Since August," I said.

"She showed me some of them. She thought it was funny. He'd text her 'last chance, Ro,' and she'd send back a winky face. She said it didn't mean anything. She said every woman wants to know her ex still wants her, the week of her wedding." Leah's voice got thin. "She said—"

"Go on."

"She said she'd have 'one last ride' before she settled down. That's what she called it. A last ride. Like a carnival."

I laughed. I didn't mean to. It came out of me short and hard, like a backfire, there on the bench in the dark.

"A last ride," I said. "With a race car driver."

"Luther—"

"Leah, can I ask you something, and you tell me the truth?"

She nodded.

"Why's she marrying me?"

Leah was quiet so long I thought she wasn't going to answer.

"Your house," she said finally. "The one on the lake in Cornelius. With the boat dock. She's been talking about that house since your first date. And the salon's in trouble. She owes the landlord eleven thousand dollars in back rent. She thought once you were married—" Leah stopped. "She said you were 'steady.' She said you were the kind of man who'd never leave once he said I do. She said that's what made you perfect."

"Steady," I said.

"And Chance," Leah said, "is broke. He lost everything in that restaurant in Charlotte. He's living in a condo his girlfriend pays for. He can't give her anything but—" She waved her hand at the dark, at the cabin, at all of it. "That."

We sat there for a while. An owl was hooting somewhere in the pines, way off.

"What are you going to do?" Leah asked.

I took the watch out of my pocket and turned it over in my hand under the light of the path lamp. On the back, engraved in the gold, it said: CHANCE AVERY. TALLADEGA. 2006. VICTORY LANE.

"I'm going to get married at four," I said. "Or I'm not."

Leah looked at the watch. Then at me.

"Whatever you do," she said, "I'm not going to lie for her. Not tomorrow. Not ever again."

My son Tre came over to my cabin at eight-thirty in the morning with two coffees from the lodge restaurant and a sausage biscuit in a paper bag, wearing basketball shorts and the T-shirt he'd slept in. He's twenty-eight, a crew chief on a late-model team in Hickory, and he was my best man.

"Big day, Pop," he said, and handed me a coffee. Then he looked at my face. "What's wrong? You look like you blew an engine on the last lap."

I told him. I told him all of it, sitting at the little table by the window of the groom's cabin while the sun came up over the lake.

Tre didn't say anything until I finished. Then he stood up, and set his coffee down very carefully, and walked to the door.

"Where you going?"

"To find Chance Avery."

"Sit down, son."

"Pop, I'm going to—"

"You're going to sit down," I said, "because you're a crew chief and you know better. You don't fix a problem by throwing a wrench at it."

He stood there with his hand on the doorknob, breathing hard. Then he came back and sat down.

"So what do we do?" he said. "Cancel? I can go tell everybody. I'll go stand in the parking lot and tell people to turn around."

"No."

"No?"

"A hundred and forty people drove up here, Tre. Your Aunt Bernice flew in from Tulsa. They're going to want to know why. And if I cancel it this morning, by tonight Rochelle will be telling every one of them that I got cold feet. That I'm a mean, cold man who left her at the altar. That I was drunk, or crazy, or that I hit her. You know she will."

Tre thought about that.

"Yeah," he said. "She would."

"So I'm going to stand up there at four o'clock, in front of everybody," I said. "And when it's time for my vows, I'm going to tell the truth. Once. Short. Calm. No yelling, no names. And then I'm going to give Chance back his watch."

My son looked at me for a long time.

"Pop," he said slowly. "That's the most savage thing I've ever heard."

"It's not savage. It's the plugs. I'm just reading the plugs."

Tre picked his coffee back up and grinned for the first time since he'd walked in.

"The videographer clips a microphone on the groom," he said. "For the vows. It goes right into the speakers in the chapel so the people in the back can hear. I watched him test it at the rehearsal."

"Then I guess they'll hear," I said.

My phone buzzed on the table. A text from Rochelle.

Morning husband. Don't be late. Wear the blue tie.

That was it. Not a word about three in the morning. Not "we need to talk." She'd decided I would do exactly what she'd told me to do. Forget it.

I typed back: I'll be there.

At noon, somebody knocked on my cabin door, three short raps, the way a landlord knocks.

It was Pauline Vance. Rochelle's mother. Seventy years old, in a mother-of-the-bride dress the color of a peach, with her hair already done up for the photos and a corsage box in her hand.

"Luther," she said. "May I come in?"

"Yes, ma'am."

She came in and set the corsage box on the table and didn't sit down. Pauline has never once sat down in any room I was in. She stands, like she might need to leave fast.

"I brought your boutonniere," she said. "The florist gave it to me by mistake."

"Thank you."

She didn't move toward the door. She looked at the blue tie hanging on the back of the closet door. Then at me.

"Leah talked to me this morning," she said.

"I figured she might."

"She's very upset. Leah gets upset about things. She always has." Pauline folded her hands in front of her. "I'm going to say something to you, Luther, and I want you to hear it the way I mean it. Whatever happened last night, or didn't happen, is between you and Rochelle."

"It happened, Mrs. Vance. I was standing there."

"Whatever happened," she said again, a little louder, "a wedding day is a holy day. Every marriage has a bump in the road. My husband, God rest him, had his bumps. Plenty of them. And I stood by him for forty-four years, because that's what you do. You forgive, and you go forward, and you don't embarrass your family in front of a hundred and forty people."

"Who's embarrassing who?"

"Don't be smart with me." Her mouth went tight. "My daughter has worked very hard to put this day together."

"I paid for this day, Mrs. Vance. Thirty-eight thousand dollars. I've got the receipts in my truck."

"And you'll get your money's worth," she said. "You'll get a good wife who keeps a nice home. She's had a hard life, Luther. Chance put her through hell. Do you know what it's like to be married to a man who's on television every weekend, with women throwing themselves at him in every town on the circuit?"

"Then why'd she let him in last night?"

Pauline didn't answer that.

"Did you know?" I asked. "That she's been texting him since August?"

She looked at the window. At the lake.

"A man forgives on his wedding day," she said finally. "That's all I came to say. A real man forgives. A small man holds a grudge."

"Mrs. Vance," I said. "Did you know Chance was in her cabin last night?"

There was a long silence. Somewhere outside, the caterers were setting up chairs on the lawn, and I could hear the metal clank of them unfolding.

"I saw his car," she said quietly. "On my way back from the ladies' room. Around midnight." She lifted her chin. "And I went to bed. Because it was none of my business, and because I knew in the morning my daughter would get up and put on a white dress and marry a good, steady man, and everything would be fine."

"Steady," I said.

"Yes. Steady." She picked her purse up off the table. "Wear the blue tie, Luther. Be at the chapel by three-thirty for pictures. And whatever you're thinking about doing, don't."

She left. She didn't close the door all the way. Pauline never closes doors all the way.

I stood there holding the boutonniere box. Then I opened it. A white rosebud, with a sprig of baby's breath and a pearl pin.

Steady. That word again. It was starting to sound like something they'd written on a price tag.

At one o'clock, Gene Whitley came by with a box under his arm.

Gene is sixty-eight. He's owned Whitley Motorsports since 1994, and he's the only man I've ever worked for who could look at a dyno sheet and tell you what the engine builder had for breakfast. He'd driven up from Mooresville early, he said, to beat the traffic and drop off a present from the shop before the ceremony.

The present was an old piston, polished up like chrome and mounted on a walnut base, with a little brass plate that said LUTHER BROOKS, 31 YEARS, STILL RUNNING.

"The boys chipped in," Gene said. "Ugly as sin. I told them you'd love it."

"I do love it."

He looked at me over the top of his glasses. Gene has known me since I was twenty-four.

"What's wrong with you?"

So I told him. Short version. The cabin, the robe, the watch.

Gene sat down on the edge of the bed and didn't say anything for a while. Then he took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes.

"Chance Avery came by the shop in September," he said. "Did you know that?"

"No."

"Walked in like he owned the place. Wanted a job. Said he was done with television, wanted to get back on the competition side. Spotter, driver coach, anything." Gene put his glasses back on. "I told him we didn't have anything. And he said, real casual, 'Well, Gene, Luther and I are about to be practically family. Maybe he'll put in a word.'"

I sat down in the chair by the window.

"Practically family," I said.

"I thought it was a strange thing to say about the man marrying your ex-wife." Gene stood up and picked his hat up off the bed. "Now I don't think it's strange at all. I think somebody was planning on having a husband with a lake house and a boyfriend with a job in that husband's shop."

He went to the door and put his hat on.

"Whatever you do this afternoon, son," he said, "I'll be in the fourth row. And if anybody from the Avery side wants to make something of it, half the fabrication department is sitting right behind me."

"I'm not going to need the fabrication department, Gene."

"I know you won't," he said. "But they're coming anyway. There's an open bar."

At two o'clock, I was in the shower when I heard the cabin door open.

I came out in a towel with shaving cream still on one side of my face, and Chance Avery was standing in my little kitchen with his sunglasses on his head, holding a bottle of bourbon by the neck like he'd brought a housewarming present.

"Door was open," he said.

"Pauline was here. She doesn't close doors."

"Yeah. I remember." He set the bourbon on the counter. Pappy Van Winkle. A two-thousand-dollar bottle, if it was real. "Peace offering."

"Put your shoes back on and take it with you."

"Come on, Luther. Five minutes. Man to man." He leaned on the counter. "We go back. You built my Talladega motor. I'd never have won that race without your engine. I've told everybody that for twenty years. On TV."

"You told everybody it was the driver."

He laughed. "Well, it was a little bit the driver."

I wiped the shaving cream off my face with the towel and pulled on a T-shirt. I didn't hurry. A man in a towel is at a disadvantage in an argument, and I wanted to give him time to talk.

"What do you want, Chance?"

"I want my watch back." He said it simple. "That's all. That watch is the only thing I've got left from the good years. I sold the rest. The trophies, the helmet, everything. Restaurant took it all. That watch is it." He pulled out his wallet. "I'll give you five thousand dollars for it. Cash. Right now. And I'll stay out of your way today. I won't even come to the ceremony. Courtney and I'll go home."

"Courtney."

"My girlfriend. She's down in the parking lot." He flipped through bills. "Five thousand. Come on. It's just a watch."

"Then why do you want it so bad?"

He stopped counting.

"Because," I said, "if a hundred and forty people see me hand you your Talladega watch at four o'clock, they're all going to ask the same question. How did Luther Brooks get Chance Avery's watch the night before his wedding? And Courtney's going to be sitting next to you in row three, wondering the same thing."

Chance put his wallet away slowly.

"You're going to make a scene," he said. "At your own wedding."

"I'm going to tell the truth at my own wedding. If that makes a scene, it's not my scene."

"You're a mechanic, Luther." His voice went harder. "You're a grease-under-the-nails guy who builds motors in a shop. I'm on television. I've got two hundred thousand followers. You start telling stories about me, I'll have a lawyer on you so fast your head'll spin. Defamation. Slander. You'll be paying me for the rest of your life."

"It's not slander if it's true. I've got a watch with your name engraved on the back that says it's true. And Rochelle's sister was on the porch next door."

He stared at me.

"Leah," he said.

"Leah."

"Leah won't say anything. Leah's scared of her own shadow."

"Leah's been scared for a long time," I said. "I don't think she's scared anymore."

Chance looked at the bourbon on the counter. Then he looked at me, and I watched the TV smile go away, and I saw the man under it. Forty-eight, broke, living in a condo his girlfriend paid for, with nothing left from the good years but one gold watch, which was in my jacket pocket.

"She called me," he said. "You should know that. Monday. She called me. She said, 'Last chance, Chance,' like it was funny. She said you were boring. She said you were safe. She said she was marrying a house." He picked up the bourbon. "I didn't break into anything, Luther. I just showed up where I was invited."

"I know," I said. "That's the worst part."

He went to the door. Then he stopped, the way everybody seemed to stop in my door that weekend.

"What're you going to do with my watch?"

"Give it back," I said. "At four. In front of God and everybody."

He left. He closed the door all the way, at least.

I went to the window with the towel around my neck and watched him go. He didn't go down the path to the parking lot. He went the other way, down toward the bridal cabin, walking fast with the bourbon bottle swinging from his hand.

He knocked. The door opened about a foot. I saw a peach-colored sleeve. Pauline. Then Rochelle's face behind her mother's shoulder, with half her hair pinned up and curlers in the other half.

Chance talked for maybe a minute, waving the hand that didn't have the bottle in it. Rochelle said something back. Pauline looked up the path, right toward my window, and I didn't step back. I let her see me standing there.

The door shut. Chance stood on that porch for a second with his shoulders down. Then he walked off toward the parking lot with the bottle, and a minute later I heard the big V8 start up and idle, and not leave.

So now they knew I had the watch. All three of them.

That was fine. Engines don't care if you know they're coming apart. They come apart anyway.

I finished shaving. My hand was steady. Thirty-one years of setting valve lash with a feeler gauge will do that for you.

Then I put on my suit. Charcoal gray. White shirt. And the blue tie, because she'd asked me to, and because I wanted her to see me in it when I walked out of that chapel.

At three-fifteen, Tre knocked and came in wearing his own suit, with his hair combed for once.

"Videographer's name is Hollis," he said. "Nice guy. I told him you were nervous about your vows and wanted to make sure everybody could hear you, even the old folks in the back. He turned the microphone up two notches."

"Thank you, son."

"Courtney's in row three already," Tre said. "Chance's girlfriend. Real pretty. Sitting by herself. He's out in the parking lot on his phone, pacing." He paused. "And Pop? Rochelle's sister came and found me in the lodge. Leah. She said to tell you she'll be standing right next to the bride. And she said, 'Whatever he says, I'll back him up. Out loud.'"

I put the watch in the inside pocket of my jacket, right over my heart.

"Then let's go get married," I said.

The photographer wanted pictures of the bride and groom before the ceremony, out on the dock with the lake behind us, because the light was good at three-thirty. She was a young woman named Tiffany with three cameras around her neck, and she'd been texting Rochelle about "the first look" for six months.

"The first look" is when the groom stands with his back turned and the bride walks up behind him and taps him on the shoulder, and he turns around and sees her in the dress for the first time, and cries, and the photographer gets it all.

I stood on the end of the dock with my back turned, looking out at Lake Norman. A bass boat went by slow, two old men in it, and one of them waved. I waved back.

"Okay, Luther," Tiffany called. "She's coming! Don't turn around till she taps you!"

I heard Rochelle's heels on the dock boards. Click, click, click. Then a hand on my shoulder.

I turned around.

She was beautiful. I'll say that, and I'll mean it. The dress was ivory, off the shoulder, with lace down the arms, and her dark hair was pinned up with little pearls, and her grandmother's lace handkerchief was tucked into the bodice where it peeked out. She'd had her makeup done by somebody who knew what they were doing.

She smiled at me for the camera. A big, wide, wedding-day smile.

"Look at you," she said. "In the blue tie."

"You asked."

Tiffany's camera was going click-click-click. "Beautiful! Now hold hands! Rochelle, look up at him!"

Rochelle took my hands and looked up at me and kept smiling, and under her breath, through her teeth, so the camera couldn't hear, she said:

"Leah's been running her mouth to everybody, hasn't she?"

"Leah hasn't said a word to anybody but your mother and me."

"Mama told me." The smile stayed. "Whatever Leah said, she's jealous. She's always been jealous of me. She's forty-one and she's never been married, and she can't stand that I'm getting married twice."

"Tiffany, can we get one with the lake?" I said, louder.

"Oh, love it! Turn this way! Luther, put your arm around her!"

I put my arm around her waist. She leaned into me for the camera, and her voice dropped even lower.

"Last night was nothing," she said. "It was goodbye. That's all it was. Every woman deserves a goodbye. Chance and I had nine years, Luther. You don't just close a door on nine years without saying goodbye."

"You said goodbye to him in a robe that says Bride on the back."

"Don't be crude."

"I'm not the one who was being crude at midnight."

"And now kiss!" Tiffany called.

Rochelle went up on her toes and kissed me. I let her. The camera clicked. When she came down, the smile was still there for Tiffany, but her eyes weren't smiling at all.

"Listen to me," she said, very low. "In thirty minutes you're going to stand up there and say 'I do' in front of everybody we know. My mother. My clients. Your boss is here, Luther. Your whole shop is here. You're not going to embarrass yourself in front of your whole shop."

"No," I said. "I'm not."

She took that the way she wanted to take it. She patted my chest, right over my jacket pocket, right over the watch.

"Good," she said. "And whatever you think you're going to do with Chance's watch, forget that too. He lost it at the gym on Thursday. He's already told Courtney. He'll swear to it, and so will I. It's your word against two people, Luther, and one of them's on television."

"Three people," I said. "Leah was on the porch."

For just a second the smile flickered. Then it came back, brighter.

"Leah," she said, "will do what Mama tells her. She always has. And I meant what I said last night. We're not married yet. So forget it. By five o'clock we will be, and none of it will matter."

"Last one! Walk back toward the lodge holding hands! Don't look at me, look at each other!"

We walked back up the dock holding hands, looking at each other, and Tiffany got the shot. I've seen it since. It's a good picture. You'd never know.

The Pine Cove Chapel is a little white wooden church with a steeple, set back in the pines above the lake, with a gravel path lined with mason jars full of candles. It seats about a hundred and fifty if everybody scoots in. That afternoon it was full.

My side had my whole shop from Mooresville in their good shirts, the engine builders and the fabricators and the guys from the dyno room, and my boss, Gene Whitley, who's owned the team since 1994. Tre's girlfriend. My Aunt Bernice from Tulsa in a hat with a bird on it. Some old friends from the short tracks.

Rochelle's side had her clients from the salon, and her cousins from Gastonia, and Pauline in the front pew in her peach dress with her corsage.

And in row three, on the aisle, Chance Avery in a navy blazer, with his sunglasses tucked into his pocket for once, sitting very straight. Next to him was a young woman with long blonde hair, maybe thirty-five, in a yellow dress. Courtney Sapp. She was holding his hand. He wasn't holding hers back.

I stood at the front of the chapel next to Reverend Dale Spurlock, who'd married half of Mooresville and buried the other half. Tre stood beside me as best man. The little lapel microphone was clipped to my lapel right under the white rosebud, with a wire running down inside my jacket to a pack on my belt.

The organ started up. The doors at the back opened.

The flower girl came first, Rochelle's grand-niece, throwing rose petals in clumps. Then the bridesmaids in sage green. Then Leah, the maid of honor, alone. She came down the aisle with her eyes on me the whole way, not smiling, and when she got to the front she didn't go stand on the bride's side right away. She stopped in front of me for one second, and nodded once.

Then everybody stood up, and the organist played "Here Comes the Bride," and Rochelle came down the aisle on the arm of her uncle Ray, with her grandmother's handkerchief tucked in her bodice and her veil down, smiling under it like she'd won the lottery.

When she reached me, her uncle lifted her veil and kissed her cheek and gave her hand to me. Her hand was warm and perfectly steady.

"Dearly beloved," Reverend Spurlock began.

I don't remember much of the first part. He talked about love being patient and love being kind. He read from Corinthians. Somebody's baby fussed in the back and got carried out. I stood there holding Rochelle's hands and looking at her face, and she looked back at me, and every once in a while, very slightly, her eyebrow went up, like she was asking me a question she already knew the answer to.

Then the reverend said, "Luther and Rochelle have written their own vows. Luther, I believe you're going first."

I let go of Rochelle's hands.

I didn't take out a card. I didn't need one. I turned a little, so I was half facing her and half facing the chapel, the way the reverend had showed us at the rehearsal so everybody could see.

"Rochelle," I said.

My voice came out of the chapel speakers, clear as anything. The little microphone was good. Hollis the videographer gave me a thumbs-up from the side aisle.

"I've been building engines for thirty-one years," I said. "Everybody in this room who knows me knows that. And when a car comes back to the shop after a race, the first thing you do is pull the plugs. Because the plugs tell you what happened the night before. You can't hide a bad night from the plugs."

A few of the guys from my shop nodded. They'd all pulled plugs.

Rochelle's smile tightened. Just a little.

"Last night at three o'clock in the morning," I said, "I walked down to the bridal cabin to bring you your grandmother's handkerchief. The one you've got on right now. You left it at the rehearsal dinner, and I thought you'd want it."

Somewhere in the pews, a woman said, "Aww."

"When I got there," I said, "Chance Avery was coming out your door."

The "aww" stopped like somebody had cut the wire.

I didn't raise my voice. I didn't have to. The microphone did it for me.

"I asked you about it," I said. "Right there on the porch. And you said, 'We're not married yet, Luther, so forget it.'"

The chapel went so quiet you could hear the candles hiss in the mason jars on the path outside.

Rochelle's face went pink, then red, then a strange flat color like old putty. She took a step toward me and grabbed at my lapel, at the microphone, and I stepped back half a step, easy, and her fingers closed on air.

"He's lying," she said, loud, to the room. "He's lying! He's — he's been drinking. Luther, stop this. Stop it right now."

"I've had two cups of coffee and a sausage biscuit," I said. "My son brought them. Ask him."

"I can confirm the biscuit," Tre said from beside me, deadpan, and somebody in my shop laughed out loud and then turned it into a cough.

"I'm not going to stand up here and promise to forget it," I said. "I can't. I'm a steady man. Your mother says so. Your sister says so. Steady men don't forget things. That's what makes us steady."

Pauline Vance had both hands pressed flat on the front pew like she was trying to hold it down.

"Mama," Rochelle said. "Mama, tell him—"

"And Chance," I said. I turned toward row three. "I believe you dropped this on her porch step."

I reached inside my jacket, took out the gold watch, and held it up so the light from the chapel windows caught it. Then I walked down the two steps from the altar, down the aisle three rows, and held it out to him.

"Talladega, 2006," I said. "Victory Lane. It's got your name on the back. I built that engine. Figured I'd better bring back the watch, too."

Chance Avery sat there in row three with his face the color of a white T-shirt. He didn't reach for it.

Courtney Sapp did. She took the watch out of my hand and turned it over and read the back of it. Then she looked at the man beside her.

"You told me," Courtney said, in a voice that carried all over that little chapel without any microphone at all, "that you lost this watch at the gym on Thursday. You had me looking under the treadmills."

"Court, baby, let me—"

"You said you were at your brother's last night. In Concord. You said you'd sleep on his couch so you wouldn't have to drive home drunk from the rehearsal party."

"Court—"

"We weren't even invited to the rehearsal party!"

She stood up, set the watch in his lap like she was setting down a dead mouse, picked up her purse, and walked out down the center aisle in her yellow dress with her head high. Nobody said a word. A hundred and forty people turned their heads and watched her go, all the way out the back door, which banged shut behind her.

Chance sat there with the watch in his lap.

Then he got up too, and went out the side door, fast, with his head down, and he didn't look at Rochelle once.

I walked back up to the altar.

Rochelle was crying now. Real tears this time, mascara and all. Her uncle Ray had sat down hard in the front pew next to Pauline, and Pauline was dabbing at her own face with a tissue.

"Luther," Rochelle said. "Please. Please. Not like this. Not in front of everybody. We can talk about it. Just — finish the vows, and we'll talk about it tonight. I promise. I'll explain everything."

"There's nothing left to explain, Ro."

"There is! There's everything to explain! It was a goodbye! It was nothing! I was nervous, I was scared, you were—" She grabbed my sleeve, the blue tie, anything. "You can't leave me standing up here! Not today! Leah, tell him! Tell him I love him!"

Leah stepped up beside her sister. She was holding her bouquet of sage and white roses in both hands so tight the stems bent.

"I heard you last night, Ro," Leah said. Quiet, but the microphone on my lapel was close enough to pick it up. "I heard everything through the wall. I heard you laughing. And at noon I heard you tell Mama that Luther would do what he was told because he's 'steady.'" Her voice shook. "I'm not going to lie for you. I'm done lying for you. I've been doing it since we were kids."

Rochelle stared at her sister like she'd never seen her before.

Then she turned back to me, and something changed in her face. All the tears dried up at once, like somebody turned off a faucet. Her chin came up. Her eyes went flat and hard, and that little smirk came back, the one from the cabin door at three in the morning.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. You want to do this in front of everybody? Fine. I'll say it again. Everybody hear me?" She turned to the pews, to her clients, her cousins, my whole shop, the reverend, her mother. "We're not married yet, Luther, so forget it!"

She said it straight into my lapel microphone. The chapel speakers carried it all the way to the back row.

Eight words. Thirteen hours after the first time.

Nobody moved. Nobody said anything. Gene Whitley, my boss, slowly took off his reading glasses and put them in his shirt pocket.

I unclipped the little microphone from under my rosebud and handed it to Tre. I took the boutonniere off my lapel and laid it on the altar rail next to the reverend's Bible.

Then I turned to the room.

"Y'all drove a long way," I said, without the microphone, loud enough. "The barbecue's paid for. The band's paid for. The bar is open and it's on me until it runs dry. Go down to the lodge and eat. Please. I'd hate for it to go to waste."

My Aunt Bernice from Tulsa stood up in her hat with the bird on it.

"I'll take a plate," she announced, to nobody in particular.

Pauline Vance stood up too, in the front pew, in her peach dress, with her tissue balled up in her fist.

"Luther Brooks," she said, "a real man forgives. I told you that this morning. A small man holds a grudge, and a small man embarrasses a family in church."

"Mrs. Vance," I said, "you saw his car at midnight and went to bed. You don't get to tell me about small."

Her uncle Ray, Pauline's brother, reached up from the pew and took her by the wrist.

"Pauline," he said, tired, like a man who'd been saying it for seventy years. "Sit down."

She sat down.

Half my shop laughed. Some of the salon ladies laughed too, and then looked guilty about it.

I turned back around to Rochelle. She was still standing at the altar in her ivory dress with her grandmother's handkerchief in her bodice, and that smirk was still on her face, but it was coming apart at the corners, like wet paper.

"Well?" she said. "Aren't you going to say anything?"

I looked at her for a long time. Thirty-eight thousand dollars. Since August. A last ride.

"You're right, Rochelle," I said. "We're not married yet. So forget it."
