"I filed the abuse report yesterday. Now he'll lose everything in court."

My wife said it to her sister in our kitchen in Boise, on a Saturday afternoon, with two women laughing and the radio playing low, and exactly one more person in the room than either of them knew about. Me. I was standing in the doorway from the garage with four bags of groceries, $118 worth, and a gallon of milk hanging off one finger.

Tamara raised her hand. Her sister Daphne slapped it. A real high five, the kind you give somebody when the Broncos score.

I looked at Tamara's left arm when she raised it. There was a bruise just above the elbow, purple going green at the edges, about the size of a man's thumb. Twenty-six years we'd been married. In twenty-six years I had never once raised my voice at her, let alone a hand.

I walked in, set the bags on the counter, took out the tub of ice cream and put it in the freezer.

"Hi, Daphne," I said. "Staying for supper?"

Both of them turned around like somebody had set off a smoke alarm.

Six days later, in Courtroom 3 of the Ada County Courthouse, my wife's lawyer stopped talking in the middle of a sentence, turned around, and looked at the man sitting in the second row behind my wife like she'd never seen him before.

What Tamara didn't know was what was sitting in the cup holder of my truck, out in the garage. A white plastic hospital band with my name on it, and Thursday's date.

My name is Lorenzo Gill. I'm 54 years old, and I'm a network administrator for the Boise School District. I keep forty-one school buildings talking to each other, which means I spend my days in closets full of blinking lights. The one thing that job has taught me is that a computer never forgets anything. People delete things. Machines just move them somewhere else. Hold on to that.

Tamara found her voice first.

"Lorenzo. You're home early."

"Fred Meyer wasn't busy."

"How long were you standing there?"

I folded one of the paper bags flat and put it in the drawer where we keep them. "Long enough to know we need more milk than this."

Daphne picked her purse up off the counter and held it in front of her like a shield. She's two years older than Tamara, sells houses in the Foothills, and has a laugh you can hear from the parking lot. She wasn't laughing now.

"I should go," she said.

"You just got here," I said. "Your car's still warm. I parked next to it."

"Tam, call me."

"I will."

Daphne went out the front door so fast she left it open. Tamara stood by the sink with her arms crossed over her chest, and that bruise right there on the outside of her arm where anybody could see it.

"Whatever you heard," she said, "you didn't hear it the way it sounded."

"How did it sound?"

"Don't do that. Don't do your calm thing."

"I'm putting groceries away, Tam."

"You're doing your calm thing." Her voice climbed. "You always do this. You stand there like a wall and let me be the crazy one."

"Who gave you the bruise?"

She pulled her sleeve down over it. "You know who."

"I don't, actually."

"Then I guess a judge will tell you."

She walked out of the kitchen and up the stairs, and I heard our bedroom door close, not slammed, just closed, which with Tamara is worse.

I finished putting the groceries away. Eggs, bread, the chicken thighs, the coffee. I lined the cans up in the pantry with the labels facing out, because that's what I do when my hands need something to do. Then I went out to the garage and sat in my truck.

The hospital band was right where I'd tossed it Friday morning, curled up in the cup holder next to a pack of gum. GILL, LORENZO. DOB. A barcode. And under that, the date and time I was admitted to the emergency room at Saint Alphonsus. Thursday evening, a little before nine.

Thursday afternoon, I'd been crawling under the server racks at Capital High when a kidney stone the size of a grain of rice decided it was time to move. I've had broken ribs. I've had a nail gun go through my boot. That stone put me on the floor of a network closet sweating like a racehorse. A custodian named Felix drove me to the ER in his own car. They gave me a CT scan, a bag of fluids and enough morphine to make the ceiling tiles interesting, and they didn't let me go home until the sun was coming up Friday.

I'd called Tamara three times from that hospital bed. Thursday, around nine, she'd texted back.

"At Daphne's tonight. Wine night. Hope you feel better."

I took out my phone and found that text and read it again.

At Daphne's tonight.

Then I took a picture of the hospital band on the dashboard, front and back, and another picture of the text. I'm an IT guy. We back things up.

There's one more thing you should know, and it's going to matter. Over the garage door there's a little camera. There's one on the front porch and one on the back patio, and one in the kitchen pointing at the back door, because three years ago somebody walked in that door and took Tamara's purse off the counter while we were watching TV. I put the system in myself. Tamara uses the app on her phone to see who's at the door. She's never once asked where the video goes.

It goes two places. A copy goes up to the cloud, where the app can see it. And a copy goes to a little black box on a shelf in the basement, next to the furnace, with a blue light on the front that blinks once every two seconds.

I was looking at that blue light in my head, sitting in my truck, when the doorbell rang.

I came around the side of the house. There was an Ada County Sheriff's cruiser at the curb, and a deputy on my front porch. Big guy, late thirties, shaved head, holding a manila envelope. His name tag said DUNN.

"Lorenzo Gill?"

"That's me."

"Sir, I've got some paperwork to serve you. Can we talk inside?"

"Kitchen's this way."

He followed me in. Tamara was already halfway down the stairs, and she'd changed into a T-shirt with short sleeves. She sat down on the third step from the bottom and put her face in her hands.

Behind the deputy, Daphne came back up the walk with her phone held up in front of her, filming.

"Ma'am," Deputy Dunn said, without turning around, "I'd ask you to stay outside."

"It's my sister's house."

"Then I'd ask you to stand on the porch of your sister's house."

Daphne stood on the porch. She kept filming through the screen door.

Dunn opened the envelope on my kitchen counter, right next to the paper bags I'd just folded.

"Mr. Gill, this is an ex parte temporary protection order. That means a judge signed it this morning based on your wife's petition, without hearing from you. It's not a finding that you did anything. It means you have to leave the residence today, you can't contact Mrs. Gill directly or come within a hundred yards of her or this house, and there's a hearing set for Friday at nine where you get to tell your side. You understand?"

"I understand."

"You can take what you need for a few days. Clothes, medications, work stuff. I'll stand by while you pack."

"Okay."

He looked at me a second longer than he needed to. I think he'd served a lot of these, and I don't think a lot of guys said okay.

"Can I ask you something, Deputy?"

"You can ask."

"What day does it say this happened?"

He flipped a page. "Petitioner states the incident occurred Thursday, the ninth, at approximately ten p.m., in the kitchen of the residence. Respondent grabbed her by the arm and threw her into the pantry door."

"Thursday at ten p.m."

"That's what it says."

"Could you read that part one more time? Slow?"

From the stairs, Tamara lifted her head. "Why are you making him read it twice?"

"I just want to be sure I heard the day right," I said.

Dunn read it again, slower. Thursday, the ninth. Approximately ten p.m. In the kitchen.

"Thank you," I said.

And then I laughed. I didn't mean to. It came up out of me like a hiccup, one short bark of a laugh, right there in my kitchen with a deputy and a protection order on the counter.

Dunn's eyebrows went up a quarter of an inch.

"Something funny, sir?"

"No, sir. I'm sorry. Long week."

On the stairs, Tamara was staring at me. The tears had stopped. Something else had come into her face, something careful.

"He's laughing at me," she said to the deputy. "You see? This is what he does."

"Ma'am, I'm going to ask you to wait upstairs while he packs."

She went up. I packed one duffel bag. Underwear, socks, three work shirts, my blood pressure pills, my laptop, the charger. I took the framed picture of my mother off the dresser, and then I put it back, because it was going to look like I was moving out, and I wasn't. I was leaving for six days.

On the way out, I stopped in the kitchen doorway and looked at the pantry door. White paint, a brass knob. Not a mark on it.

Daphne was still filming on the porch when I came out.

"Smile for the judge, Lorenzo," she said.

"Hi, Daphne," I said. "Drive safe."

I put the duffel bag in the truck and backed out, and Deputy Dunn waited at the curb until I was all the way to the end of the street. That was fine. I didn't have anywhere to be.

I pulled into the Albertsons lot on Broadway and parked under a light and opened the camera app on my phone.

Tamara and I share the account. It's the same app, same login, same everything. I went to the kitchen camera and scrolled back to Thursday.

Thursday at four in the afternoon, there was a clip of the cleaning lady leaving. Then nothing. No clips Thursday evening at all. No clips Thursday night. The next clip was Friday morning, a little after six, when I walked in the back door in my hospital socks and fell asleep in my recliner.

That kitchen camera records every time something moves. The furnace kicks on and the curtain moves and it records. Twelve hours with nothing on it doesn't happen.

I tapped the little menu in the corner and found the activity log. Most people don't know it's there.

Friday, a little after one in the morning. Fourteen clips deleted. User: Tamara.

At one in the morning on Friday, while I was lying in a hospital bed with a tube in my arm, my wife had sat somewhere with her phone and erased everything the kitchen camera saw on Thursday night.

She erased it from the cloud. From the app. From the place she knew about.

She didn't erase the black box in the basement, because she didn't know there was a black box in the basement.

And the black box only keeps thirty days before it starts writing over itself.

I was sitting there doing the math on that, under the parking lot light, when my phone buzzed on the seat. A text. From Tamara's number. The protection order said she couldn't contact me either, which I guess she'd forgotten.

"Why were you laughing?"

I looked at it for a long time. Then I put the phone face down on the seat, and started the truck, and drove to the only man in Boise I knew who'd understand what I was about to ask.

Phil Cordero lives in a split-level out in Garden City with a bass boat in the driveway that he hasn't put in the water since 2019. He spent twenty-six years as a detective with the Ada County Sheriff's Office, retired with bad knees and a good pension, and now he spends his days telling the guys at the Ace Hardware which wrench they need. He's 63, and he's the only person I know who reads the whole newspaper, including the obituaries, out loud.

He opened the door in sweatpants and a Boise State hoodie, took one look at the duffel bag, and stepped aside.

"Couch is made up," he said. "Beer's in the door of the fridge. Tell me while I'm still sober enough to be useful."

I told him. The kitchen, the high five, the bruise, the deputy, the date. I showed him the picture of the hospital band. I showed him the text, At Daphne's tonight. I showed him the activity log.

Phil put on his reading glasses for the activity log. He read it twice. Then he took the glasses off and set them on his knee.

"Fourteen clips."

"Fourteen."

"At one in the morning."

"While I was on morphine."

"Huh." Phil scratched his jaw. "You know what that is, in my old line of work?"

"What?"

"That's what we call a confession that hasn't happened yet."

"She'll say she deleted them because they were nothing. Her in a bathrobe. The cat."

"You don't have a cat."

"She doesn't know that's the part that matters."

Phil laughed so hard he had to put his beer down. "Okay. Okay. Now tell me about this box."

"Network video recorder. Four-terabyte drive. It sits next to the furnace on a shelf I built. Every camera writes to it locally, same time it writes to the cloud. If she deleted from the app, she deleted the cloud copy. The local copy's still on the drive."

"For how long?"

"Thirty days, then it starts writing over the oldest stuff. So I've got till the second week of next month. Unless she finds it."

"Does she go in the basement?"

"Twice a year. Christmas decorations."

"And Christmas is in five weeks." Phil leaned back. "And if somebody tells her what you are, Lorenzo, she's going to start wondering what's plugged into what."

"That's what I'm worried about."

"Well, you can't go get it."

"I know."

"I'm serious. You set one foot on that property and you violate the order, and then it doesn't matter what's on the box. You're the guy who broke a protection order. Judges hate that more than they hate liars."

"I know, Phil."

"So who's your lawyer?"

"I don't have a lawyer. I had a kidney stone."

"You've got a lawyer now." He picked his phone up off the arm of the couch and scrolled. "Marta Velez. She took me apart on the stand in 2011 on a burglary case and I've hated her ever since. Best there is."

"Will she see me on a weekend?"

"She'll see you Monday at eight, because I'm going to call her right now and tell her the guy she's meeting is the guy who fixed the Wi-Fi at her kid's school."

"I didn't fix the Wi-Fi at her kid's school."

"Lorenzo, you fixed the Wi-Fi at everybody's kid's school."

I slept on Phil's couch that night with my boots by the door, and I'll tell you, I didn't sleep much. Around two in the morning I sat up in the dark and looked at my phone, and there were three more texts from Tamara's number. Why were you laughing. Answer me. You're not going to scare me. I didn't answer any of them. I took a screenshot of each one and put them in a folder with the hospital band.

Marta Velez's office was on the fourth floor of a brick building on Bannock Street, with a view of the Capitol dome and a dying ficus in the corner. She was in her late fifties, five feet tall in heels, with gray hair cut short and reading glasses on a beaded chain. She read the protection order petition all the way through before she said a word to me.

"Your wife says you grabbed her arm Thursday at ten p.m. and threw her into the pantry door."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Did you?"

"I was in the ER at Saint Al's from about a quarter to nine Thursday night until six the next morning."

"Can you prove it?"

I put the picture of the wristband on her desk. Then I put a release form next to it that I'd printed at Phil's.

"I already signed a records release. The hospital will send everything straight to you. Admission, discharge, the CT scan, the nurse's notes. A custodian named Felix Ortega drove me there in his own car. He'll say so."

Marta looked at the picture of the band for a long moment.

"Mr. Gill," she said, "most of the men who sit in that chair want to tell me how much their wife is a terrible person. You came in with a records release."

"I work in IT. We like documentation."

She almost smiled. "Then here's mine. Number one. You do not go to that house. You do not drive by that house. You do not text her back, even to say stop texting me, because she's the one violating the order and I want her doing it alone. Number two, I'm sending a preservation letter to your wife and her lawyer today, telling them that any video from that house is evidence in a pending case and if anything gets deleted, the judge is going to hear about it."

"Won't that tip her off to the box?"

"It'll tip her off that video matters. She already knows that. She's the one who deleted it." Marta wrote something on a yellow pad. "Number three. The order lets you go back once with a deputy to get your personal property. It's called a civil standby. You're entitled to your work equipment. Is that recorder work equipment?"

"I bought it with my own money. It's on a shelf I built. It's not hers."

"Then on the property list, I want it written as exactly what it is. 'One network video recorder, basement, next to furnace, property of respondent.' "Then we go to the hearing Friday with a hospital band, a records release and a custodian named Felix." She took her glasses off and let them hang. "Which, between you and me, Mr. Gill, is more than most people walk in there with."

I took it.

"Lorenzo." Russ sounded like a man who'd rather be doing anything else. "Listen. I just got a call from HR."

"Okay."

Anybody with an active protection order involving violence can't be on school grounds until it's resolved. They're putting you on paid leave as of right now."

"I understand."

"I'm sorry. I know you. I know this is crap."

"Russ, the hearing's Friday."

"Then I'll see you Monday. I hope." He paused. "For what it's worth, the guys in the server room took up a collection. Somebody bought you a fruit basket. It's on your desk. I'm not allowed to let you come get it."

I hung up and sat there with the phone in my lap.

"Your job?" Marta said.

"Paid leave. My sister-in-law called HR."

Marta wrote that down too. "Good."

"Good?"

"She's making moves. People who are telling the truth don't need to make this many moves." She closed the yellow pad. "Go home, Mr. Gill. To your friend's home. Don't do anything I wouldn't do."

"Okay."

She stepped back. We went in. Dunn walked half a step behind me with his hand resting on his belt, easy, not threatening. The house smelled like somebody else's cologne. I always turn that light off. Tamara always says I'm the only man in Idaho who turns off a basement light.

I went down first. Dunn came behind me.

Evan Shipley was crouched in front of my furnace shelf with a Phillips screwdriver in his hand and the black box pulled halfway out onto the edge of the shelf, its cables hanging. He'd gotten one screw out of the back panel. He was working on the second.

I'm 54, and I sit in a chair most days, but I grew up pulling hay bales in Nampa and some things stay in your arms. I didn't swing at him. I grabbed the box. Both hands, right on top of his, and held on.

"Ma'am, step back from the stairs."

Out at the curb, a second cruiser had pulled up. They put Evan Shipley in the back of it, still in his credit union fleece, and as it pulled away I saw him through the back window with his forehead against the glass.

"I don't know yet," I said. "I was in the hospital."
