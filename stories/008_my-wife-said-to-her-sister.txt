Friday morning, a little after seven. Before I'd gotten home. Daphne walked in the back door in her real estate blazer with two coffees. Tamara pushed up her sleeve. Daphne whistled.

"That's a good one," Daphne said on the screen. "That's a really good one. Hold still."

She took pictures with her phone. Close. Far. With a ruler from the junk drawer, laid flat against Tamara's arm.

"You're sure about this?" Daphne said.

"He'll never fight it. He doesn't fight anything. He'll just move into some apartment and keep paying the mortgage, because that's who he is."

"And the video?"

"Gone. I deleted it last night." Tamara tapped her phone. "Nothing from yesterday exists anymore. Just my arm."

Daphne set down the ruler and raised her hand, and Tamara slapped it.

Alcala clicked the screen off.

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That second clip," Marta said finally, very quietly, "is the best thing anyone has ever shown me."

"Your sister-in-law just became an accessory to a false report," Alcala said to me. "And Mr. Shipley just became my suspect in a domestic battery." She looked at me. "You okay, Mr. Gill?"

"I'm fine."

"You don't look fine."

"I'm 54 and I just found out my wife's been sleeping with a credit union for two years," I said. "I'm about as fine as that gets."

Phil laughed into his hand, and then all four of us were laughing in that little room like idiots. It was that or the other thing.

"Hearing's tomorrow at nine?"

"Courtroom 3," Marta said.

"I'll be in the back row," Alcala said. "With a warrant."

Friday morning, six days after that kitchen, I wore my one good suit and sat at the respondent's table in Courtroom 3 of the Ada County Courthouse with Marta on my left. My cheek had gone from purple to the yellow-green of an old banana. I didn't cover it. Marta said don't.

Tamara sat at the other table in a navy dress with long sleeves and her lawyer, a man named Carl Benning in a gray suit who'd billed her, Marta told me later, for fourteen hours that week. Daphne sat in the first row behind her, and next to Daphne, in the second row, wearing a sport coat and sunglasses pushed up on his head like he was at a ballgame, sat Evan Shipley.

He'd come to support her. Out on bond for hitting me, and he sat right behind her.

Detective Alcala was in the back row in a blue blazer, with Deputy Dunn next to her.

The judge was a woman in her sixties named Judge Hollister with half-glasses on the end of her nose and a voice like a school librarian.

Tamara testified first. She cried in the right places. She described Thursday night. She said I came home from work angry, that I'd been drinking, that I grabbed her arm in the kitchen around ten and threw her into the pantry door. Benning held up Daphne's photos, the ones with the ruler, and the judge looked at them and made a note.

"Mrs. Gill," Benning said, "had your husband ever done anything like this before?"

"Not like this. But he has a temper. He keeps it inside. He just gets quiet and stares at you."

"Thank you. Nothing further."

Marta stood up. She didn't hurry.

"Mrs. Gill. You said this happened at about ten p.m. on Thursday the ninth."

"Yes."

"You're sure about the time."

"Yes. I remember because the news was starting."

"And your husband was in the kitchen with you."

"Yes."

"Your Honor, I'd like to hand the court and counsel Respondent's Exhibit A." Marta walked a stapled packet up to the clerk. "Certified records from Saint Alphonsus Regional Medical Center. Mr. Gill was admitted to the emergency department at eight forty-four p.m. on Thursday the ninth with a kidney stone, and discharged at five fifty-eight a.m. on Friday. Page three is the nursing log. He was checked on every hour. At ten p.m. a nurse named Carla Ruiz wrote that he was 'resting, asking for more ice chips.'"

She set a small plastic bag on the table in front of the judge.

"And this is the patient band they cut off his wrist when he left."

Judge Hollister picked up the bag and looked at the band for a long moment over her half-glasses.

Tamara's mouth opened. Nothing came out. She turned around in her chair to look at Daphne. Daphne was looking at the floor.

Benning stood up. "Your Honor, my client may simply be mistaken about the time. It's been a very traumatic—"

He stopped. He was standing half turned toward the gallery, and I watched his eyes land on the second row, on Evan Shipley's face, and stay there. Benning had seen the police report on the basement. He'd seen the name. He'd just never seen the man, and the man had come in and sat down right behind his client.

"Mr. Benning?" the judge said. "You were saying?"

"I'd like a moment with my client, Your Honor."

"You can have a moment after Ms. Velez finishes."

"Your Honor," Marta said, "if counsel's going to argue his client got the time wrong, then I'd like to show the court what was happening in that kitchen at the right time. Respondent's Exhibit B is a video recording from a camera in the Gills' kitchen, collected by court order and preserved by the Ada County Sheriff's Office. Detective Alcala is here to authenticate it."

"Objection," Benning said, and his voice wasn't steady. "We haven't—"

"You received a copy at eight this morning, counselor," Alcala said from the back row.

The judge looked at Benning over her glasses. "Overruled. Play it."

They played it. Not all of it. Just enough. Evan's hand on her arm, the bang of the pantry door, and his voice, filling up Courtroom 3, clear as a church bell.

"Tell everybody he did it. He's never home, he's got no witnesses, it's your word against a guy who talks to computers."

Then the Friday morning clip. Daphne. The ruler. The high five.

In the first row, Daphne stood up, sat down, and stood up again, and then just stood there with her purse in both hands like she didn't know which door to use.

Behind her, Evan Shipley had pulled his sunglasses down over his eyes, which in a courtroom at nine in the morning made him look exactly like what he was.

Judge Hollister took off her half-glasses and set them on the bench.

"The temporary protection order against Mr. Gill is dissolved, effective immediately. The petition is dismissed. Mr. Gill may return to his residence today." She turned to Tamara. "Mrs. Gill, I am referring this transcript and this exhibit to the Ada County Prosecutor's Office for review for false report and perjury. You'll want to speak with counsel about that. I'd suggest new counsel."

Benning was already putting papers in his briefcase.

"Detective Alcala," the judge said. "I believe you have business in my courtroom."

"Yes, Your Honor."

Alcala and Dunn walked up the aisle together to the second row.

"Evan Shipley," Alcala said, "you're under arrest for domestic battery against Tamara Gill on the ninth, and for conspiracy to file a false police report. Stand up, please."

"This is insane," Evan said. "She pulled away. I didn't do anything. Tam, tell them."

Tamara didn't turn around.

"Tam!"

"Hands behind your back, sir," Dunn said. "You remember how."

They walked him out the side door. Halfway there, he stopped and looked back over his shoulder at Tamara. She was sitting perfectly still at her table, looking straight ahead at the empty witness box, and she never turned her head.

I picked up my folder and walked out into the hallway with Marta. Phil was waiting by the drinking fountain, grinning.

I'd made it maybe twenty feet when I heard her heels behind me.

"Lorenzo. Wait. Please."

Marta stepped in front of me. "Mrs. Gill, talk to a lawyer before you say one word to my client."

"I just need one minute. He owes me one minute."

I put my hand on Marta's arm. "It's okay."

Tamara stopped in front of me. Up close, she looked smaller than she had in the courtroom. Her eyes were red. She'd pushed her sleeve up without thinking, and there was the bruise, almost gone now, just a yellow shadow above her elbow.

"I didn't know he'd do that," she said. "In the basement. I didn't tell him to go down there. He just said he'd take care of it."

"Okay."

"And Thursday, he scared me. He did. He hurt me. And I was scared of what you'd say, and Daphne said, she said nobody would ever believe a man like you would..." Her voice cracked. "I wasn't thinking. I was so scared, Lorenzo. You have to understand that."

"I understand you were scared."

"So you'll tell them? The prosecutor? You'll tell them you don't want to press anything? You'll tell them I made a mistake?"

"You didn't make a mistake. You made a plan. I watched you high five it."

"I'm your wife."

"You were my wife on Thursday too."

She grabbed my sleeve. Her fingers were cold.

"Please. Twenty-six years. I'll do anything. I'll go to counseling. I'll sign anything you want. Just don't let them do this to me. You're the only one who can make it stop."

I looked down at her hand on my sleeve. Then I looked at her.

"Tam, on Saturday you told your sister I'd lose everything in court," I said. "You were half right. Somebody did."

I took her hand off my sleeve, gently, the way you take a kid's hand off a hot stove, and I walked down the hall to the elevator where Phil was holding the door.
