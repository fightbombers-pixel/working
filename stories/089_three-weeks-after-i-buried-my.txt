"I just have to play nice a couple more months. Once his dad's estate clears and it's in our account, I file and walk with half. He's so wrecked he'll believe anything."

My wife said that into her phone, on speaker, standing at our kitchen island in Beckley, West Virginia, three weeks after I buried my father. She was stirring a pot of chili. A man's voice on the other end laughed, and three people heard it.

Her. Him. And me, standing in the laundry room off the garage with my boots still on, because I'd come home four hours early from an inspection that got canceled when a section foreman broke his ankle.

"Then it's you and me," the man said. "Myrtle Beach, babe. Ocean view."

"Ocean view," my wife said, and she laughed too.

My father's estate came to about six hundred and ten thousand dollars. Two hundred and twelve acres of timber in Raleigh County, a paid-off farmhouse, and a savings account he'd been adding to since Eisenhower. He'd worked forty-one years underground and never bought himself a new truck.

Half of that was what she meant.

I stood in the laundry room and listened to the chili bubble. Then I did something I'd learned to do in thirty years of walking into places where the air might kill you. I backed up. Quietly. I went out through the garage, eased the side door shut, walked back to my truck in the driveway, and slammed the truck door as hard as I could.

Then I came in the front door, loud, stomping my boots on the mat.

"Misty? I'm home early."

By the time I got to the kitchen her phone was face-down on the counter and she was smiling at me.

"Hey, baby. What happened?"

I didn't answer right away. I took the thick brown folder out from under my arm, the one marked ESTATE OF THURMAN BISHOP that I'd been carrying everywhere for three weeks, and I set it on the island right in front of her, next to the chili.

"Lawyer called," I said. "Estate's going to take a while longer than we thought."

She looked at the folder. Her smile didn't move, but her hand on the spoon stopped stirring.

Eleven weeks later, she'd be sitting across a conference table from me, listening to her own lawyer say a word she'd never learned. Commingled.

What she didn't know was what my father had written in the margin of page four of his will, in his own shaky handwriting, a month before he died.

My name is Randall Bishop. I'm 57, and I'm a federal mine safety inspector out of the Beckley field office. I've been in and out of coal mines in southern West Virginia since I was twenty-six, first as a miner and then as the guy with the clipboard everybody hates to see.

Here's what that job teaches you. The air that kills you doesn't smell like anything. You can't see it. You can't feel it. You test it, every time, even when you're sure. Especially when you're sure. Hold on to that.

Because I'd been breathing the air in my own house for nineteen years and never once tested it.

"How much longer?" Misty asked.

"Roscoe says probate in Raleigh County runs ninety days minimum. Then he's got to close the estate account and cut the checks."

"Ninety days." She went back to stirring. "Well. That's fine. It's not like we're in a hurry."

"No," I said. "We're not in a hurry."

She came around the island and put her arms around me from behind and laid her cheek against my back. She smelled like chili powder and the vanilla lotion she always wore.

"I'm so sorry, baby," she said. "I know how hard this is. I'm here. Whatever you need."

"I know you are," I said.

I stood there and let her hold me, and I looked at the kitchen window over the sink, where her phone was reflected, face-down on the counter. And I thought about my father, who'd died in the VA hospital three blocks from here with my hand on his arm, and who'd told me in the last coherent conversation we ever had that he'd fixed things so I'd be all right.

I hadn't known what he meant.

I was starting to.

That night, after Misty went to bed, I sat in my truck in the driveway with the dome light off and called my partner at the field office, Junior Pettry.

Junior is forty, built like a fire hydrant, and has been my partner on inspections for eight years. He talks a mile a minute and knows everybody in four counties, mostly because he's related to half of them.

"Bishop," he said. "It's eleven o'clock. Somebody die? Oh, crap, I'm sorry, man, I didn't mean..."

"It's fine. Junior, you know a man around here who says babe a lot, has an ocean view in Myrtle Beach on his mind, and is sleeping with my wife?"

There was a long silence on the line.

"Randall," Junior said carefully. "Are you okay?"

"I came home early today. I heard her on speaker."

"What'd she say?"

I told him. Word for word. It wasn't hard. I'd been hearing it in my head for six hours.

Junior was quiet for a while. When he talked again, his voice was different. Lower. No jokes.

"I don't know who the guy is," he said. "But my cousin Darnell's wife does hair at the place on Robert C. Byrd Drive where Misty goes. I can ask."

"Don't ask anybody anything yet."

"Then what do you want me to do?"

"Nothing," I said. "I just needed to say it out loud to somebody who wasn't her."

"You going to confront her?"

"No."

"No? Randall. She's planning to take half your dad's money."

"She has to get it into our account first," I said. "That's what she said. Once it's in our account."

Junior was quiet again. I could almost hear him thinking.

"So don't put it in your account," he said.

"I'm going to see Roscoe in the morning."

Roscoe Lilly has been practicing law in Beckley since before I was born. He did my father's will, and my father's father's will, and he has an office on Main Street over a sporting goods store with a stuffed bobcat in the window. He's 79, wears suspenders and a bow tie, and talks so slow you'd think he was paid by the hour. He is.

He was also one of my father's oldest friends. They'd played rook every Thursday night at the VFW for thirty years.

"Randall," he said, waving me into a leather chair that wheezed when I sat in it. "How are you holding up, son?"

"Not great, Roscoe. I need to ask you some questions about the estate. And about divorce."

His bushy white eyebrows went up and stayed up.

I told him. I didn't leave anything out. When I got to the part about Myrtle Beach, he took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes.

"Well," he said. "Thurman always did say you had a gift for marrying above your judgment."

"Is she right? If the money goes in our account, does she get half?"

Roscoe put his glasses back on and folded his hands on his belly.

"In West Virginia," he said, "property you inherit is your separate property. It doesn't belong to the marriage. If you get divorced, it stays yours. That's the law."

"Then how does she plan to walk with half?"

"Because there's a catch, son. It stays yours only as long as you keep it separate. You take that inheritance and put it in a joint checking account with her name on it, and you pay the mortgage out of it, and you buy groceries out of it, and pretty soon it's all mixed up in the same pot as the marriage money. Lawyers call that commingling. And once it's commingled, a judge can call the whole pot marital property and split it down the middle."

"So once it's in our account..."

"She files and walks with half," Roscoe said. "Your wife has been reading up on this. Or somebody's been reading up for her."

I sat back in the wheezing chair.

"So I don't put it in our account."

"You're the executor," Roscoe said. "When we close the estate, the checks come to you as the sole beneficiary. Not to you and Misty. To Randall T. Bishop. You open an account at a different bank, in your name alone, and you put it there, and you don't pay so much as a light bill out of it. You don't add her as a beneficiary. You don't add her as a signer. You don't even tell her what bank it's in."

"She's going to ask."

"Then you tell her the truth, son. You tell her it cleared." He smiled a little. "Thurman always said the best lie is the truth told to somebody who isn't listening."

I nodded. Then I asked the other thing.

"Roscoe. My dad told me, right before he died, that he'd fixed things so I'd be all right. Do you know what he meant?"

Roscoe looked at me for a long moment. Then he got up, slowly, and went to an old green filing cabinet in the corner, and opened the bottom drawer.

He came back with the original will and a long white envelope, sealed, with my name on it in my father's handwriting.

"He came in here in March," Roscoe said. "Walked up those stairs himself, with his oxygen tank. He changed one paragraph of his will and he wrote something in the margin, and then he wrote you this letter." He set the envelope on the desk between us. "He told me to give it to you only if you ever came in here asking about divorce."

I looked at the envelope. My father's handwriting, all capitals, the way he wrote everything.

FOR RANDALL. ONLY IF YOU NEED IT.

"He knew," I said.

"I couldn't tell you what he knew," Roscoe said. "Lawyer-client. But he walked up those stairs with an oxygen tank, son. A man doesn't do that for nothing."

I reached for the envelope. Then I stopped.

"Not yet," I said.

"No?"

"I want to see it first. With my own eyes. Whatever it is." I stood up. "I test the air, Roscoe. I don't take anybody's word for it. Not even his."

Roscoe slid the envelope back in the drawer and closed it.

"It'll be here," he said.

I was halfway down the stairs, past the bobcat in the window, when my phone buzzed. A text from Misty.

Hey baby, a friend of mine is a realtor and he's interested in your dad's land. Can he call you? He's real nice. His name is Trey.

I didn't answer the text. I drove home and found Misty in the kitchen with her reading glasses on, a legal pad in front of her and a calculator, which in nineteen years of marriage I had seen her use exactly never.

She flipped the legal pad over when I came in.

"There you are," she said. "Did you get my text? About Trey?"

"I got it."

"He's so nice, Randall. He helped my sister Dena find her condo. He says land in Raleigh County is a hard sell right now, with the mines closing, and a lot of people who inherit land get stuck paying taxes on it for years. He said he could take it off your hands quick. Clean."

"Take it off my hands."

"Find you a buyer. Whatever." She took her glasses off and came around the counter. "Baby, you've got enough on your plate. You don't need to be dealing with timber and surveyors and property taxes. Let somebody help you."

"Dad loved that land," I said.

"I know he did." She put her hand on my chest. "But your dad's gone, honey. And you can't keep everything."

I looked down at her hand on my chest. Small, with the wedding ring I'd bought her at a jewelry store in Charleston in 2006. I'd paid it off in installments.

"There's something else," she said. "About the money. When the estate closes."

"What about it?"

"Well, I was thinking. Our joint checking at City National is really the safest place for it. It's FDIC insured. And it's easier, you know, if it's all in one spot. Then we can sit down together and figure out a plan. Pay off the house. Maybe take a trip. You need a trip, baby. You haven't had a vacation in four years."

"Where would we go?"

"I don't know. The beach?" She smiled. "Somewhere with an ocean view."

I want to tell you I felt something big right then. Rage, or heartbreak, or something you'd see in a movie. Mostly what I felt was tired. The way you feel at the end of a double shift when the mantrip finally brings you up to daylight and you realize how dark it was down there.

"Okay," I said.

"Okay? You'll put it in the joint account?"

"I'll put it where it's safest," I said.

She kissed me on the cheek. "That's my guy."

My phone rang twenty minutes later while I was out in the garage pretending to sort fishing tackle. A 304 number I didn't know.

"Mr. Bishop? Trey Bostic, Bostic Realty. Misty's friend. First of all, I'm so sorry about your father."

I knew the voice before he got to the second sentence. Smooth, a little bit Charleston, a little bit used car lot. The voice that had said Myrtle Beach, babe, ocean view.

I sat down on an upside-down five-gallon bucket.

"Thank you," I said.

"Look, I know this is a hard time. I don't want to push. But Misty mentioned you might be looking at selling the property out on Clear Fork, and I've got a client who's interested. Hunting club out of Virginia. They'd pay cash. Close in thirty days. No inspections, no hassle."

"How much?"

"Eleven hundred an acre."

I did the math in my head. Two hundred and thirty-three thousand dollars for two hundred and twelve acres of land my grandfather bought in 1946.

"That seems low," I said.

"Mr. Bishop, with all due respect, it's Raleigh County. The coal's gone. There's no market. Eleven hundred is a gift."

"I'd like to walk the land with you before I decide anything."

"Absolutely. Absolutely. How's Saturday?"

"Saturday's fine."

"Great. Great. And Mr. Bishop? Again, sorry for your loss. Misty says you've been taking it real hard."

"Has she," I said.

The Clear Fork property is at the end of a gravel road off Route 3, past an old church and a trailer with a hand-painted sign that says FRESH EGGS. My grandfather bought it with mustering-out pay after the war. My father cut timber off it every ten years like clockwork and paid for my college with the 1984 cut.

Junior rode out with me Saturday morning. He brought his cousin Hobie, who is a licensed timber cruiser for a lumber company in Rainelle and had agreed to walk the property with us for a case of beer and a tank of gas.

Trey Bostic was already there, leaning against a white Range Rover in a pastel quarter-zip pullover and clean khakis, like he was going to play golf instead of walk two hundred acres of hollow. He was maybe forty-five. Tan in November, which in West Virginia means a tanning bed.

He shook my hand with both of his.

"Mr. Bishop. So sorry for your loss."

"You said that on the phone."

"Well, I mean it." His eyes went to Junior and Hobie. "Who are your friends?"

"Junior's my partner at work. Hobie's a timber cruiser."

His smile got a little smaller. "A timber cruiser."

"I figured as long as we're walking it, I'd like to know what's on it."

We walked for three hours. Hobie went tree to tree with a little measuring tape and a clicker counter, muttering to himself. He'd wrap the tape around a trunk, squint up at the canopy, click the counter, write something in a waterproof notebook. Trey followed along behind, getting mud on his khakis and checking his phone every five minutes.

Around noon, we came out on the ridge where you can see down three hollows at once. My father used to bring me up there to eat bologna sandwiches when I was a kid.

Hobie closed his notebook.

"Randall," he said. "You've got about a hundred and forty acres of mature hardwood on here. Red oak, white oak, poplar, some cherry up the north slope that'll make a furniture mill cry. I'd put the standing timber alone at three-eighty, maybe four hundred thousand. That's before the land."

Junior whistled.

Trey laughed. "That's a cruiser's number. Those guys always come in high."

"I work for a mill," Hobie said. "We come in low. That's how we make money."

Trey turned to me with his hands spread. "Mr. Bishop. Listen. Timber's a long game. You'd have to log it, you'd have to deal with contractors, the price swings. My client's offering you cash today."

"Eleven hundred an acre."

"Cash today."

"For land with four hundred thousand dollars of timber on it."

"Hey, the market is what the market is."

"Who's your client, Mr. Bostic?"

"I can't disclose that."

"A hunting club from Virginia, you said."

"That's right."

"What's the name of it?"

He hesitated. Just a half second. But I've spent thirty years watching section foremen hesitate when I ask them where their methane readings are, and a half second is all you need.

"I'd have to check with them," he said.

"You do that."

He looked at me differently then. The sorry-for-your-loss look was gone. What was underneath was colder.

"Mr. Bishop," he said, "with respect, Misty says you're not doing too well. Not sleeping. Not eating. A man in that condition shouldn't be making big decisions about two hundred acres of property. He might make a mistake he'd regret."

"Misty says a lot of things to you, doesn't she, Trey?"

His jaw tightened.

"We're friends."

"Real good friends," I said. "Ocean view friends."

For a second, nobody said anything. Up on the ridge, a crow went off somewhere down the hollow. Trey Bostic's face went still in the way a man's face goes still when he's trying to remember exactly what he said and who might have heard it.

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said.

"Sure you don't."

He took his phone out of his pocket like it had buzzed, though it hadn't, and walked off down the logging road toward his Range Rover. Halfway down, he got on the phone with somebody. The wind carried his voice back up the ridge.

"No, babe, listen to me," he was saying. "He knows something. I don't know what. He brought a cruiser."

Junior looked at me.

"Babe," he said.

"I heard."

Junior's cousin Hobie, who knew nothing about any of this, looked from one of us to the other.

"Did I just mess something up?" he asked.

"No, Hobie," I said. "You just told me what my dad's land is worth. That's all I wanted to know."

Down at the bottom of the logging road, Trey Bostic's Range Rover spun its wheels in the mud, caught, and fishtailed off toward Route 3.

Misty's sister Dena showed up at the field office the following Wednesday.

Dena is three years younger than Misty, works at the DMV in Beckley, and is the only person in Misty's family who ever once asked me how I was doing and waited for the answer. She came into the lobby in her DMV polo with a purse clutched in front of her like a shield.

"Can we talk somewhere?" she said. "Not here."

We went out to her car in the parking lot and sat in it with the heater running.

"Trey Bostic," she said, staring straight ahead through the windshield. "I'm the one who introduced them. Two years ago. He sold me my condo. I had no idea."

"How long, Dena?"

"Since last Christmas, I think. Maybe before." She took a breath. "Randall, I need to tell you something and I need you to know I didn't know until Sunday."

"Okay."

"She's been planning this since February. Since your dad's diagnosis. She told me Sunday, at Mom's, after a couple glasses of wine. She laughed about it. She said Thurman was the best thing that ever happened to her retirement fund."

I didn't say anything.

"And the land," Dena said. "Trey's not working for any hunting club. He set up a company. Bostic Land Holdings. He's going to buy your dad's land himself for eleven hundred an acre, and he's already got a buyer lined up at twenty-eight hundred. Some natural gas pipeline outfit that needs a right-of-way through Clear Fork. He's going to flip it in a week. Misty gets half the profit." She turned and looked at me for the first time. "She showed me the email on her phone. She was proud of it."

I sat there with my hands on my knees.

"Why are you telling me this, Dena?"

"Because your dad used to fix my car for free," she said. "For ten years. Every time it broke down, I'd call Thurman, and he'd come out with his tools, and he'd never let me pay him a dime. And she's going to spit on his grave for a beach condo." Her chin wobbled. "I'm done covering for her."

"Would you say that to a lawyer?"

"I'd say it to a judge."

Seventy-nine days after my father's funeral, Roscoe Lilly closed the Estate of Thurman Bishop and handed me two checks across his desk. One for the savings, one for the farmhouse, which had sold to a schoolteacher from Shady Spring. The land stayed in my name. Six hundred and ten thousand dollars, give or take, all of it payable to Randall T. Bishop. Nobody else.

I drove to a credit union in Princeton, thirty miles away, where nobody knew me, and opened an account in my name alone. No joint owner. No beneficiary. I deposited both checks. I didn't so much as buy a pack of gum with it.

That night, Misty met me at the door with a glass of bourbon.

"Well?" she said. "Did Roscoe close it?"

"He closed it."

"And the money? Did it clear?"

I took the bourbon and looked at her over the top of the glass.

"It cleared," I said.

She smiled the biggest smile I'd seen on her face in a year. She kissed me. She said she was proud of me. She said she was going to make my favorite dinner, pot roast, the way my mother used to make it.

The next morning at a quarter after ten, a process server walked into the Beckley field office of the Mine Safety and Health Administration, in front of my supervisor and nine coworkers and Junior Pettry eating a honey bun at his desk, and handed me a petition for divorce.

The whole office watched me read the first page. My supervisor, Gloria Whitt, who has been running that office for twelve years and has seen men cry over citations, stood in her doorway with her coffee mug and didn't say a word.

Junior put his honey bun down.

"Is that what I think it is?" he said.

"It's what we thought it would be."

"She waited one day," Junior said. "One day after the checks."

"She wanted to be sure it cleared."

My phone buzzed. A text from Misty.

Sorry baby. It's nothing personal. Half of 610 is 305. Get a lawyer.

I showed it to Junior. He read it twice and handed it back.

"You want me to say something about her?" he asked. "Because I've got some things lined up."

"No," I said. "I want you to cover my two o'clock at the Marfork portal. I've got to go see Roscoe."

They came after my job four days later.

Gloria Whitt called me into her office on Monday morning and shut the door, which she does maybe twice a year. She sat down behind her desk and folded her hands on a printout.

"Randall, the district manager in Mount Hope got a phone call Friday afternoon," she said. "A man who wouldn't give his name. He said you've been drinking since your father passed. He said you showed up to an inspection at the Marfork portal smelling like whiskey, and that you're not fit to be underground making safety calls."

I sat very still.

"I wasn't at Marfork Friday," I said. "Junior was. I was at Roscoe Lilly's office."

"I know. I checked the schedule." She tapped the printout. "But a complaint like that, the district has to look at it. You know the rules. Anybody who signs off on a mine's air has to be fit to do it."

"Then test me."

"Randall, you don't have to..."

"Gloria. Test me. Today. And pull my detector logs for the last ninety days. Every reading I've signed. Every portal. Every time stamp."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she picked up her phone.

The nurse from the occupational health office came over at eleven with a breathalyzer. I blew a zero-point-zero-zero in front of Gloria and the nurse and Junior, who insisted on watching. Gloria pulled ninety days of my detector logs and every one of my inspection reports, and she sent them up to Mount Hope with a memo that said, in her words, that she'd stake her own badge on mine.

Two days later, she called me back into her office.

"District's closing it," she said. "Unfounded. It's in your file that way. In writing."

"Thank you, Gloria."

"Don't thank me yet." She turned her monitor around so I could see it. "The district logs every incoming number. That anonymous call came from a landline. I looked it up." She pointed at the screen. "It's registered to Bostic Realty on Kanawha Street."

Junior, in the doorway, said a word I won't repeat.

"He wanted me fired before the settlement conference," I said. "A man who loses his federal job can't afford to fight about land."

"Well," Gloria said, "now it's in a federal file that a realtor on Kanawha Street made a false report against a mine safety inspector. I'd hold on to that, if I were you."

I did.

Gretchen Blankenship had a reputation in southern West Virginia. She was the lawyer wives hired when they wanted the house, the truck and the bass boat, and she usually got all three. Her office was in a new brick building out by the mall in Beckley, glass doors, a receptionist with headphones, a conference room with a long walnut table and a view of the Applebee's parking lot.

Two weeks after I was served, Roscoe and I walked into that conference room at nine in the morning for what Gretchen's letter called a settlement conference.

Misty was already there, sitting next to Gretchen, in a navy blazer I'd never seen. She didn't look at me. Gretchen did. She was about fifty, sharp black bob, reading glasses pushed up on her head, and the look of a woman who already knew the answer to every question she was going to ask.

"Mr. Lilly," she said to Roscoe. "Always a pleasure. I didn't know you still did divorces."

"I don't," Roscoe said, lowering himself into a chair with a grunt. "I do estates. This one's both."

Gretchen opened a folder.

"I'll keep this brief," she said. "My client was married to Mr. Bishop for nineteen years. During the marriage, Mr. Bishop received an inheritance of approximately six hundred and ten thousand dollars, which my client understands was deposited into the parties' joint account at City National. Under West Virginia law, inherited funds commingled with marital funds become marital property subject to equitable distribution. We're asking for half. Three hundred and five thousand dollars. And, because Mr. Bishop has refused a reasonable cash offer on the Clear Fork property, we'll be asking the court to order the land sold and the proceeds split as well."

She closed the folder and looked at me over her reading glasses.

"Or we can save everybody a lot of time and money, and Mr. Bishop can write a check."

Roscoe didn't say anything. He reached into his old leather briefcase, took out a single sheet of paper, and slid it across the walnut table to Gretchen with two fingers.

"What's this?"

"Statement from the Joint Checking account at City National," Roscoe said. "Randall and Misty Bishop. Last ninety days. You'll want to look at the deposits."

Gretchen pulled her glasses down onto her nose and read it.

"Two paychecks a month from MSHA," Roscoe said slowly, like he was reading a bedtime story. "A paycheck every two weeks from Dr. Pauley's dental office. Some cash deposits I imagine Mrs. Bishop can explain. Not one dollar from the Estate of Thurman Bishop."

Gretchen turned to Misty. Misty was staring at the paper.

"That's not right," Misty said. "He told me it cleared."

"It did clear," Roscoe said, and he slid a second sheet across the table. "Into this account. Pinnacle Federal Credit Union, Princeton. Opened the same day the estate closed. Sole owner, Randall T. Bishop. No beneficiary, no joint signer. Two deposits from the estate, totaling six hundred nine thousand, eight hundred and twelve dollars. No withdrawals. Not one. He hasn't bought a cup of coffee with it."

Gretchen read the second sheet. Then she took off her glasses.

"Misty," she said, very quietly, "you told me it went into the joint account."

"He said it cleared!"

"Did he say where?"

Misty opened her mouth. Then she closed it.

"I'll save you some research, Ms. Blankenship," Roscoe said. "It hasn't been commingled. It's never touched marital money. It's his separate property, same as the day his daddy died."

"Inheritance can still be considered in the overall equitable distribution," Gretchen said, but you could tell she was saying it the way a pitcher throws to first base when he knows the runner's already safe.

"Well, then," Roscoe said, "you'll want to look at page four."

He took my father's will out of his briefcase, the original, with the blue backing, and opened it to page four. He turned it around so Gretchen could see.

"Paragraph six," he said. "Read it out loud, if you don't mind. My eyes aren't what they were."

Gretchen looked at him for a long moment. Then she read it.

"I give, devise and bequeath all the rest and residue of my estate, real and personal, to my son, Randall Thurman Bishop, as his sole and separate property, free of any claim of any present or future spouse, and it is my express intention that no part of this bequest ever be treated as marital property in any proceeding whatsoever."

She stopped. She was looking at the margin.

"There's handwriting," she said.

"There is," Roscoe said. "Read that too."

In the margin, in shaky capitals, in blue ballpoint pen, it said: SEPARATE MEANS SEPARATE. NOT ONE ACRE. T.B. 3/14.

Gretchen read it out loud. Misty's hands came up off the table and then went back down again, flat, the way you'd steady yourself on a rocking boat.

"March fourteenth," I said. "Six weeks before he died. He walked up Roscoe's stairs with an oxygen tank to write that."

Nobody said anything.

"And this," Roscoe said, sliding over the last thing in his briefcase, "is a sworn affidavit from Dena Wiley. Mrs. Bishop's sister. Regarding a conversation at Mrs. Bishop's mother's house in which Mrs. Bishop described her plan to file for divorce once the estate cleared into the joint account. And regarding an email Mrs. Bishop showed her, from a Mr. Trey Bostic of Bostic Land Holdings LLC, regarding the purchase of the Clear Fork property for eleven hundred an acre and its resale to Mountaineer Gas Transmission for twenty-eight hundred an acre."

Gretchen read the affidavit. It was two pages. It took her a while.

"One more thing, while you're reading," Roscoe said, and he set down a single page on Mine Safety and Health Administration letterhead. "Two weeks ago, somebody called Randall's district manager and said he was drinking on the job. Federal office. Federal job. The complaint was closed as unfounded after Randall blew a zero in front of three witnesses. The call came from the landline at Bostic Realty."

Gretchen looked at the letterhead. Then she looked at Misty, and something in her face closed like a door.


When she was done, she set it down and turned to Misty.

"Is this true?"

"Dena's lying. Dena's always been jealous of me."

"Misty." Gretchen's voice had gone very flat. "Did your boyfriend try to buy your husband's land at a third of its value so he could flip it to a pipeline company and split the profit with you?"

"He's not my boyfriend," Misty said. "He's a friend."

"Ms. Bishop, I'm your lawyer. Is it true?"

Misty looked at me across the table. After nineteen years, I finally watched her try to find the right thing to say and come up empty.

Then she did the thing I'd seen her do a thousand times when she didn't know what to do next. She picked up her phone.

"I'm calling Trey," she said. "He'll explain it."

"Misty, I really wouldn't..." Gretchen started.

But Misty had already dialed, and she'd already put it on speaker, because that's how she'd talked on the phone for nineteen years, in the kitchen, at the store, in the car, on speaker, like the whole world was her living room.

It rang twice.

"Hey, babe." Trey Bostic's voice filled the conference room. Smooth. A little Charleston. "How'd it go? We get our three hundred?"

Misty's face went still.

"Trey, I'm in the meeting. You're on speaker."

There was a pause on the line.

"Who's there?"

"My lawyer. And Randall. And his lawyer." Her voice was shaking. "Trey, the money never went in the joint account. He put it in his own name. And the will says it's separate. And Dena signed something about the land and the pipeline."

The pause got longer.

"So there's no three hundred," Trey said.

"There's... I don't know what there is."

"And the land?"

"He's not selling it."

"Huh," Trey said. And then, in a voice I'd never heard him use, flat and practical, like a man canceling a cable subscription: "Well, then I don't really see what we're doing here, Misty."

"What do you mean?"

"I mean I've got a closing at eleven," Trey Bostic said, and the line went dead.

Misty sat there holding the phone. The screen said CALL ENDED, and she just kept looking at it, like maybe it would change.

Gretchen Blankenship closed her folder.

"Mr. Lilly," she said. "I think my client and I need a few minutes."

"Take all the time you need," Roscoe said.

He reached into his briefcase one more time, and he took out a long white envelope, sealed, and he set it on the table in front of me.

FOR RANDALL. ONLY IF YOU NEED IT.

"I think you need it now, son," Roscoe said.

I picked it up. My father's handwriting. All capitals. I slid my thumb under the flap and took out a single sheet of yellow legal paper, folded in thirds.

I read it to myself first. Then I read it again. The third time, I read it out loud, because I wanted her to hear it.

"Randall. I saw Misty's car at the Bostic Realty office on Kanawha Street twice in March. Wednesday nights. Your night shifts. I saw that white Range Rover in your driveway once too, when I drove by to drop off your mother's pie plate. I didn't know how to tell you. You were happy, or you looked it, and I was dying, and I didn't want the last thing I ever did to be breaking your heart. So I fixed it the only way a sick old man with an oxygen tank can. I wrote it down. If you're reading this, you found out on your own. I'm sorry, son. Don't sell the land. Your granddad bought it for you. Love, Dad."

I folded the letter back up along the same creases.

Across the table, Misty was crying. Real crying, the ugly kind, with her mascara running into the collar of the navy blazer.

"He knew," she said. "Thurman knew. All this time he knew, and he never said one word to me."

"He said it to me," I said.

"Randall. Please." She reached across the walnut table toward my hand. "I made a mistake. Trey was a mistake. I was scared, you were gone all the time, your dad was dying, and I didn't know what was going to happen to us. We can still fix this. I'll go to counseling. I'll do anything."

I looked at her hand on the table. The ring was still on it. I'd paid for that ring in nine installments.

"You told him I was so wrecked I'd believe anything," I said.

"I didn't mean it."

"You said it on speaker. In my kitchen. With the chili on the stove."

She pulled her hand back like the table was hot.

I stood up and put my father's letter in the inside pocket of my jacket, over my heart, where I keep the gas detector when I go underground.

"I test the air for a living, Misty," I said. "Dad just got to it first."
