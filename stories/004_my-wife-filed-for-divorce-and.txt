"I'm taking half. That prenup from your broke days won't save you."

My wife said it at my shop's thirtieth anniversary pancake breakfast, standing in bay three of Villa Diesel and Fleet in Springfield, Missouri, in front of thirty-one employees, about sixty customers and a griddle full of blueberry pancakes. She said it while a skinny process server in a Cardinals cap was still holding out the envelope to me. She'd timed it so the server would walk in right after my speech, right after everybody clapped.

Half of Villa Diesel was a little over two million dollars, the way the bank had valued it last spring. Thirty years ago, when Cheryl signed that prenup, half of my business was half of one wrecker with a bad transmission and a rented bay on Commercial Street.

I took the envelope from the server. I signed his little receipt on the hood of a Kenworth. Then I handed my spatula to my shop foreman, walked into my office, opened the old Mosler safe in the corner, and came back out with a yellowed copy of a document stapled in the corner with a staple that had rusted.

I held it out to the process server.

"Do me a favor, son. Take this to her lawyer. Tell him to read page four."

Nine days later, her lawyer would be sitting across a conference table from me with page four in his hand, and he'd need to ask his secretary for a glass of water.

What Cheryl didn't know was the name typed under the line on page eleven marked Counsel for Cheryl Ann Dobbs.

My name is Arturo Villa. I'm fifty-seven. I've been a diesel mechanic since I was nineteen, and I started this shop in 1994 with one bay, one wrecker and a loan from my uncle that I paid back in cash in a coffee can. Today it's fourteen bays and the fleet contracts for half the trucking outfits on Interstate 44. If you work on diesels long enough, you learn that a diesel never lies to you. It tells you exactly what's wrong, every time, if you're willing to listen. Most people just turn the radio up. Remember that.

The process server looked at the yellow pages in his hand like they might be radioactive.

"I just deliver, sir."

"Then deliver."

He went. Cheryl watched him go. She'd had her hair done for this, and she was wearing the pearl earrings I'd bought her for our twenty-fifth, and she was smiling the way she smiles at a restaurant when the waiter gets her order wrong and she's about to enjoy telling him.

"You've got nothing to say?" she said. "Thirty years and you've got nothing to say?"

"Have a pancake, Cheryl. They're blueberry."

Somebody laughed. I think it was Big Earl, who runs my tire bay. He turned it into a cough.

"You think that piece of paper means anything?" She said it loud, for the room. "I was twenty-four years old. I didn't even read it. Your mother made me sign it in her kitchen. I signed it laughing. I said, and I remember it exactly, Arturo, I said, 'Like you'll ever have anything worth protecting.'"

"I remember too," I said.

"Well, now you've got something. And I'm taking half."

She turned around and walked out through the big bay door into the parking lot, past the bouncy castle we'd rented for the customers' kids and the banner that said 30 YEARS, THANK YOU SPRINGFIELD.

Out in the lot, a silver Lexus was idling with the window down. A man was in the driver's seat, fifty or so, with a tan in March and a white smile and an arm hanging out the window like he was in a commercial. He waited till she got in. Then he leaned across her, looked straight at me through the open bay door, and called out across the parking lot.

"No judge in Missouri enforces a thirty-year-old napkin, Arty!"

Arty. Nobody's called me Arty since my father died.

The Lexus pulled out. The license plate frame said POLLARD COMMERCIAL REALTY.

I finished the breakfast. You don't walk out on sixty customers because your wife decided to put on a show. I flipped pancakes until eleven, shook every hand in the building, and gave away thirty Villa Diesel coffee mugs, and when the last kid got pulled off the bouncy castle crying, I went into my office and shut the door and sat down at my desk and looked at the wall for a long time.

On the wall is a photo from 1994. Me at twenty-five, skinny as a dipstick, standing in front of a rented bay with a wrecker that had MIGUEL'S TOWING painted over with primer. Cheryl is next to me, twenty-four, in cutoffs, holding up a hand-lettered sign that says VILLA DIESEL, with the S backward. She did that on purpose, to make me laugh.

My daughter Gabriela knocked and came in without waiting. She's twenty-eight and she runs the payroll for the shop, which means she knows exactly what everybody makes, including her parents.

"Dad."

"Hey, mija."

"Who was the guy in the Lexus?"

"I think his name's Pollard."

"Lance Pollard," she said. "He sold the Hendersons their warehouse. He wears so much cologne you can smell him in the next county. Mom's been 'looking at investment properties' with him since Christmas." She sat down across from me. "Dad, she's been talking to me for weeks about how she's going to be 'taken care of.' I thought she meant you."

"Did she ever say anything about the prenup?"

"She said it's a joke. She said Grandma made her sign it on a napkin."

"It wasn't a napkin," I said. "And it wasn't Grandma's kitchen."

Monday morning, I sat in the office of Nadine Ferrell on Walnut Street. Nadine is a short, round woman in her sixties with a gray braid down her back and reading glasses pushed up on her head, and she's handled two of my employees' divorces and both of them still send her Christmas cards, which tells you something.

She had the prenup spread out on her desk, all eleven pages, and she read every word of it while I sat there drinking her terrible coffee.

"Okay," she said finally. "Let me put this in plain English, because you're paying me by the hour and plain English is faster."

"Please."

"In June 1995, a month before your wedding, you and Cheryl Ann Dobbs signed this agreement. It says the business you were running, Villa Diesel, which at the time was apparently one wrecker and a lease, is your separate property. Here, page four, paragraph two: and any increase in its value, any business it acquires, any real property bought in its name, stays your separate property, forever, no matter who works there or how big it gets."

"That's page four."

"That's page four. And it says the house you buy together, the savings, the retirement accounts, anything in both names, gets split down the middle. And it caps alimony at three years." She looked at me over her glasses. "That's a fair agreement, Arturo. It's not a mean one. She gets half of everything you built together at home. She just doesn't get the shop."

"The shop's my whole life."

"I know. That's why your mother made you get it." She flipped to the last page. "Now. Here's the interesting part. Usually, when a wife tries to throw out a prenup, she says she didn't have a lawyer. She says the husband's lawyer wrote it, she was young, nobody explained it, she was pressured. Missouri judges listen to that. But look here."

She turned the page around.

There were four signatures. Mine. Cheryl's, big and loopy, with a little heart over the i in Cheryl that she stopped doing in about 1998. My lawyer back then, a man named Ortega who died years ago. And under the line marked Counsel for Cheryl Ann Dobbs, in neat block capitals:

EVERETT J. BELLAMY.

"She had her own lawyer," Nadine said. "Your mother paid for it, you told me. But it was her lawyer. Not yours."

"Old man Bellamy. He was a lawyer before he was a judge. Cheryl's dad played golf with him."

"Everett Bellamy," Nadine said, "is eighty-four years old now, retired from the Circuit Court bench, and lives in a condo out by the Highlands golf course." She pushed her glasses up. "And do you know who filed your wife's divorce petition on Friday afternoon?"

"I didn't read the papers that close."

"Conrad Bellamy. Of Bellamy and Bellamy." She let it sit. "Everett's son."

I laughed. I didn't mean to. It came out of me like air out of a tire, and Nadine laughed too, a short, wheezing laugh, and for a minute we were just two people in an office on Walnut Street laughing at a piece of paper.

"Does he know?" I asked.

"I doubt it. His father retired from practice twenty-six years ago. Conrad was in law school when this was signed. And something tells me your wife told him exactly what she told you. A napkin. A kitchen."

"She signed it in his father's office."

"Then Conrad Bellamy," Nadine said, "is in for a very interesting week."

Her paralegal leaned in the door. A young guy with a lot of tattoos and a laptop under his arm.

"Ms. Ferrell? You asked me to run the property search on Mr. Villa. You're going to want to see this."

He set the laptop down on the desk. It was a commercial real estate listing. A photo of my shop, my actual shop, taken from Kearney Street with the big VILLA DIESEL sign in the frame. Two point six acres. Fourteen-bay heavy equipment facility. Asking price four point one million.

Listing agent: Lance Pollard, Pollard Commercial Realty.

Seller: Cheryl Villa.

"She's selling the shop," I said. "She doesn't own the shop, and she's selling it."

"She's trying," Nadine said, and the laugh was gone out of her voice. "Arturo, you don't do anything about this. Not one thing. You don't call him, you don't call her, you don't put a sign up. You let them show it. You let them walk right into it."

Nine days later, on a Monday, I sat in the conference room of Bellamy and Bellamy, on the fifth floor of a brick building downtown, with Nadine on my right and a plate of grocery-store cookies nobody touched in front of us. The room had a long table, leather chairs and a big oil painting of an old man in a judge's robe hanging on the wall.

Cheryl sat across from me, in a new blazer, with a new handbag on the table in front of her like a trophy. Conrad Bellamy sat next to her. He was in his middle fifties, with a good haircut, a gold watch and the easy manners of a man who'd never once had to ask anybody for a loan.

Through the glass wall of the conference room, I could see Lance Pollard sitting in the lobby, scrolling his phone, his cologne probably soaking into the furniture.

"Thank you for coming in," Conrad said. "I think we can resolve this quickly and keep it civil. Mrs. Villa has been a partner in that business for thirty years. She kept the books for a decade. She raised your daughter while you built it. Our position is simple. Villa Diesel is marital property. It's worth about four point two million dollars. She's entitled to half."

"Two point one," Cheryl said. "I did the math."

"And that prenup?" Nadine asked pleasantly.

Conrad smiled. "Ms. Ferrell. My client was twenty-four. She signed it at her future mother-in-law's kitchen table, without counsel, under pressure, a month before the wedding, with the invitations already mailed. She never read it. She has told me, very credibly, that she laughed while she signed it. That's not an agreement. That's a hostage situation. Any judge in Greene County will throw it out."

"You've read it, then?"

"I've read the petition. I've heard my client. I don't need to read a thirty-year-old napkin to know what it is."

Nadine reached into her briefcase and took out the yellowed copy, the one I'd sent with the process server nine days earlier, still in its envelope, still sealed. She slid it across the table.

"We sent this over on the day you served him. It's been sitting in your mail room. It's eleven pages. You'll want page four, and then you'll want page eleven."

Conrad sighed like a man indulging a child. He opened the envelope. He took the pages out and flipped through them with one finger.

He read page four. I watched his eyes go down the paragraph. Any increase in its value. Any business it acquires. Any real property bought in its name. He read it again.

Then he flipped to page eleven.

I don't know how to describe what happened to his face. He went pale. All the color went out of it in about two seconds, like somebody pulled the plug on a sink. He looked at the signature block. He looked up at the oil painting of the old man in the judge's robe on the wall behind him. He looked back down.

"Conrad?" Cheryl said.

He didn't answer her. He picked up the phone on the table and pressed a button.

"Denise. Could you bring me a glass of water, please."

"Conrad, what is it?"

"Mrs. Villa," he said, very carefully, "did you have an attorney when you signed this?"

"No. I told you. It was at his mother's house. I just signed."

"It wasn't at his mother's house, Mrs. Villa." He turned the page around so she could see it. "It was at my father's office. On Jefferson Avenue. My father's initials are on every page. He was your attorney. He represented you."

Cheryl stared at the page. At her own signature, with the little heart over the i.

"That's... that's Everett. That's Daddy's golf friend. He was just there to witness."

"He signed as your counsel," Conrad said. "Mrs. Villa, my father never witnessed anything in his life. If he signed as your counsel, he went over every page with you." He drank half the water the secretary brought in without looking at her. "Ms. Ferrell, I'd like a recess."

"Take all the time you need," Nadine said.

He took Cheryl out into the hallway. Through the glass, I watched them talk. I watched her hands start moving, fast. I watched him shake his head, once, twice, three times. I watched Lance Pollard look up from his phone in the lobby and stand up.

They came back in eleven minutes later. Conrad didn't sit down.

"Mr. Villa, Ms. Ferrell, I'm withdrawing as Mrs. Villa's counsel, effective today. My father drafted this agreement for her. If its validity is challenged, he is the most important witness in the case. My firm has a conflict." He looked at Cheryl. "I'm sorry. I'll refer you to someone."

"You told me it was a napkin!" Cheryl said. "You said no judge would ever... you SAID..."

"You told me it was a napkin, Mrs. Villa."

Cheryl grabbed her new handbag off the table. She stood there a second, breathing hard, and then she looked across the table at me, and her face was red all the way down her neck.

"Fine," she said. "You think you've won? You haven't won anything. Lance has a buyer coming to look at the shop on Friday. Cash. And when he sees what that place is worth, you're going to wish you'd just given me half."

She walked out. Through the glass, I watched her grab Lance's arm in the lobby and say something, and I watched Lance look through the glass at me and smile his commercial smile and give me a little two-finger salute.

"A buyer," Nadine said quietly. "For a building she doesn't own."

"On Friday."

"Arturo," Nadine said, "I'd very much like to be there on Friday."

Friday at two, a black Escalade and Lance Pollard's silver Lexus pulled into my lot on Kearney Street one after the other. I was in bay seven with my arm inside the engine of a Peterbilt that belonged to a hog farmer from Buffalo. Big Earl was on the tire machine. Gabriela was in the office with the door open.

Lance got out first. He was in a navy suit with a pocket square, and he walked up to the buyer's door and opened it for him like a valet. The buyer was a heavy man in his sixties in a Carhartt jacket and a John Deere cap, and he looked around my lot the way men look at a truck they're thinking of buying, at the tires first.

"Fourteen bays," Lance was saying. "Heavy lift in every one. Two point six acres. The current operator's on his way out, very motivated situation, very motivated, and the seller can close in thirty days."

"The current operator," I said, sliding out from under the Peterbilt and wiping my hands on a red rag, "is me."

Lance stopped. His smile stayed, but it had to work harder.

"Arty! Didn't see you there. We're just taking a quick look. Won't be in your way."

"You're in my bay."

The buyer looked at me, then at Lance.

"This your place?"

"Thirty years," I said.

"He says you're on your way out."

"He says a lot of things." I turned around. "Gabriela? Could you bring the deed, please? The one from the safe."

My daughter came out of the office with a manila folder. She didn't look at Lance. She walked straight to the buyer and handed him the top sheet.

"That's the warranty deed for this property," she said. "From 1998. When the shop moved here from Commercial Street. The owner of record is Villa Diesel and Fleet, LLC. The sole member of the LLC is my dad. My mom has never been on the title."

The buyer read it. He read it all the way down, slowly, the way a man reads something when he's about to write a check with a lot of zeroes on it.

Then he folded it and handed it back to Gabriela, and he turned to Lance.

"Son," he said, "you brought me out here from Joplin to look at a building the seller doesn't own?"

"There's a divorce pending," Lance said quickly. "The wife's entitled to half of the business, which includes..."

"Her lawyer quit on Monday," I said. "Because his own daddy wrote her prenup. Page four. You want me to get you a copy?"

The buyer looked at Lance for a long time. Then he looked at me. Then he laughed, one short bark, and shook his head.

"Mr. Villa, I've been buying trucks thirty-five years. You've done work on three of mine, I think. My company's Cooper Freight."

"I know. Your number four's got an injector problem. I told your driver."

"He didn't tell me." The buyer climbed back into his Escalade. "Mr. Pollard, don't call me again."

The Escalade pulled out. Lance stood in the middle of my bay with his pocket square, and the smell of his cologne fighting the diesel, and for the first time since I'd seen him leaning out of a Lexus, he didn't have anything ready to say.

"You think you're smart," he said finally. "You think a piece of paper makes you smart. She's going to get another lawyer, Arty. A better one. And I'm going to make sure she does. I've got friends in this town."

"So do I. They're all in the waiting room."

He looked over at the waiting room window. Three of my fleet customers were standing at it with their coffee, watching. One of them raised his mug.

Lance turned around and walked back to his Lexus, a little too fast.

I was still standing there wiping my hands when the shop phone rang in the office. Gabriela answered it. She listened for a second. Then she held the phone out toward me through the door, and her eyebrows were up somewhere near her hairline.

"Dad. It's for you. She says her name is Rosalind Pollard." Gabriela covered the mouthpiece. "She says she's calling from Little Rock. She says she's Lance's wife."

I took the phone into the office and shut the door.

"Mr. Villa?" A woman's voice, Southern, careful, the kind of voice that's practiced being calm. "I'm sorry to call you at work. My name is Rosalind Pollard. I've been married to Lance for twenty-two years. We have a house in west Little Rock and two boys in high school."

"Mrs. Pollard."

"Lance tells me he works in Springfield Monday through Thursday. Commercial real estate. He comes home on weekends." She took a breath. "Last week our bank called me about an account I'd never heard of. A business account in his name, in Missouri. There was a deposit into it in February. Twenty-five thousand dollars. The memo line said 'escrow, Villa.'"

I sat down in my chair. Behind me, through the window, Big Earl was rolling a tractor tire across the lot.

"Mrs. Pollard, how did you find me?"

"I looked up Villa. You're the only Villa in Springfield with a sign big enough to see from the highway." She almost laughed. "And then I saw your shop on his company's website. For sale. And I thought, either this man is selling his business, or my husband is doing it again."

"Again?"

The line was quiet for a moment.

"Mr. Villa, can I come up and see you? Not on the phone. I'd rather do this in person."

"My lawyer's office is on Walnut Street," I said. "Can you come Wednesday?"

"I can come tomorrow."

Before Rosalind Pollard ever got to Springfield, I had to talk to my wife.

She called me that night, Friday, at a little after nine, and said she was coming to the house to get the rest of her things, and she wanted to talk, and would I please be there. We'd lived in that house on South Fremont for twenty-two years, a brick ranch with a big backyard and a pecan tree I planted the day Gabriela was born. Since the breakfast, she'd been staying at an extended-stay hotel near the Bass Pro, which I was paying for, because the money in our joint account was half hers and I'd never once said a word about it.

She came in without knocking, with two empty suitcases and a face that looked like she hadn't slept since Monday.

"You set that up," she said. "Friday. With the buyer. You and Gabriela. You set Lance up."

"Lance set himself up. He listed a building with your name as the seller. You don't own it. You never did."

"I own half of everything. I'm your wife."

"You own half the house. Half the savings. Half my 401k. Half the cabin at Table Rock. That's about six hundred thousand dollars, Cheryl. That's what the prenup says, and it's fair, and you're going to get every dime of it."

"Six hundred thousand." She laughed. "Out of what, four million? Five?"

"Out of what we built at home. You don't get the shop. You signed that you wouldn't get the shop."

"I signed it when the shop was a wrecker and a pile of tires! I signed it laughing, Arturo! Because it was a joke! Because you were a joke! A twenty-five-year-old grease monkey with a coffee can full of your uncle's money!"

"I remember."

"Like you'll ever have anything worth protecting. That's what I said. Everybody laughed. Your mother laughed."

"My mother didn't laugh," I said. "My mother never laughed in her life. She paid for your lawyer and she made sure he was the best one in town, because she wanted it fair."

"He wasn't my lawyer. He was a golf buddy of my dad's. He sat there and read some boring stuff out loud and I signed it."

"He read it to you. Out loud. Every page."

She stopped. She looked at me like I'd tricked her into something.

"I did your books for ten years," she said, quieter. "For free. In that horrible little office with the space heater. I answered your phone. I ran out for parts in my own car. You don't think I earned something?"

"I think you earned a lot. I think you earned the house and the cabin and half of everything we ever put in a bank. And I think, in 2006, when you quit doing the books, you said you'd never set foot in that shop again, because it smelled like diesel and you'd married beneath you. You said that at your sister's birthday. In front of eleven people."

"I was drunk."

"You were drunk. But you meant it. You've meant it for thirty years, Cheryl. You just never thought it'd be worth anything."

She sat down hard on the arm of the couch. One of the empty suitcases fell over.

"Lance says it's community effort. Lance says a judge has to look at what I put in."

"Did Lance tell you he's married?"

She looked up.

"He's separated."

"He's got a wife in Little Rock and two boys in high school, and his wife is driving up here tomorrow to talk to my lawyer, because she found twenty-five thousand dollars in an account in his name with 'escrow, Villa' on the memo line." I watched her face. "Did you give Lance twenty-five thousand dollars in February, Cheryl?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. A diesel never lies to you, and neither does a face that's been married to you for thirty years.

"It was escrow," she whispered. "For the lake house. In Branson. He's buying a lake house for us. On Table Rock. It's in escrow."

"Escrow's held by a title company. Not in a realtor's personal account in another state."

"He said it was faster."

"I'll bet it was."

The front door opened again. Gabriela walked in, with her car keys still in her hand. She'd come over to get the payroll laptop, which she'd left in the den, and she stopped dead in the hallway when she saw her mother.

"Mom."

"Gabby. Tell your father he's being cruel. Tell him."

Gabriela stood there with her keys. She's always been more like me than her mother, and she's always hated that, a little.

"Mom," she said. "I read the prenup when I was nineteen. It was in the safe with the shop insurance and Grandma made me read it. She said a girl who's going to do payroll for a business ought to know who owns it." She swallowed. "Mom, page four is really clear. It's like two sentences. I don't know why you never just read it."

Cheryl looked at her daughter for a long time. Then she picked up her empty suitcases, both of them, and walked out the front door without packing a single thing.

Her car sat in the driveway for a while with the engine running. Then it pulled out.

On Monday, Nadine called me at the shop.

"She's got a new lawyer. A man named Wilcox from Kansas City. Big ads on billboards. He filed a motion this morning to set aside the prenuptial agreement."

"On what grounds?"

"Duress. Lack of meaningful counsel. She's going to testify that she didn't understand what she signed, that Everett Bellamy was a family friend who never explained anything, and that she was pressured by your mother a month before the wedding." Nadine paused. "The hearing's in two weeks, Arturo. And she's going to say all of it under oath."

"And Everett?"

"I'm driving out to the Highlands tomorrow," Nadine said, "to have a cup of coffee with an eighty-four-year-old retired judge. Wish me luck."

She called me back the next afternoon from her car. I could hear the turn signal ticking.

"Well?"

"He made me the worst cup of coffee I've ever had in my life," Nadine said. "Instant. In a mug from the Missouri Bar convention in 1979. And then he went into his spare bedroom and came back with a banker's box."

"A banker's box of what?"

"Arturo, I'm not going to tell you on the phone. I want you to hear it the same time the judge does." The turn signal stopped. "But I'll tell you two things. He remembers your mother. He says she waited in his lobby for over an hour and read a Reader's Digest cover to cover and never once came in the room. And he asked me if your wife still dots the i in Cheryl with a little heart."

"She stopped in about 1998."

"He'll be sorry to hear it," Nadine said. "He's coming to the hearing himself. He won't hear of a deposition. He says he hasn't been in a courtroom in fourteen years, and he'd like to go out on a good one."

My phone buzzed with a text before I'd even put it down. It was from Cheryl.

"Wilcox says the old man has dementia. Wilcox says he won't even remember my name."

The Greene County Judicial Courts Building on Boonville Avenue has a courtroom on the third floor with wood paneling and a clock that runs four minutes slow. I know, because I sat there for forty minutes before the hearing started and checked it against my phone.

Cheryl sat at the other table with her new lawyer, Wilcox, a tall man in a shiny suit who talked like he was on one of his billboards. Lance Pollard sat in the second row behind them in a blazer, with his arm draped across the back of the bench like he owned it. Gabriela sat behind me.

The judge was a woman in her sixties named on the nameplate as Judge Harmon, with half-glasses and the patience of somebody who'd heard every divorce in southwest Missouri twice.

Cheryl testified first.

"I was twenty-four," she said. "I was a kid. The wedding was in five weeks. His mother said if I didn't sign it, there'd be no wedding. Mr. Bellamy was my father's golfing friend. He was just there. He didn't explain anything. I thought it was a formality. I never read it. I didn't understand a word."

"Did you feel you had a choice?" Wilcox asked.

"No." She dabbed her eyes. "I felt trapped."

"Thank you, Mrs. Villa."

Nadine stood up. She didn't cross-examine Cheryl. She just said, "Your Honor, I'd like to call Everett Bellamy."

There was a stir in the room. Wilcox turned around in his seat. Lance Pollard took his arm off the back of the bench.

The door at the back of the courtroom opened, and an old man came in on a cane. He was tall and thin, with white hair combed straight back, and he wore a three-piece suit that had probably been in fashion when I opened my shop. He walked the whole length of the aisle without help, slowly, and every lawyer in the room stood up as he passed, out of habit, because for twenty years they'd stood up whenever Everett Bellamy walked into a courtroom.

He took the oath. He sat down. He put a thin manila folder on the rail in front of him.

"Judge Bellamy," Nadine said. "Did you represent Cheryl Ann Dobbs in June of 1995?"

"I did."

"Did you explain the prenuptial agreement to her?"

"Every page." His voice was dry and clear. "I always did. My clients were usually young and usually in love. That's when they most need a lawyer, and least want one."

"How do you remember a meeting thirty years ago, Judge?"

"I don't," he said. "I wrote it down. I've kept file notes on every client I ever had. My son thinks it's a sickness." He opened the manila folder with long, shaky fingers. "June the twelfth, 1995. Client Cheryl Ann Dobbs. Reviewed the agreement with client paragraph by paragraph, one hour and ten minutes. Client asked no questions. Specifically advised client to read page four, paragraph two, regarding the growth of the business, and explained that if the business became valuable, she would not share in it."

He turned a page.

"Client laughed," he read. "Client stated, quote, 'Like he'll ever have anything worth protecting,' end quote. Advised client to take the agreement home and read page four overnight. Client declined. Signed at 4:40 p.m. Mrs. Villa, senior, waiting in the lobby, did not enter the room."

Nobody in the courtroom said anything. The slow clock ticked.

Cheryl was looking at the old man in the witness box as if she'd never seen him before in her life. Maybe she hadn't, really. Maybe she'd never looked.

"No further questions," Nadine said.

Wilcox stood up. He looked at the old man. He looked at the manila folder. He sat back down.

"No questions, Your Honor."

Judge Harmon took her half-glasses off.

"The motion to set aside the prenuptial agreement is denied," she said. "Mrs. Villa had independent counsel, who explained the agreement, who advised her to read the provision she now says she didn't understand, and who wrote down, at the time, her reason for not reading it. The agreement stands. Villa Diesel and Fleet, LLC is Mr. Villa's separate property." She looked out over the courtroom. "Judge Bellamy, thank you for coming in. I clerked for you in 1991. You haven't changed."

"Neither has my handwriting," the old man said.

Out in the hallway, people were clustered around the elevators the way they always are after something happens. A couple of lawyers. A bailiff. A man with a parking ticket. Lance Pollard came out of the courtroom fast, his phone already at his ear, and he got about ten steps before he stopped dead.

A woman was standing by the drinking fountain. She was about fifty, small, in a gray coat, with her purse held in front of her in both hands. Next to her stood Nadine's tattooed paralegal, who'd driven her over from Walnut Street.

"Hello, Lance," Rosalind Pollard said.

Lance's phone came down from his ear very slowly.

"Roz. What... what are you doing in Springfield?"

"I came up to talk to Mr. Villa's lawyer. On Saturday. I told her about the account. I told her about the woman in Fayetteville, too. The one in 2019. The one with the 'escrow' for the lake house in Eureka Springs."

Cheryl had come out of the courtroom behind him. She stopped.

"Who's this?" Cheryl said. "Lance, who is this?"

"Cheryl, this is... she's..."

"I'm his wife," Rosalind said. "I'm sorry, Mrs. Villa. I really am. Did he tell you about the lake house on Table Rock?"

Cheryl's mouth opened. Nothing came out.

"There isn't one," Rosalind said gently. "There wasn't one in Eureka Springs either."

Lance's phone buzzed in his hand. He looked at it like it was a snake, and then he answered it, because some men will answer a phone in a burning building.

"Gary. Hey. Can I call you back, I'm in the middle of..."

We could all hear it. Gary was loud. Gary was his managing broker at Pollard Commercial Realty, which, as it turned out, was not Lance's company at all. It just had his name on it because he'd paid for the license plate frames himself.

"Lance, I just got off the phone with the Missouri Real Estate Commission. A listing on Kearney Street with a seller who doesn't own the property? And a lawyer named Ferrell faxed me a bank record of a client's twenty-five thousand dollars going into your personal account? You're done. Your license is hanging on my wall, and I'm taking it down this afternoon and mailing it to Jefferson City. Don't come to the office. I'll box up your stuff."

The line clicked.

Lance stood in the hallway of the Greene County courthouse with his phone in his hand and his wife on one side of him and my wife on the other, and both of them were looking at him the same way, which I guess is the one thing they'll ever have in common.

"Cheryl," he said. "Babe. I can explain the money."

"Then explain it," Rosalind said. "I'd like to hear it too."

He couldn't. He turned around and walked toward the stairs, fast, and Rosalind watched him go without moving, and then she reached into her purse and took out a cashier's check and walked over and held it out to Cheryl.

"Twenty-five thousand," she said. "It was still in the account. I froze it last week. It's yours. I'm sorry it's the only thing about him that was real."

Cheryl took it. She looked at it. Her hand was shaking. Then she turned around and looked at me, standing by the elevators with Gabriela, and after thirty years of marriage my wife finally looked at me like I might be the one who had something worth protecting.

"Arturo," she said. "Arturo, please. I didn't read it. I swear to God, I never read it. Can we talk? Can we just go somewhere and talk, like before? Thirty years. Please."

I pushed the elevator button. The doors opened.

"You had thirty years to read page four, Cheryl," I said. "It's two sentences."

The doors closed.
