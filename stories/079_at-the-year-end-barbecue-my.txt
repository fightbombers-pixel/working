"I found someone truly successful, Dwayne."

My wife said it at the Crenshaw Oilfield Supply year-end barbecue, out in the pipe yard under a white tent, in front of about two hundred employees, customers and their families. She said it loud enough that the line at the beer trailer turned around. She said it standing next to a tall man in a pressed pearl-snap shirt who had his hand on the small of her back.

I was behind the big offset smoker in my apron, the same place I'd been at every one of these barbecues for thirty-one years, with a fourteen-pound brisket on the cutting board and a carving knife in my hand.

Trina and I had been married twenty-six years. She hadn't come to a company barbecue since 2015. She'd always said it smelled like diesel and people who work for a living.

So I sliced off the end of the brisket, the burnt end, the best piece on the board, laid it on a paper plate with a slice of white bread and a pickle, and held it out across the smoker to the man with his hand on my wife.

"Good for you," I said. "Brisket's on the house."

He took the plate. I think he was too surprised not to.

Six minutes later, that same man would be standing in front of me holding out his hand, and his hand would be shaking so hard the pickle slid off his plate into the dirt.

What he didn't know, and what my wife didn't know, was what was in the envelope in the inside pocket of Royal Crenshaw's sport coat. My name was typed on the front.

My name is Dwayne Coburn. I'm fifty-four years old, and I've worked at Crenshaw Oilfield Supply in Odessa, Texas, since I was twenty-three, starting as a yard hand loading drill pipe onto flatbeds in August heat. For the last nine years I've been vice president of operations, which mostly means I know where every truck, every load and every dollar in this company is at any given hour. The first thing old Royal Crenshaw taught me, back when I still had all my hair, was this: in the oil patch, everybody lies on the phone, but the paperwork can't. The bill of lading doesn't care how nice you are. Hold on to that.

"Who is he?" I said to Trina.

"This is Bodie," she said. "Bodie Kincannon. He's the vice president of sales at Permian Sand and Logistics." She said the title like it was a medal. "He's a real executive, Dwayne. He flies to Houston twice a month. He's got a corner office."

"Nice to meet you, Bodie."

"Likewise, man." Bodie had a good handshake voice, a big easy West Texas drawl, and very white teeth. He looked at the smoker, at my apron, at the grease on my forearms. "Trina tells me you're the pitmaster around here."

"Something like that."

"No offense, buddy. Somebody's got to cook."

Around us, the tent had gone quiet the way a room goes quiet when everybody pretends they aren't listening. My crew chief, Ramon, was standing by the potato salad with a spoon in midair. Nell Ybarra from purchasing was by the tea dispenser with her mouth open. Three tables over, a couple of our biggest customers from Midland were looking at their plates very hard.

"I'm moving out this weekend," Trina said. "I've already got most of my things at Bodie's. I wanted to tell you in person, so you couldn't say I hid it."

"In person," I said. "At my company barbecue."

"You're always here. Where else was I supposed to find you?"

That was when the PA system squealed. Up on the flatbed trailer we use for a stage every year, Royal Crenshaw was tapping the microphone.

Royal is eighty-one years old. He founded this company in 1971 with one truck and a loan from his father-in-law, and he still comes in every morning at six in boots and a starched shirt. He had a cane now, and his hands shook a little, but when he got on a microphone the whole tent shut up.

"Folks," Royal said. "I'll keep it short, because Dwayne's brisket is getting cold, and I've been waiting all year to eat it."

People laughed. Trina rolled her eyes.

"Fifty-two years," Royal said. "Fifty-two years I've run this outfit. And on January first, I'm going to quit. Don't clap, you'll hurt my feelings." More laughter. "The board voted in November. We kept it quiet till today because I wanted to tell you all myself. I've got a man who's been here thirty-one years. Started loading pipe. Never missed a payroll, never lost a customer, never once lied to me, which is more than I can say for my own sons."

Over by me, Bodie Kincannon leaned over to Trina.

"This is good," he said quietly. "I need to meet this guy. Our contract's up for renewal. Whoever he is, I need to be his best friend by New Year's."

He set his plate down on the edge of the smoker and walked toward the stage, smoothing his shirt. Trina went with him, her hand on his arm.

"Royal," Bodie called out, all teeth, before the old man could finish. "Bodie Kincannon, Permian Sand. Congratulations, sir. I'd sure love to shake the hand of your new CEO."

Royal looked down at him from the trailer. Then Royal smiled, the way he smiles at a salesman who's just talked himself into a corner.

"Well, son," Royal said into the microphone, "you just did. He handed you that brisket."

And he pointed his cane straight at me.

The whole tent turned around. Two hundred people. I put down the carving knife and wiped my hands on my apron.

Bodie Kincannon froze about halfway between the stage and the smoker. My wife froze next to him. I watched it hit them both, the same as a pressure gauge dropping to zero.

Then Trina did the thing I'll remember the rest of my life. She let go of Bodie's arm like it was hot, walked straight back across that pipe yard to the smoker, in front of every single person under that tent, threw both arms around my neck and kissed me on the cheek.

"Oh my God, honey," she said, loud. "Why didn't you tell me?"

I didn't hug her back. I just stood there with my greasy hands out to the side so I wouldn't get brisket on her blouse.

"I did tell you," I said. "In November. At Rosa's Café. You laughed."

Behind her, Bodie had come up holding out his hand to shake. That was when the pickle went in the dirt.

I didn't take his hand. I'll tell you why in a minute. Because the title and the corner office and the barbecue, all of that was the small thing. The big thing was a manila folder sitting on my desk at that very moment, in my office on the second floor, with the words "Frac Sand Renewal: Sealed Bids" on the tab and Bodie Kincannon's company name inside it. And something in that folder that I'd noticed at four o'clock that afternoon and hadn't understood yet.

"Mr. Coburn," Bodie said, "sir. I think we got off on the wrong foot."

"Did we?"

"I'd love to buy you a drink sometime and start over. Here's my card." He tucked it into the bib pocket of my apron, since I wasn't going to take it. "Call me anytime. Anytime."

"I'll keep it," I said, "for the file."

Royal finished his speech without me. Somebody started a slow clap and then everybody was clapping, which I hate, and Trina stood next to me through all of it with her arm around my waist, smiling for the people taking pictures on their phones, as if she'd never stood in that pipe yard and told me she'd found somebody truly successful.

When the clapping died down, she leaned in close.

"We need to talk tonight," she said. "At home. Just us."

"Okay."

"I didn't mean any of that. I was confused. Bodie was… he was a mistake. I'll make it right."

"What about your things at his place?"

She smiled at me with her teeth, the way she smiles at a customer at her salon who's complaining about the color.

"Things can come back," she said.

Then she walked out to the parking lot, and I watched through the tent flap as she climbed into Bodie Kincannon's black Tahoe, and they sat in it for ten minutes with the engine running, arguing. I could see her hands going.

Nell Ybarra came up beside me at the smoker with a plate she wasn't eating.

"Dwayne," she said quietly. "I'm so sorry. That was awful."

"I've had worse days."

"Have you?" Nell is forty-four. She's run purchasing for six years, and she's the only person in the building who reads every page of every contract, including the parts in small print. "Dwayne, can I ask you something about work? I know this is the wrong time."

"It's the right time. It's the only thing today that makes sense."

"The sand bids," she said. "The sealed ones that came in Wednesday. Did you take that folder home at any point?"

I set my knife down.

"Last week," I said. "Monday night. I read through all three at my kitchen table. Why?"

Nell looked across the pipe yard at the black Tahoe in the parking lot.

"Because Permian Sand's bid," she said, "is exactly one dollar a ton under Basin Rock's. To the penny, on every line. And it's got the same spelling mistake."

"What spelling mistake?"

"Basin Rock typed Monahans wrong. M-O-N-A-H-A-M-S. On the delivery schedule, every line, twelve times. Somebody over there can't type." Nell's voice dropped. "Permian Sand's bid says Monahams too. Twelve times. Dwayne, nobody makes the same typo twelve times by accident. Somebody at Permian Sand was looking at Basin Rock's sealed bid when they wrote theirs."

I stood there with brisket grease drying on my hands.

Monday night, nine days ago. My kitchen. The three sealed bids spread out on the table under the light. And me laughing out loud, because Basin Rock had misspelled a town we've been hauling to since 1974, and I'd said, "Look at this, they can't even spell Monahans," and Trina had looked up from her phone at the other end of the table and said, "What's so funny?"

And I'd shown her.

Then I'd gone to bed and left the briefcase on the kitchen table, open, the way I've left it on that table for twenty-six years, because it never crossed my mind that anybody in my own house would read it.

"Nell," I said, "don't tell anybody yet. Not Royal, not anybody. Put the three bids in your safe tonight. Monday morning, my office, eight o'clock."

"Dwayne." She looked at me. "Are you okay?"

"Ask me Monday," I said.

I got home at nine that night. The house was dark except for the kitchen. Trina was sitting at the table in a bathrobe with a glass of white wine and two candles lit, which I hadn't seen since our twentieth anniversary.

"Hi," she said. "I made lasagna. It's in the oven."

"I ate brisket."

"I know. I wanted to have it here anyway." She pushed a chair out with her foot. "Sit with me. Please."

I sat. Not in the chair she pushed out. In my own chair at the other end, where I'd read the bids.

"Dwayne," she said, "I made a terrible mistake today."

"Which part?"

"All of it. Bodie. The barbecue. I don't know what I was thinking. He's been calling me all night. I haven't picked up."

"You sat in his truck for ten minutes after."

"I was telling him it was over." She reached across the table. I didn't reach back. "I was telling him I'm married. That I love my husband."

"This afternoon you said you found somebody truly successful."

"I didn't know!" Her eyes filled up. She's always been able to cry on cue; she did it to the city inspector when he tried to shut down her salon in 2018. "You never tell me anything. You come home smelling like diesel and you fall asleep in the recliner and you never tell me what's happening in your life."

"I told you at Rosa's Café. November ninth. I said, 'Royal wants me to take over.' You said, 'Take over what, the smoker?' And you laughed and asked for the check."

"I thought you were joking."

"I wasn't."

"Well how was I supposed to know that? You don't act like a CEO. You don't dress like a CEO. You drive a Silverado with a hundred and ninety thousand miles on it."

"It runs fine."

"Dwayne!" She put the wine glass down hard. "I'm trying to save our marriage here."

"Since about four-fifteen this afternoon," I said.

She stood up. The bathrobe swung.

"That's not fair. I came back. I kissed you in front of everybody. I chose you."

"You chose the man with the envelope."

"You're being cruel."

"No, Trina," I said, "I'm being exactly as boring as you've always said I was. I'm just paying attention for once." I got up and took my keys out of the bowl. "I'm going to sleep at the yard tonight. There's a cot in the shop office."

"You're leaving? Me? Now?"

"You told two hundred people you're moving out this weekend. I'm just giving you the house to do it in."

I walked out to the Silverado. My phone buzzed on the seat before I'd started it. A text from a number I didn't have saved.

"Dwayne, it's Bodie Kincannon. Got your number off the company directory. Hate how today went down. Think there's a lot we can work out, business and personal. Coffee Monday? I'll come to you."

I read it twice. Then I put the truck in reverse.

Monday morning, at eight o'clock sharp, Nell Ybarra laid three sealed bids side by side across my desk.

Basin Rock Sand, out of Kermit. Permian Sand and Logistics, out of Midland. And Llano Silica, out of San Angelo, which had come in high and never had a chance.

The contract was for frac sand delivered to our customers' well sites. Nine point four million dollars a year, three years, starting January first. The current contract was Permian Sand's, and it expired December thirty-first at midnight. Everybody in the building knew Bodie Kincannon had been sweating it since September.

"Look," Nell said, and ran her finger down the price columns. "Basin Rock, thirty-eight dollars and forty cents a ton, delivered to the Monahams yard. Permian, thirty-seven forty. Basin Rock, forty-one twenty to Kermit. Permian, forty twenty. Every single line. One dollar under. And the delivery schedule…" She flipped the page. "Monahams. Monahams. Monahams. Twelve times. In both."

"When did Permian's bid come in?"

"Wednesday afternoon. Basin Rock's came in two weeks ago. Sealed. It went from my safe to your briefcase on Monday the fourth, and back into my safe Tuesday morning."

I sat back in my chair. Out the window, a flatbed was pulling out of the yard with a load of casing.

"Nell," I said, "I need to tell you something, and then I need you to decide whether you still want to help me."

I told her. About the kitchen table, and laughing at the typo, and Trina asking what was funny. About the briefcase left open overnight. About Trina's things already at Bodie's, and the black Tahoe in the parking lot.

Nell didn't say anything for a while. Then she took off her glasses and cleaned them on her blouse, which is what she does when she's about to say something she means.

"Dwayne, I've worked for three vice presidents in this building," she said. "Two of them would've shredded that bid and never told anybody it leaked out of their house. You're telling me."

"It leaked out of my house. That makes it my problem."

"It makes it the company's problem," Nell said. "And you can't touch it. You know that, right? You can't decide this one. If you throw out Bodie's bid, his lawyer says you did it because he's sleeping with your wife. If you keep it, you're rewarding a cheat."

"I know."

"So what are you going to do?"

"I'm going to walk upstairs," I said, "and tell Royal Crenshaw that my wife may have stolen a sealed bid off my kitchen table and given it to her boyfriend. And then I'm going to recuse myself, and let the audit committee decide. With you presenting."

Nell put her glasses back on.

"Me?"

"You found it. You read the small print. Nobody in Odessa is going to say Nell Ybarra did anybody a favor."

Royal heard the whole thing sitting behind his big oak desk with both hands on the head of his cane. He didn't interrupt once. When I was done, he was quiet so long I thought he might have fallen asleep.

"Thirty-one years," he finally said. "Thirty-one years, and the first thing you do as CEO is come in here and tell on yourself."

"I'm not CEO till January."

"You're CEO right now, son. That's why you came in." He turned to Nell. "Audit committee meets on the twenty-ninth. You'll lay it out. Mr. Kincannon will be invited to present his bid in person and answer questions, same as every year. Dwayne won't be in the room for the vote." He looked back at me. "But you'll be in the building. I want you where I can see you."

"Yes, sir."

"And Dwayne." Royal's voice went low. "Get yourself a lawyer. Not the company's. Yours."

At eleven-thirty I was across a desk from Marlena Sloat, attorney, in a little office in a strip center on 42nd Street between a nail salon and a vape shop. Marlena has represented half the divorcing oilmen in Ector County. She wears turquoise rings on every finger and talks like she's reading a weather report.

"Texas is a community property state," she said. "Everything either of you earned since 1998 is half hers, half yours, no matter whose name is on it. That's the bad news. The good news is the same rule works backwards. When the divorce is final, everything you earn after, including a CEO salary, is yours alone. When does the new job start?"

"January first."

"When did the board vote?"

"November."

"Is there a signed contract?"

"Royal gave it to me at the barbecue. In the envelope. I haven't signed it."

Marlena's eyebrows went up a quarter of an inch, which for Marlena is a gasp.

"Don't," she said. "Not until you and I read every page. And I'd like a copy of anything showing your wife photographed or copied company documents."

"I don't have anything like that. Just a typo."

"Typos have put better people than Bodie Kincannon out of business." She wrote something down. "And Mr. Coburn? Whatever this man says to you, say it back in writing."

My phone buzzed on her desk.

It was the front desk at Crenshaw Oilfield Supply.

"Mr. Coburn? There's a Mr. Kincannon in the lobby. He says he has coffee for you and he'll wait."

He was still there when I got back at a quarter past twelve, sitting on the leather couch in our lobby under the framed photo of Royal's first truck, with two cups from the Starbucks on 42nd going cold beside him. He stood up when I came in, all teeth.

"Dwayne. Thanks for seeing me."

"I haven't yet."

"Five minutes. That's all I ask."

I took him upstairs. Not into my office. Into the small conference room with the glass wall, where Nell could see us from her desk, and I left the door open.

"Look," Bodie said, before he even sat down. "I'm a straight shooter. I'll be straight. Friday was ugly. I didn't know who you were. If I'd known, I'd never have…" He spread his hands. "You know."

"Never have what? Taken my wife to my company barbecue?"

"Never have embarrassed you like that. That's not who I am."

"Who are you, Bodie?"

"I'm a guy who's been supplying sand to this company for six years without one missed load." He leaned forward. "And I'm a guy who's about to lose a nine-million-dollar contract over something personal. That's not right and you know it."

"Nobody's decided anything about the contract."

"Come on. You're the new boss. You've got my bid on your desk. You think I don't know what happens next?"

"I'm not deciding it."

He laughed. "Sure."

"I've recused myself. The audit committee decides on the twenty-ninth. You'll be invited to present, same as every year. Nell will email you the time."

He stopped laughing. He looked through the glass wall at Nell, who looked right back at him and didn't blink.

"Recused," he said. "Huh." Then he put both forearms on the table and lowered his voice, like we were two old boys at a deer camp. "Okay. Then let's talk about the personal side. Man to man. Trina's a great gal. But she's a lot of maintenance, if you know what I mean. And it's pretty clear she wants to come home. So here's what I'm saying. I step back. Completely. I don't call her, I don't see her. She's all yours. And in return, you put in a good word with the committee. That's all. Just a word."

I looked at him for a long time.

"You're offering me my wife back," I said, "for a sand contract."

"I'm offering everybody a happy ending."

"She's not yours to give back, Bodie. She was never mine to trade, either. She's a grown woman. She picks what she picks."

His face changed then. The smile went away, and what was under it wasn't nearly as handsome.

"You're making a mistake, pitmaster," he said. "She's going to take you to the cleaners. Half of everything. Half that CEO money. Her lawyer already told her."

"Already?"

"Friday night." He sat back, pleased with himself. "Before she ever came home to make you lasagna. She called him from my truck."

"Bodie," I said, "thank you. That's the most useful thing anybody's said to me all day."

He frowned like he'd stepped on something.

"Now I'm going to ask you to leave," I said. "And Nell's going to walk you down, and she's going to write down that you came in here and offered to trade my wife for a contract. Because in this building, we write things down."

He left. He took both coffees with him.

Trina's lawyer's letter came by email at four o'clock on Wednesday, the twentieth. Her lawyer was a man named Russ Pittman from a big office in Midland with billboards on the interstate. The letter said Trina was filing for divorce, that I had "systematically concealed a major change in the family's financial circumstances," and that she would be seeking "a just and right share of all compensation, equity and benefits arising from Mr. Coburn's appointment as chief executive officer, which was negotiated during the marriage."

I forwarded it to Marlena. Then I drove to the house, because Kaycee had called and said, "Daddy, you need to come home right now, Mom's packing your stuff."

She wasn't packing my stuff. She was packing her own, into the big plastic tubs from the garage, but she'd stacked a few of my things on top for effect: my deer rifle in its case, my box of old Royal Crenshaw Christmas cards, a framed picture of me at the 2004 barbecue holding up a brisket.

Our daughter was standing in the living room with her arms crossed. Kaycee is twenty-four, a nurse at Medical Center Hospital, and she's got her grandmother's temper and my patience, in that order.

"Tell him," Kaycee said to her mother. "Tell him what you just told me."

"Kaycee, stay out of this."

"No. You said it in front of me, you can say it in front of him."

Trina turned around holding a stack of bath towels.

"Fine," she said. "I said that you lied to me for a month about the most important thing that ever happened to this family, and that a judge is going to see that, and that I'm entitled to half of it. That's what I said. Because it's true."

"I told you in November."

"You made a joke in a restaurant!"

"I said, 'Royal wants me to take over.' I said it twice. You said, 'Take over the smoker.' You thought it was so funny you told the waitress."

"You should have made me listen!"

"How?"

"I don't know! You're the big executive now, figure it out!" She threw the towels in a tub. "Twenty-six years, Dwayne. Twenty-six years I've sat at a table with you while you talked about trucks and pipe and loads and I never heard one word about money, about stock, about anything. And then I find out at a barbecue, in front of two hundred people, with Bodie standing right there, like an idiot."

"With Bodie standing right there," I said. "Where you'd put him."

"I was leaving you because you were nothing!" She stopped. She heard it. Kaycee heard it too. "I mean… I thought… you know what I mean."

"I know exactly what you mean," I said. "I knew it Friday. I just wanted to see if you'd say it twice."

"Mom," Kaycee said quietly. "Oh my God."

Trina picked up her purse.

"Russ says I'm entitled to half the CEO deal. Half the stock. Half everything. He says you can't hide behind January first."

"Trina," I said, "I want you to listen to me now, because I'm not going to make you do it again. I haven't signed the CEO contract. I'm not going to sign it until this is over, and when I sign it, it'll be a new contract for a single man. The board will see to that. And there's something you should tell Russ before he gets any further."

"What?"

"That the audit committee is meeting on the twenty-ninth about the frac sand contract. Permian Sand's bid is one dollar a ton under a competitor's sealed bid, to the penny, on every line. And it's got the competitor's spelling mistake in it twelve times. The same spelling mistake I laughed about at our kitchen table on December fourth, when you asked me what was so funny."

Kaycee looked at her mother. Her mother didn't look at anybody.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Trina said.

"You might want to ask Bodie, then. He'll be at our office Friday at two, presenting."

She walked out. She left the tubs. She left my rifle and the Christmas cards and the photo of the 2004 brisket, and she drove off in the Lexus I'd bought her for her fiftieth birthday, going to the address in Midland where her things already were.

Kaycee came and stood next to me and leaned her head on my shoulder, the way she used to when she was nine.

"Daddy," she said, "did she really take your papers?"

"I don't know for sure, baby."

"Yes you do," Kaycee said.

At a little after one on Friday, the twenty-ninth, my phone rang. It was Nell.

"He's here early," she said. "And Dwayne? He brought her."

The big conference room at Crenshaw Oilfield Supply has a long oak table that Royal bought from a bankrupt bank in 1986, a framed aerial photo of the pipe yard, and a coffee urn that has never once made good coffee. I wasn't in the room for the vote. Royal had promised that. But the committee had asked me to be there for the first ten minutes, to state on the record why I'd stepped aside, and then walk out.

So I was sitting in the chair by the door, not at the table, when Bodie Kincannon walked in with a leather portfolio and a fresh haircut, and my wife walked in behind him in a navy blue dress I'd never seen.

Royal was at the head of the table. Next to him, the two outside board members on the audit committee: a retired banker named Vernon Askew and a woman named Dr. Patrice Lyles who ran the petroleum engineering program at the college. Nell sat at the far end with three folders in front of her.

"Mrs. Coburn," Royal said, pleasant as a Sunday. "I don't believe you're on the agenda."

"I'm here to support Bodie," Trina said. "And as Dwayne's wife, I think I have a right to see this is done fair."

"As Dwayne's wife," Royal said, "you have the right to sit in the lobby. But I'll let you stay, since you came all this way. Sit by the wall."

She sat by the wall. Two chairs from me. She didn't look at me.

"Mr. Coburn," Royal said. "For the record."

I stood up.

"On December fourth, I took the three sealed bids for the frac sand renewal home in my briefcase to review them. I left the briefcase on my kitchen table overnight. On December thirteenth, Permian Sand's bid came in. On December fifteenth, Ms. Ybarra brought a problem with it to my attention. Because of my personal relationship with some of the people involved, I've recused myself from this decision." I looked at Royal. "That's all I've got."

"Thank you, Dwayne."

I sat back down. I should have left right then. Royal looked at me, and then he looked at Trina, and he said, "Stay a minute, son," and I stayed.

"Mr. Kincannon," Royal said. "Go ahead."

Bodie stood up, opened his portfolio, and gave the best sales pitch I've ever heard. I'll give him that. Six years of on-time loads. A new transload facility in Pecos. Thirty-seven forty a ton, the lowest price in the Permian Basin, a dollar under anybody. He had a slideshow. He had a chart with an arrow going up. He made a joke about the Cowboys that got a laugh out of Vernon Askew.

When he was done, Dr. Lyles folded her hands.

"Mr. Kincannon. Your price is exactly one dollar under Basin Rock's on every single line item. How did you arrive at that?"

"Market intelligence, ma'am. We know our competitors."

"That well?"

"We've been in this business a long time."

"Ms. Ybarra," Royal said. "Would you show the committee what you showed me?"

Nell opened her first folder and laid two pages side by side in the middle of the oak table. Basin Rock's delivery schedule. Permian Sand's delivery schedule. She'd highlighted one word on each, twelve times, in yellow.

"Basin Rock's bid," Nell said, "was sealed and delivered to my office on December first. Their estimator misspelled Monahans. M-O-N-A-H-A-M-S. Every line. Permian Sand's bid was delivered on December thirteenth. It contains the identical misspelling. Every line. In the same twelve places."

Vernon Askew picked up both pages and held them close to his face.

"Well, I'll be," he said.

"I called Basin Rock's estimator on the eighteenth," Nell said. "A man named Hector Ruelas. I asked him if he'd ever shared his bid with anybody. He said no. He said he's been typing Monahans wrong for eleven years and his boss is going to kill him. Then he asked me why I wanted to know, and I didn't tell him."

Bodie Kincannon was still standing. He'd put his hands flat on the table.

"That's a coincidence," he said. "Monahans is a hard word."

"It's eight letters," Royal said. "I've been spelling it since 1971."

"Mr. Kincannon," Dr. Lyles said, very calm. "Did you have access to Basin Rock's sealed bid before you prepared yours?"

"No, ma'am."

"Did anyone provide you any information about it?"

Bodie looked at the wall where my wife was sitting. It was half a second. Everybody at that table saw it.

"No, ma'am."

"Ms. Ybarra," Royal said. "The second folder."

Nell opened it. Inside was a single sheet of paper, typed and signed. She slid it across to Dr. Lyles.

"This is a memo I wrote on December eighteenth," Nell said. "At about twelve-thirty in the afternoon. Mr. Kincannon came to this building uninvited and met with Mr. Coburn in the small conference room, with the door open. I was at my desk. I heard him offer to, and I'm quoting, 'step back completely' from Mrs. Coburn if Mr. Coburn would 'put in a good word with the committee.' Mr. Coburn told him she wasn't his to trade and asked him to leave. I wrote it down immediately afterward and Mr. Coburn signed it as accurate."

The room was dead quiet. Even the coffee urn had quit gurgling.

Trina turned her head slowly and looked at Bodie.

"You did what?" she said.

"Trina, sit down."

"You offered to give me back? For the contract?"

"It was a negotiation. That's business. You don't understand business."

"You told me you loved me!"

"Mrs. Coburn," Royal said, "this isn't the time."

"No, I want to know." She was on her feet now. "Bodie, you told me Dwayne would sign it before New Year's. You said once he found out about us he'd be too embarrassed to say no. You said the contract was a done deal, that's why you needed…"

She stopped.

Bodie Kincannon's face went a color I've only seen on a man after he's touched a live line.

"That's why he needed what, Mrs. Coburn?" Dr. Lyles said gently.

Trina looked at me. For the first time since she'd walked in, she looked right at me. I didn't say anything. I didn't help her. I just looked back.

"He needed the numbers," she said, in a very small voice. "Off the kitchen table. He said it was just for… for reference. He said everybody does it. He said Dwayne would never notice, because Dwayne never notices anything." She swallowed. "I took pictures of the pages with my phone while he was asleep. I sent them to Bodie. That's all. That's all I did."

"That's all," Royal said.

"She's lying," Bodie said. "She's trying to save herself. She's the one who…"

"She just said she did it," Vernon Askew said. "Son, nobody lies to put themselves in that kind of trouble."

Bodie snapped his portfolio shut.

"This is a setup," he said. "This whole thing. He set me up. He left that briefcase out on purpose."

"I left my briefcase on my own kitchen table," I said. "In my own house. For twenty-six years. I'd do it again tomorrow. That's what a house is for."

Royal tapped his cane once on the floor.

"Mr. Kincannon," he said. "This committee doesn't need to vote. Permian Sand and Logistics is disqualified from this bid and from any future bid with Crenshaw Oilfield Supply. Your current contract expires Sunday at midnight, and it won't be renewed. Ms. Ybarra will be sending a copy of these pages to your company's president and to Basin Rock's lawyer this afternoon. What they do with them is their business." He nodded toward the door. "Nell, would you walk Mr. Kincannon out? Take the stairs. He looks like he could use the exercise."

Bodie looked at Trina. Trina looked at him. Then he picked up his portfolio and walked out without her, and Nell followed two steps behind him with her folders under her arm like a bailiff.

Through the glass, I saw him stop on the landing and get out his phone. I heard him say, "Mr. Gentry, it's Bodie, I need to explain something before you hear it from…" and then the stairwell door shut on him.

Dr. Lyles cleared her throat.

"Royal," she said, "what about Mrs. Coburn? She's admitted to photographing confidential company documents."

"She's not our employee," Royal said. "Basin Rock may have something to say about it. Their lawyer is a man who doesn't like to lose." He looked at Trina. "Mrs. Coburn, my advice is that you call your own lawyer. Not Mr. Pittman. A real one."

Trina stood in the middle of that conference room in her navy dress with nobody on her side of the table. Then she turned to me.

"Dwayne," she said. "Please. Can we talk outside? Just you and me. Please."

I looked at Royal. He nodded.

We stood in the hallway outside the conference room, by the framed picture of the 1974 barbecue, the one with Royal in a cowboy hat and a brisket the size of a suitcase. She had mascara running down both cheeks. She was holding her phone in both hands like it might save her.

"I'm so sorry," she said. "I'm so sorry. He made it sound like nothing. He made it sound like you'd never know and nobody would get hurt. I was so angry at you for being… for being you. For being so ordinary. And then Friday, in that tent, when Royal pointed at you…" She was crying hard now. "I'm the idiot. I'm the nothing. Not you. I'll do anything. I'll tell Russ to drop the whole thing. I'll sign whatever Marlena wants. Just please, please don't let them come after me. Please be on my side. You're my husband. I found out at the barbecue that I married somebody truly successful. I just didn't know it."

I took Bodie Kincannon's business card out of my shirt pocket, the one he'd tucked into my apron at the barbecue, the one I'd said I'd keep for the file. I put it in her hand.

"You found someone truly successful, Trina," I said. "Here's his number."
