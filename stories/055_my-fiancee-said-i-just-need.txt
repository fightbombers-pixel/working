"I just need one last trip with my ex before I marry you. For closure."

My fiancée said it across a table of six sample cakes at Rosie's Bakery on South Grand, in front of the baker, her maid of honor, and a teenage girl refilling the coffee pot. Four people heard it. One of them dropped a fork.

I looked down at the slice of lemon cake on my plate. Eleven weeks to the wedding. I had already put $14,600 into it, and the woman across from me had put in a Pinterest board.

"Of course," I said.

Then I picked up the little card Rosie had given us, the one with the boxes for flavor and filling and frosting, and I folded it in half and slid it into my shirt pocket without checking a single box.

Nine days later, my fiancée was sitting in a white wicker chair in a parish hall with forty-one women watching her, holding a gift card she couldn't stop reading, and her hands were shaking so bad the envelope rattled.

What she didn't know, sitting there at Rosie's licking frosting off her thumb, was whose name was printed on the contract for the barn.

My name is Grady Sorrell. I'm 46, and I build stairs. Railings, banisters, curved treads, the kind of staircase people put on the cover of their Christmas card. I've done it in Springfield, Illinois, for twenty-two years, and the one thing the trade teaches you is that a staircase is only as good as what holds it up. You can sand and stain all day long, but if the stringer is cracked, somebody's going down. Hold on to that.

Marcia was smiling at me like I'd passed a test.

"See?" she said to Brianna. "I told you he'd understand. Grady's not like other guys."

Brianna Keefe, her best friend since high school and her maid of honor, did not smile back. Brianna was looking at me the way you look at a man who just stepped on a rake and hasn't felt the handle yet.

"Which ex?" Brianna asked.

"Shane," Marcia said, like it was obvious. "Who else? Twelve years, Bri. We never really ended it right. He moved to Peoria, I moved on, and there's just stuff we never said. I don't want to walk down the aisle carrying that."

Rosie Dunn, the baker, had suddenly become very interested in a smear on the display glass.

"Where's the trip?" I asked.

"Galena. Just four days. We'll walk around, talk it out, maybe do a wine tasting. Totally innocent. Separate rooms." Marcia reached over and squeezed my hand. "You trust me, right?"

"Of course."

"See, this is why I'm marrying you." She turned to Rosie. "Okay, I think we're doing the lemon with the raspberry filling. Three tiers. Grady, you can put the deposit down now, right? Rosie needs it by Friday."

Rosie slid the order form across the counter. It had a line at the bottom for a signature and a box that said DEPOSIT: $450.

"How much was the tasting?" I asked.

"Forty," Rosie said.

I took two twenties out of my wallet and laid them on the counter. Rosie looked at the twenties, then at me, then very carefully did not look at Marcia.

"That's for today," I said. "Thank you, Rosie. That chocolate one was really something."

"Babe," Marcia said. "The deposit."

"I've got to get back to the Hendricks job. We'll do the paperwork later."

"Later when? I'm leaving Saturday."

"Then it'll keep till you're back."

She rolled her eyes and laughed, and the teenager with the coffee pot laughed too, nervously, because when people don't know what's happening they laugh along with whoever sounds the most sure of themselves. Marcia always sounded sure.

"He gets like this when he's on a deadline," she told Rosie. "Oh, and Grady, remember, after the honeymoon we're going to the title office about the house. I want my name on it before we do the kitchen."

"I remember," I said.

I did remember. She'd brought it up three times in a month. Remember that, too.

I walked out to my truck and sat there with the engine off and Rosie's folded card in my pocket. Through the bakery window I could see Marcia talking with her hands, laughing, and Brianna sitting very still beside her.

My phone buzzed in the cup holder.

It was a text from Brianna.

"Can you meet me tonight? Not about cake. Please don't tell Marcia."

My shop sits behind my house on Walnut Street, a pole barn with a wood stove and a radio that's been on the same classic rock station since the Clinton administration. When Brianna's car pulled up a little after seven, my sister Joanie was already there, sitting on a sawhorse and eating sunflower seeds out of the bag.

Joanie is three years older than me and runs the parts counter at the John Deere dealer out on Dirksen Parkway. She has never liked a single woman I've dated, and she's been right about every one of them, which is the most annoying thing a sister can be.

"You told her?" Brianna asked, stopping in the doorway.

"I called her the minute I left the bakery," I said. "She's my sister. She doesn't talk."

"I talk all the time," Joanie said. "Just not to anybody who matters. Come on in, hon. He's got a chair somewhere under the sawdust."

Brianna sat on an upturned bucket. She's 40, a nurse at Memorial, with short hair and a no-nonsense way about her that I always liked. She'd been Marcia's friend for twenty-five years. She was holding her car keys so tight her knuckles were pale.

"I almost didn't come," she said.

"But you did."

"Because I can't stand up there in a purple dress in eleven weeks and hand her the bouquet while you say vows to her. I can't do it, Grady. I won't."

"Okay," I said. "Tell me."

She took a breath.

"It's not one last trip. It's not the first trip either."

Joanie stopped chewing.

"Shane came back around in February," Brianna said. "He called her on her birthday. Then they had lunch. Then they had lunch again. She told me it was just catching up. In April she told me they'd kissed in his car outside the Hilton and it 'didn't mean anything.' In May she spent a Saturday in Peoria. You thought she was at a dental conference in Bloomington."

I remembered the dental conference. She'd brought me back a pen.

"She brought me back a pen," I said.

"I know. I watched her buy it at a gas station."

Joanie let out a low whistle. "A gas station pen. That's cold, Grady. That's a whole new level of cold."

"Why tell me now?" I asked Brianna. "Why not in April?"

"Because in April she swore it was over. She swore on her dad's grave. And I believed her, because I wanted to." Brianna rubbed her face. "Then last week she asked me to cover for her. She wanted me to say I was going to Galena too. A girls' trip. Then this morning, at the bakery, she just said it right out loud in front of you, like she knew you'd roll over. And you did. You said 'of course.'"

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because she asked," I said. "She wanted a trip with her ex. I'm not going to stop a grown woman from going where she wants to go."

Brianna stared at me. Joanie smiled the slow smile she gets when she figures out what I'm doing before I've told her.

"There's one more thing," Brianna said. "And this is the part that made me get in the car."

She pulled out her phone and turned it toward me. It was a screenshot of a text, from Marcia to Brianna, dated three weeks back.

"Booked the DeSoto House!! Put it on the wedding card, he never looks at that statement. Told you I'd figure it out."

I read it twice.

"Three weeks ago," I said.

"Three weeks before she asked you. She wasn't asking you for anything this morning. She was announcing it so nobody could say she hid it."

The wedding card was a credit card in my name, with Marcia as an authorized user, that we'd opened in March for wedding expenses only. I had set it up so every charge was a dress, a flower deposit or a DJ. I paid it off every month without reading it, because I trusted the woman using it.

I got my laptop off the workbench and opened the account. Joanie came and stood behind me.

There it was, three weeks old. The DeSoto House, Galena, Illinois. $1,184. Right between the florist and a $60 charge at a place called Lash Lounge.

"Twelve hundred bucks of my money," I said, "so she can find closure with another man."

"Closure sounds expensive," Joanie said. "I've been doing it wrong. I just stopped calling people."

Brianna almost laughed, then didn't.

"What about the house?" she asked. "Did she tell you she wants to go to the title office?"

"Three times."

"Shane sells houses. Flips condos, mostly. She told me he said a woman should never marry a man without getting on the deed first. She said Shane said your house on Walnut is worth $310,000 now and you bought it for ninety."

I looked at Joanie. Joanie looked at me.

The house was my grandfather's. I bought it from his estate in 2004 and I'd put eleven years of weekends into it. Every stair in it I built myself.

"So that's the plan," Joanie said. "Marry the stair guy, get on the deed, keep the ex on the side."

"I don't know if that's the plan," Brianna said. "I just know what she said and what she did. You can figure out the rest."

"Why are you really doing this, Bri?" I asked. "She's been your friend since you were fifteen."

She was quiet for a while. The wood stove ticked.

"Because I was married once," she said. "To a man who did exactly this to me. And every one of his friends knew, and not one of them told me. I swore I'd never be one of those friends."

I nodded. That was a good enough reason for me.

"So what are you going to do?" she asked. "Are you going to confront her before she leaves?"

"No."

"Then what?"

I took Rosie's folded card out of my shirt pocket and set it on the workbench next to the laptop.

"Tomorrow morning," I said, "I'm going to call the caterer and the venue."

The barn venue was called Sangamon Hollow, out west of town past the old Route 66 alignment, a red barn with string lights and a gravel lot and a pond where people take pictures at sunset. Fern Haskell owns it. She's 64, widowed, wears flannel and pearls at the same time, and books two years out.

I drove out there at eight the next morning. Marcia had taken Friday off, she said, to pack.

Fern was on her porch with coffee. She watched me get out of the truck like she already knew it wasn't good news.

"Grady Sorrell," she said. "You're not here to add a second bar."

"No, ma'am."

"Sit down, then. You want coffee?"

"Please."

She poured it from a thermos and handed me a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST GRANDMA.

"I need to cancel the wedding," I said.

Fern set her own mug down very gently on the porch rail.

"Oh, honey."

"I know it's eleven weeks. I know there's a penalty. I read the contract."

"You'd be the first one who ever did." She went inside and came back with a folder. She opened it and ran her finger down the page. "Sorrell-Pike wedding, Saturday, August 23rd. Contract holder: Grady Sorrell. Deposit paid: $8,000. Cancellation more than sixty days out, we keep twenty percent." She looked up. "That's $1,600. I send you back $6,400."

"That's fair."

"Is it fair? Because I've been doing this thirty years and I can tell you, most men who cancel a wedding come out here with a story about cold feet. You don't look like you have cold feet."

"No, ma'am. My feet are fine."

She studied me for a while.

"Marcia know you're here?"

"Not yet."

"Mm." Fern closed the folder. "You know, her mother's in my Bible study at First Presbyterian. Lorraine Pike. Tuesday mornings. Lorraine's been talking about this wedding since Easter. She's hosting the bridal shower a week from Saturday."

"I know."

"She invited me to it."

That I didn't know. I took a sip of coffee and let it sit there.

"Am I supposed to keep this quiet?" Fern asked.

"I'm not going to ask you to lie for me, Fern. And I'm not going to ask you to say anything either. You do what you'd do with any client."

"With any client," she said, "I'd send the refund to the contract holder and a written notice to the contract holder. Which is you." She tapped the folder. "I don't call brides. Brides aren't on my contract unless the groom puts them there."

"Then that's what you'd do."

She signed the cancellation form, had me initial it in two places, and wrote out a refund check right there on the porch. She handed it to me, then held on to it for one second before she let go.

"Whatever she did," Fern said, "I'm sorry."

"So am I."

I called the caterer from the gravel lot. Bea Castillo runs a family outfit out of a storefront on Ninth Street, and she cooks the best pulled pork in Sangamon County. Bea picked up on the second ring with a lot of noise behind her.

"Castillo's."

"Bea, it's Grady Sorrell. The August 23rd wedding at Sangamon Hollow."

"The stair man! You want to add the brisket? I told your girl she'd want the brisket."

"I need to cancel."

The noise behind her stopped like somebody shut a door.

"Cancel."

"Yes, ma'am. I'm sorry. I know you've bought some things already."

"I haven't bought nothing for August, hon, it's June." She was quiet. "You want the deposit back? It's $1,500. My contract says I keep $300 if it's more than sixty days."

"Keep $300. Send me the rest."

"You okay, Grady?"

"I will be."

"Hm. Funny thing. Lorraine Pike called me last week. She booked me to do the food for the bridal shower. Little sandwiches, a fruit tray, the punch with the sherbet in it."

"I know about the shower."

"You want me to cancel that too?"

"No," I said. "That's Lorraine's. Lorraine paid for that."

"She did," Bea said. "Okay. I'll see her there, then."

I hung up and sat in the truck with Fern's check on the dashboard. Two calls, $7,600 back. The florist, the DJ, the photographer and Rosie's cake could wait till Monday. It took twenty-five minutes to cancel a wedding that had taken six months to plan.

My phone buzzed.

It was Marcia. She'd sent a picture: her own reflection in a car window, sunglasses pushed up on her head, the sign for a Casey's gas station behind her on the Galena road. She'd left a day early.

"Couldn't wait to get on the road!! Shane's driving. Don't wait up for my calls, reception is terrible out here, lol. Love you babe."

Then a second message came in under it, two seconds later.

"Oops wrong pic, meant to send Bri the cute one."

I spread two fingers on the screen and made the picture bigger.

It was a selfie in a car window, sure. But the car window was the passenger side of a silver Lexus, and in the reflection, sitting behind the wheel, was a man in a ball cap with his hand resting on her knee. On the dashboard, where the sun hit it, there was a paper sleeve from a hotel key card. I could read the red letters. THE DESOTO HOUSE. Under that, in blue pen, somebody had written "1 room, 3 nights."

Separate rooms, she'd said. Totally innocent.

I didn't answer the text. I put the phone face down on the seat, put the truck in gear, and drove to the Hendricks job, where a widow on Lincoln Park had been waiting two weeks for a white oak handrail. I installed it. It was level and it was solid, and when Mrs. Hendricks put her weight on it, it didn't move.

That's the thing about a railing. It doesn't care how you feel.

Saturday I cancelled the florist, the DJ and the photographer. The florist cried. The DJ asked if I wanted to book him for something else, maybe a divorce party, and I said I wasn't married yet, and he said, "Even better, man, a not-divorce party." The photographer kept $500 and wished me luck.

Rosie Dunn I called last.

"I never put a deposit down," I said. "So there's nothing to cancel. I just didn't want you to hold the date."

"I wasn't holding it," Rosie said. "Not after Tuesday. I've been baking for thirty-one years, Grady. You get a feel for which cakes are going to get eaten."

Sunday I took Marcia's things out of the master closet and put them in the guest room, folded, on hangers, nothing thrown. Her coffee mug with the tooth on it went in a box with her hair dryer and her dental office fleece. I didn't change the locks. It was my house and she was my fiancée, and I wasn't going to treat her like a burglar until she gave me a reason I could hold in my hand.

Joanie came over Sunday night with a rotisserie chicken and watched me tape the last box.

"You're too calm," she said. "It's starting to scare me."

"What do you want me to do? Throw her clothes on the lawn?"

"Kind of, yeah. A little bit. For the neighbors."

"She asked me for one last trip with her ex. I said of course. I'm just taking her at her word."

"Which word?"

"Last."

Joanie set down her drumstick. "Oh," she said. "Oh, Grady. That's mean. I love it."

Monday evening a little after six, the silver Lexus pulled up in front of my house on Walnut Street and Marcia got out of the passenger side.

I watched from the kitchen window. She leaned back into the car for a while. Then she pulled her rolling suitcase out of the back seat and the Lexus drove off fast, before it got to the corner, like the driver didn't want to be seen in front of my house.

She came in sunburned and smiling, with a shopping bag from a fudge shop.

"Babe! I'm home! I brought you maple walnut."

"Thanks."

She dropped the bag on the counter and kissed me on the cheek. She smelled like somebody else's car.

"Galena is so cute," she said. "You'd hate it. Too many little shops."

"How was the closure?"

"Honestly? So good. We talked about everything. I cried, he cried. It's done. It's over. I'm all yours." She stretched and yawned. "I'm going to shower and then we have to talk about the shower. My mom is going crazy. Forty-one RSVPs, Grady. Forty-one!"

"That's a lot of women."

"Your mom's coming, right? She said she's bringing the quilt."

My mother had been working on a wedding quilt since Christmas. Double wedding ring pattern, every stitch by hand. She'd planned to give it to Marcia at the shower in front of all the ladies.

"She's coming," I said.

Marcia headed for the stairs. Halfway up, she stopped. She was looking through the open guest room door at the hangers and the boxes.

"Why is my stuff in the guest room?"

"I'm refinishing the floor in the master. Figured I'd get it out of the way."

"Now? Eleven weeks before the wedding?"

"Good time to do it. Nobody's in there."

She narrowed her eyes. Then she laughed. "You and your floors. Fine. But I'm sleeping with you."

"Guest bed's made up. The fumes are bad."

"Grady."

"Of course," I said. "Sleep wherever you want."

She went up. I heard the shower go on. Then I went back to the kitchen and picked up the white business envelope that had come in the afternoon mail. SANGAMON HOLLOW, it said in the corner. NOTICE OF CANCELLATION. It had been sitting next to the fudge bag the whole time she was standing at the counter, right where she'd set her keys.

I put it in my back pocket.

Upstairs the water shut off. Her phone, which she'd left on the kitchen counter, lit up and buzzed against the granite. I didn't pick it up. I didn't have to. The preview was right there on the lock screen, big enough to read from across the room.

"Shane: Got home ok. Already miss room 112. When are you telling him about the title office?"

I left the phone where it was.

She came down twenty minutes later in her robe and went straight for it. She read the screen, glanced at me, and turned it face down.

"Shane says hi," she said. "He says thanks for being so mature about everything."

"Tell him he's welcome."

"You're being weird."

"I'm being supportive. You asked me to be."

"No, this is weird. You're doing the thing where you go all quiet and polite." She crossed her arms. "Are you mad about the trip? Because you said 'of course.' You can't say 'of course' and then be mad."

"I'm not mad."

"Then what?"

"Then nothing. You went. You came back. You found closure. That's what you wanted."

"I did." She stared at me. "I did find closure."

"I believe you."

She didn't like that. She'd come home ready for a fight she could cry her way through, and there wasn't one. She stood there in her robe for a second like a woman who walks into a room and forgets why she came in.

"Okay," she finally said. "Okay. Good. Because Saturday is a big deal for my mom, and I don't want any drama. Promise me no drama."

"I promise I won't start anything."

"Good." She softened and came over and hugged me around the middle. "You're the best, you know that? Shane said most guys would have lost it."

"Shane would know," I said.

She pulled back to look at me, and I could see her trying to decide whether that was a joke. Then my phone rang on the counter.

It said LORRAINE PIKE.

"Your mom," I said.

"Why is my mother calling you?" Marcia said. "She never calls you."

I answered it on speaker, because I had nothing to hide.

"Grady, honey," Lorraine said. Lorraine is 68 and talks like she's reading a recipe, every word measured. "Is Marcia back? She's not answering me."

"She's right here, Lorraine."

"Oh good. Marcia, sweetheart, I need the final count for Bea's sandwiches by Wednesday. And Grady, I wanted to ask you something. Fern Haskell said the strangest thing to me on the phone today."

Marcia went still.

"What did Fern say?" I asked.

"She asked me if the shower was still on. I said of course it's still on, why wouldn't it be? And she just patted my hand and said, 'Bless you, Lorraine.' Like somebody died." Lorraine paused. "Is there something I should know?"

Marcia was looking at me with her mouth slightly open.

"Lorraine," I said, "everything's on schedule for Saturday. I'll be there to help Mom carry in the quilt."

"Well, all right. Fern's getting older. Maybe she mixed me up with somebody." Lorraine sighed. "Marcia, call me back. I mean it."

The line clicked. Marcia kept staring at me.

"What was that about?"

"No idea," I said. "Fern's getting older."

Wednesday morning I was gluing up a curved handrail in the shop when a silver Lexus pulled into my gravel drive.

Shane Kittredge got out. He was 45, with a good haircut and a quarter-zip pullover in June, the kind of guy who calls every stranger "my man" and every woman "lady." He sells condos in Peoria and flips a few, and his face is on a bus bench on War Memorial Drive.

He walked into my shop without knocking, looked around at the sawdust and the clamps, and smiled like he was appraising it.

"Grady. My man. Shane Kittredge."

"I know who you are."

"Thought it was time we met, you know? Man to man. Clear the air." He put out his hand.

I had glue on both of mine. I held them up. He put his hand in his pocket.

"What do you want, Shane?"

"Hey, nothing. Just wanted to say thanks. Seriously. For letting Marcia do the Galena thing. Not a lot of guys would be that evolved."

"How'd you know where I live?"

"Marcia gave me the address. For the RSVP." He grinned. "I'm invited to the wedding. Did she not tell you? Plus-one, front table. No hard feelings, right?"

"Front table."

"Hey, closure's closure. We're all adults." He wandered over to my workbench and picked up a block plane like he'd never seen one. "This is nice work. She said you're good with your hands."

"Put that down."

He put it down. "Easy, easy."

"You came here for something. Say it."

He leaned on the bench. "Okay. Real talk. Marcia's worried about you. She says you've been weird since she got back. Polite weird. She thinks you might do something stupid, like back out. And I just want you to know, as a guy who's been there, that would be a huge mistake. She's an amazing woman."

"Timing."

"For twelve years."

"But you want her married."

"I want her happy."

"To me."

"To whoever makes her happy, man."

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"You told her a woman should never marry a man without getting on the deed first. You told her my house is worth $310,000 now. You told her I bought it for ninety."

"Who told you that?"

"Is it true?"

"I'm in real estate, Grady. I talk about houses. That's my job. I tell every client the same thing. Protect yourself."

"She's my friend."

"I think I've got the right idea and the wrong fiancée."

"I'm not making anything. I promised her no drama."

She's going to that shower Saturday, and she's going to open her presents and smile at her mother, and you're going to stand there and clap like a good boy, because that's what you do."

He climbed in the Lexus and spun gravel all the way to the street.

I wiped the glue off my hands with a rag and looked at the clock on the shop wall. Then I called the one person in this whole thing I hadn't talked to yet.

My mother picked up on the first ring. She was 74, and she'd been hand-stitching that wedding quilt every night since Christmas.

"Mom," I said. "About Saturday. I need you to bring the quilt."

"I was always bringing the quilt."

"I know. But this time I need you to give it to me, not to her."

The bridal shower was Saturday at two o'clock in the fellowship hall at First Presbyterian on Seventh Street, the one with the folding tables and the linoleum floor and the picture of the 1962 church softball team by the coffee urn.

Lorraine had done it up right. White tablecloths, pink carnations in mason jars, a banner that said MARCIA AND GRADY in glitter letters. Bea Castillo had the food out along the back wall: little sandwiches with the crusts cut off, a fruit tray shaped like a wedding bell, and the punch with the sherbet in it. There were forty-one women in that room, from Marcia's coworkers at the dental office to two great-aunts from Decatur who were both named Bernice.

Men don't usually go to bridal showers. But Lorraine had a tradition, from her own mother. The groom comes in at the end, with his mother, for the last gift.

So at a quarter to four, I opened the door to the fellowship hall and held it for my mother, and she walked in carrying the quilt in a white box with a blue ribbon.

Marcia was sitting in a white wicker chair at the front of the room, surrounded by tissue paper, wearing a paper plate on her head with all the bows stuck to it. She waved at me with both hands.

"There he is! My man! Everybody, this is Grady!"

The women clapped. A couple of them said "aww." I nodded and stood by the door with my mother.

Brianna was at a side table writing down every gift and who gave it, in a spiral notebook, the way a maid of honor does. She looked up at me once and then back at the notebook.

Fern Haskell was sitting in the third row in her pearls and her flannel. She didn't look at me at all.

"Okay, okay, a few more," Lorraine said, handing Marcia a box. "This one's from your Aunt Bernice."

"Which Bernice?"

"The tall one."

It was a toaster. Everybody laughed. Then a set of towels from the dental office, then a cookbook, then a card from a cousin with a fifty-dollar bill in it, and every time Marcia held something up she glanced at me by the door like she was making sure I was watching.

Then Lorraine picked up a plain white envelope.

"And this one is from Fern," she said. "Fern Haskell, everybody. Fern owns Sangamon Hollow, where the reception's going to be."

"Oh, Fern, you didn't have to!" Marcia said.

Fern said nothing. She just folded her hands in her lap.

Marcia slid her nail under the flap and pulled out a card. It had a picture of a dove on the front. She opened it, smiling, and started to read it out loud the way she'd read all the others.

"Dear Marcia. I'm so sorry about..."

She stopped.

She read it again, silently this time. The smile stayed on her face for about two more seconds, like a light that stays on after you flip the switch. Then it went out.

"Read it, honey," Lorraine said. "What does it say?"

"It's nothing."

"Read it."

Marcia's hands had started to shake. The envelope was rattling against the card. Forty-one women could hear it rattle.

"Fern," Marcia said. "What is this?"

"It's a sympathy card," Fern said. "I'm sorry, sweetheart. I didn't know what else to get you."

"Sympathy for what?"

"For the wedding." Fern looked at Lorraine, not at Marcia. "Lorraine, I tried to tell you at Bible study. Grady cancelled the barn a week ago Friday. I mailed the notice to the contract holder like I always do. I didn't feel right sitting here eating your sandwiches and pretending I didn't know."

The room went so quiet I could hear the coffee urn ticking by the softball picture.

Lorraine turned around very slowly. "Grady?"

Every face in that fellowship hall turned to the door. My mother put her hand on my arm.

"Is that true?" Lorraine asked. "Did you cancel the wedding?"

"Yes, ma'am."

Marcia came up out of the wicker chair. The paper plate with the bows slid off her head and landed upside down on the floor.

"You cancelled our wedding?"

"I cancelled the barn," I said. "And the caterer, and the DJ, and the florist, and the photographer."

At the back wall, Bea Castillo set down the punch ladle. "He did," Bea said. "Last week. I gave him back twelve hundred."

"Why?" Lorraine said. Her voice was shaking now, and Lorraine's voice never shook. "Grady, why would you do that and not say a word?"

I'd promised no drama. I'd promised I wouldn't start anything. But her mother had asked me a straight question in front of everyone she knew, and I wasn't going to lie to a 68-year-old woman in a church.

"Ask Marcia where she was last weekend," I said.

Lorraine turned to her daughter.

"I was in Galena," Marcia said fast. "With Bri. Girls' trip."

Over at the side table, Brianna put down her pen.

"No," Brianna said. "You weren't."

"Bri!"

"I'm not doing it, Marcia. I told you I wouldn't cover for you and I won't." Brianna stood up. Her face was red but her voice was steady. "She was in Galena with Shane Kittredge. Four days. One room. She booked it three weeks before she ever asked Grady, and she paid for it with his credit card."

There was a sound from the great-aunts like a tire losing air.

"Shane?" Lorraine said. "Shane from high school? Shane who never called you back for twelve years?"

"It was closure!" Marcia said. She swung around to me. "I told him! I asked him, right in front of everybody at the bakery, and he said yes! He said 'of course'! Tell them, Grady! You said 'of course'!"

"I did," I said.

"So you can't do this! You said it was fine!"

"You asked me for one last trip with your ex before you married me," I said. "I believed you. I took you at your word. It was going to be the last thing before the wedding. So I made sure it was the last thing."

"That's not what I meant!"

"Then you should have said what you meant."

"It was nothing! We talked! We cried! I came home to you!"

"With a fudge bag," I said. "And a text on your phone about room 112. And another one asking when you're telling me about the title office."

Lorraine's mouth pressed into a thin line. "The title office?"

"She wanted her name on my house," I said. "Before the kitchen. Shane told her a woman should never marry a man without getting on the deed first. He told her what it's worth. Three hundred and ten thousand."

"Marcia," Lorraine said. "Is that true?"

Marcia looked around the room for somebody, anybody, to be on her side. The dental office girls were looking at the floor. Bernice and Bernice had their hands over their mouths. Bea had folded her arms. Fern was holding her purse with both hands on her knees.

"Everybody does that," Marcia said. "Everybody puts both names on the house. It's normal. Mom, you and Dad had both names on the house."

"Your father and I didn't take a trip with my old boyfriend first," Lorraine said.

"You humiliated me," Marcia said to me. Her eyes had filled up and her mascara was starting to go. "In front of my whole family. In front of my mother. In a church. How could you do this to me?"

"I didn't do anything in front of anybody," I said. "I cancelled a barn on a Friday morning and I didn't say a word to a soul. You're the one who announced it in a bakery in front of four people. All I did was say 'of course.'"

She opened her mouth and nothing came out.

My mother stepped forward then. She's five foot one, and she had the white box in both arms. She walked past Marcia, past the wicker chair, and set the box on the gift table in front of Lorraine.

"Lorraine," my mother said. "I spent six months on this. Double wedding ring. Every stitch by hand." She untied the blue ribbon, lifted the lid an inch so Lorraine could see the colors, and closed it again. "I was going to give it to your daughter today. I'd like to give it to you instead, if you'll have it. You did a beautiful job on this party. You deserved a better day."

Lorraine looked at the box, and at my mother, and her chin wobbled. Two women who'd met maybe five times stood there over a quilt in a church basement and understood each other perfectly.

"Thank you, Mae," Lorraine said quietly.

Marcia made a noise. "That's my quilt."

"It was going to be," my mother said, and didn't turn around.

Marcia came at me then, across the linoleum, past the toaster and the towels, and grabbed my sleeve with both hands.

"Grady, please. Please. We can fix this. It's just a barn. We can book another barn. We can push the date. I'll never talk to Shane again, I swear to God, I'll block him right now, watch." She fumbled her phone out of her dress pocket. "Watch me. I'm doing it."

"You can do whatever you want with your phone."

"Then what do you want? Tell me what you want me to do!"

"I want my ring back," I said.

She stood there holding my sleeve. Behind her, forty-one women held their breath.

"You told Shane I'm the banister," I said. "Sturdy, always there, nobody ever looks at it. He told me that himself, in my own shop. Well, I've built a lot of staircases, Marcia. And I'll tell you what I tell every customer. You can stain it and sand it and make it shine for the Christmas card. But if the stringer underneath is cracked, it doesn't matter how pretty it looks. Somebody's going down."

She let go of my sleeve.

She looked down at her left hand for a long moment. Then she twisted the ring off, slowly, the way you take off something that's been on long enough to leave a mark, and held it out to me with her hand shaking.

I took it and put it in my shirt pocket, next to Rosie's folded cake card, where it had been riding for nine days.

From the third row, Fern Haskell cleared her throat.

"Lorraine," she said, "I've still got August 23rd open at the barn, if you ever want to renew your vows."

And Lorraine Pike, standing in the middle of her own daughter's bridal shower with a quilt in her arms, looked at the glitter banner over the gift table that still said MARCIA AND GRADY, and said in her clear recipe voice, "Bea, start wrapping up the sandwiches. Grady, would you please help me take down that banner?"
