That's what I told myself, anyway.

Leigh Ainsley was already there, standing by the rope line next to a tall girl of about seventeen with her father's chin and her mother's short gray eyes. Hannah. At their feet were two black contractor garbage bags, the heavy kind, tied at the top, and a manila envelope on top of one of them. Leigh saw me and nodded once. I nodded back. We didn't say anything. We didn't need to.

The doors slid open. People came out in clumps. A soldier coming home to a crowd that screamed. A grandma in a wheelchair. A college girl with a backpack.

Then Brice Ainsley, in a wrinkled linen shirt and a new phone in his hand, walking fast. And behind him, Kristy, with her rolling suitcase and the straw hat in her hand, like a souvenir of something.

Brice saw his daughter first. His whole face opened up.

"Hannah. Honey. Hi."

Hannah didn't move. She stood next to her mother with her arms crossed and looked at him like he was a stranger asking for directions.

Then Brice saw the garbage bags. Then he saw his wife.

"Leigh," he said. "Leigh, what is this? Not here. Please."

"Your clothes," Leigh said. "Your golf clubs are at your brother's. The locks are changed. These are from my attorney." She picked up the manila envelope and held it out. "There's a hearing in two weeks on the college money. Hannah's college money, Brice. You'll want to bring a lawyer. A good one. I hear Dubai's got great continuing education."

People at the rope line had stopped talking. A man with a sign that said WELCOME HOME GRANDPA lowered it a little to see.

"Hannah," Brice said. "Honey. Please. Let me explain."

"You missed my regional final," Hannah said. Her voice was steady. "You said you were at a conference. I won, Dad. We won. I texted you a picture. You never even opened it."

She turned around and walked toward the parking garage. Leigh looked at her husband for one more second, and then she followed her daughter, and she didn't look back.

Brice stood there holding the manila envelope and the two garbage bags at his feet, in his linen shirt, in a Minnesota airport in March, with the automatic doors sliding open and shut behind him and letting in a blast of cold air every time they did.

Then Kristy saw me.

She came straight across the arrivals hall, dragging the suitcase, straw hat swinging in her other hand. She stopped about three feet away. She looked at my uniform, the patch on my shoulder, the badge.

"You're working?"

"Just got off."

"You came here to watch." Her eyes were wet. "You came here to watch this."

"I walk through here every day."

"Eugene. Please." She took a step closer and lowered her voice. "Please. I made a mistake. A huge stupid mistake. I know that. He was... he told me things. He said Leigh was cold to him. He said I deserved to see the world. And I was so bored, Eugene. You don't know how bored I was. Thirty years of the same Sunday."

"I know exactly how many Sundays," I said. "I kept a spreadsheet."

She laughed through the tears, a short broken sound. "You did. You always did." She reached out and touched the airline patch on my sleeve with two fingers. "Can't we just stop the lawyers? Just for a minute? Can't we go home and talk? I'll put the money back. The fourteen thousand. I'll put it back tomorrow. I'll give back the miles. I just needed a break. That's all it was. Just a break."

Behind her, through the glass, I could see Brice dragging his two garbage bags out toward the taxi line by himself.

I took her hand off my sleeve, gently, and let it go.

"You got your break, Kristy," I said. "It's over now. Your flight's landed."

Then I turned around, swiped my badge on the employee door next to customs, and walked through it, and let it close behind me.
