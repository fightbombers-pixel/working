"The board voted, Floyd. You're out."

My wife said it across the long walnut table in the boardroom of the company I started in my garage thirty-one years ago, in front of four other directors, our outside lawyer and the secretary taking minutes. Then she smirked, and she leaned back in her chair, and she said the rest of it.

"My boyfriend is the new CEO now."

The man she meant was standing by the window with his hands in his pockets. Jerome Kirby, forty-six, our chief operating officer for exactly seven months. He gave me a little nod, like a car salesman who just watched you sign.

I looked down at the vote tally on the secretary's pad. Three to two. Thirty-one years, a hundred and forty employees, eleven patents, and it came down to one vote and one boyfriend.

I smiled at her. I didn't mean to. It just came.

"Check page 391 of the contract," I said.

Nineteen minutes later, the new CEO of Navarro Irrigation Supply was on the phone in the hallway with his own lawyer, reading page 391 out loud to him one word at a time, and he'd stopped smiling somewhere around the third paragraph.

What Darlene didn't know was what was in the fireproof box under my desk at home, next to my mother's rosary. A single sheet of paper with a gold seal on it, dated 1994.

My name is Floyd Navarro. I'm 60 years old, and I make parts for center-pivot irrigation systems in Lubbock, Texas. Gearboxes, drive shafts, tower couplers, the guts of those big rolling sprinklers you see crawling across cotton fields out here. My father was a welder and his father picked cotton, and the one thing they both taught me is that a field only grows what you put in the ground. Remember that.

Darlene didn't open the binder. She just looked at it like it was something the cat dragged in.

"That's the investment agreement from 2019," I said. "When Caprock Capital bought thirty percent of this company. You signed it too. Page 412."

"I signed a lot of things."

"You didn't read any of them."

I kept the patents personally and licensed them to the company, royalty-free, forever, on one condition.

"It's standard," Jerome said. "Darlene, honey, it's standard."

"You told me the board knew!"

"Madam Secretary," Gwen said, "please note the time. I move that the board terminate Jerome Kirby as chief executive officer for cause, namely undisclosed self-dealing and false statements to the board."

"Second," Ramon said.

"All in favor?"

Gwen raised her hand. Ramon raised his. I raised mine. Bert looked at Darlene, and then at the gold seal on the table, and raised his.

Darlene didn't raise hers. She was crying.

"Four to one," Gwen said. "I further move that the board reappoint Floyd Navarro as chief executive officer, effective immediately."

Four to one again.

Jerome stood up. "This is illegal. You can't..."

"Tommy," I said, and Tommy Ybarra stepped in from the hallway, where he'd been the whole time. "Mr. Kirby needs a walk to his car."

"Yes, sir." Tommy said it with a little more pleasure than he probably should have. He held the door. "This way, Mr. Kirby."

Jerome looked at Hollis. Hollis was putting his papers back in his briefcase.

"Don't call me," Hollis said.

Jerome went. Through the big window we watched him come out onto the plant floor below, Tommy one step behind him, past the CNC machines and the welders. Forty men on the floor stopped what they were doing and watched him go. Nobody took his cap off. Ruben, the young welder, flipped his mask down and went back to work.

Darlene was still sitting at the head of the table.

"Floyd," she said. "Please. I didn't know about the money. I swear to God I didn't know."

"You knew about the newspaper," I said. "You wrote it in purple ink."

"That was Jerome's idea. He said it would make it easier on you. People would be gentle."

"You told Benny Castillo I had Alzheimer's. Benny called me from his truck, Darlene. He thought I was dying."

"I didn't tell Benny anything!"

"You told the whole county. Benny reads the paper."

"I was going to fix it after. Once things settled down."

"After you sold my company for thirty-eight million to a man who was paying your boyfriend on the side."

"I didn't know about Hollis!"

"You didn't know about Hollis, you didn't know about the fee, you didn't know about page 391, and you didn't know about the fire box." I leaned on the walnut table. "Darlene, you were on the board for twenty years. What did you know?"

She didn't answer. She looked around the table for help, at Bert, at Gwen, at the lawyer from Dallas, and every one of them found something on the table to look at.

Harriet set a single sheet of paper in front of her. "That's a demand for a retraction of the statement you gave the Avalanche-Journal, Mrs. Navarro. Seventy-two hours. After that, it's a defamation suit. And your lawyer in Midland should know that patents issued before a marriage are separate property in Texas, no matter how many times she says otherwise."

Darlene looked at the certificate, and at the retraction, and at me.

"What do you want me to do?" she said.

"Go home and read something, Darlene. For once."

Gwen slid a new document across the table. Two pages. A reinstatement of the patent license, with my name on the Licensor line and the company's on the other.

I picked up the silver pen Jerome had brought, the one he'd meant for my surrender, and signed my name.

Then I picked up the phone in the middle of the table and dialed the shipping dock.

"Lupe," I said. "It's Floyd. Load the High Plains trucks. All six of them. And call my daughter and tell her to come back to work Monday. Her desk is still hers."
