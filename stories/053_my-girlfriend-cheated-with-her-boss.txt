"It's what ambitious people do, Eddie."

My girlfriend said it at her own promotion dinner, in the private room of the steakhouse on the top floor of the Silver Gulf Casino in Biloxi, with twelve of her coworkers around the table and a bottle of champagne that cost more than my first car going around in a silver bucket.

She said it with a little laugh. She said it like she was explaining a tax deduction to a child.

In her hand was the card I'd just slid across the white tablecloth to her. A thick cream-colored card with the Silver Gulf logo embossed at the top, the kind of stationery executives use, with four lines of handwriting in blue ink.

A woman at the end of the table giggled, then stopped. Somebody's fork hit a plate.

I'd been with Shannon Tullos for three years. Two of those years I'd spent fourteen days at a time on a drilling rig in the Gulf of Mexico, a hundred and thirty miles offshore, so she could have the house on Back Bay with the dock and the screened porch that I was still paying for.

I picked up my champagne glass.

"Congratulations on your success," I said, and raised it to her.

Then I reached across, took the card out of her fingers, folded it once, and put it in the inside pocket of my sport coat.

Six days later, Lamar Duncan, vice president of marketing for the Silver Gulf Casino Resort, would be walking across the employee parking lot with everything from his office in one cardboard box and two security guards walking on either side of him, and his wife would be watching him do it from her Mercedes with the window rolled down.

What Shannon didn't know was that there was a second piece of paper in the envelope I'd found that card in, folded behind it. And it had the casino's corporate credit card number on it.

My name is Eddie Nash. I'm fifty-one years old, and I've been a mechanic on offshore drilling rigs for twenty-three years, mostly pumps, compressors and the big diesel engines that keep the lights on out there. Out on a rig, you learn something the hard way, usually by watching somebody else learn it. You never trust a gauge. Gauges stick. Gauges lie. You trust the paper log, the one somebody signed with a pen at the end of a shift.

Hold on to that.

The room had gone very quiet. Twelve people trying to decide whether to laugh.

Shannon decided for them. She laughed again, bright and loud, and patted my hand on the tablecloth.

"Eddie's just not a corporate guy," she said to the table. "He doesn't get how it works. Do you, babe? He thinks everybody gets promoted by—"

"Doing their job," I said.

"—doing their job." She finished my sentence the way she always finished everybody's sentences, like she was helping. "It's cute. It's sweet. It's very Eddie."

A couple of people laughed with her then. Not many.

A young woman in a navy blazer across the table wasn't laughing at all. She was looking at me, and then down at her plate, and then at me again.

That's when the door of the private room opened, and a tall man in a gray suit walked in with a glass of bourbon in his hand.

"There's my director of marketing!" he said.

Everybody turned. Shannon lit up like a slot machine paying out.

Lamar Duncan was forty-nine, with silver at his temples and a tan that didn't come from working outside. He came around the table and put his hand on Shannon's shoulder and left it there, and then he looked down at me with a big friendly smile.

"And you must be Eddie. The oil man. Heard a lot about you." He stuck out his free hand. "Everything all right in here, partner? Looked a little serious when I came in."

I looked at the hand. I looked at the other hand, still resting on my girlfriend's shoulder.

"Everything's fine," I said. "We were just talking about ambition."

Here's how I found that card.

That Friday afternoon, I was three days into my fourteen days home, and I'd decided to surprise Shannon. Her promotion dinner was that night. She'd been named director of marketing on Tuesday, and she'd told me about it on the phone while I was still offshore, screaming so loud my crew boss could hear it through my headset. Director. Corner office. A company Tahoe. A forty-thousand-dollar raise.

So Friday, while she was at the salon getting her hair done, I took her Lexus to the car wash on Pass Road, and then I brought it home and detailed it myself in the driveway. Vacuumed it. Armor All on the dash. Cleaned out twenty-three Starbucks napkins from the console. Because that's what a man does when he's proud of his woman and doesn't have a lot of words for it.

In the driver's door pocket, under a stack of parking stubs, there was a cream-colored envelope with no stamp and her first name written on the front in blue ink.

I almost put it back unopened. I want that on the record. I almost put it back.

Then I saw the logo in the corner. Silver Gulf. And I thought, maybe it's a congratulations card from her boss, and I'll put it on the kitchen counter so she can see I found it.

I opened it.

The card said:

Shannon. For my rising star. The Westin was worth every penny. Director looks good on you. Wear the black one Friday. L.

Folded behind it was a hotel receipt. The Westin, Jackson, Mississippi. One king room, two nights, the weekend before last. The weekend she'd told me she was at a marketing conference in Jackson. Room service, two hundred and eighteen dollars. A bottle of Veuve Clicquot from the minibar.

And at the bottom, the folio was charged to a company American Express card ending in 7713, with a note typed in by somebody at the front desk: Silver Gulf Casino. Client development.

I sat down on the curb of my own driveway, with the vacuum hose still in one hand, and read it four times. I whistled the whole time, some tuneless thing I didn't even know I was doing, the way I do when I'm nervous. My neighbor across the street, Mrs. Bertha Saucier, came out to get her mail and asked if I was okay, and I said I was fine, I was just thinking about a pump.

Then I put the card and the receipt back in the envelope, and I put the envelope in my sport coat, and I wore the sport coat to dinner.

And she wore the black one.

After dinner, we drove home in her Lexus with the windows up. She didn't say anything until we were on Highway 90 with the Gulf on our left, black and flat under the moon.

"You embarrassed me," she said.

"I handed you a card."

"In front of my team. On my night." She kept her eyes straight ahead. "You couldn't wait two hours? You couldn't wait until we got home?"

"You could've waited until we got home to tell me it's what ambitious people do."

She didn't answer that.

We got home, and she went straight to the kitchen and poured herself a glass of wine, and I stood in the doorway with my hands in my pockets. I didn't sit down. I didn't want to get comfortable in a conversation like that.

"How long?" I asked.

"Does it matter?"

"It matters to me."

She took a long drink. "Since the spring. Since the Mardi Gras ball. He's not — Eddie, it's not like that. It's not some love affair. It's business."

"Business."

"Do you know how many women in that building got passed over for director? Do you know how many years I've been doing two people's jobs for one person's paycheck while Lamar's golf buddies got promoted?" Her voice got louder. "I had a chance. I took it. That's all. Men have been doing it forever. Nobody calls them names."

"I didn't call you a name."

"You were thinking it."

"I was thinking about the Westin," I said. "The weekend you told me you were at a conference in Jackson. I called you Saturday night from the rig. You said you were in your room, tired, reading."

"I was in my room."

"Whose room, Shannon? It was on his company card."

She set the glass down hard. A little wine jumped out onto the counter.

"You're gone half the year, Eddie! Half the year! You're out on that rig with a hundred men and a microwave, and I'm here alone in this house, and I'm supposed to just sit on the porch and wait for you like some — some fisherman's wife in an old movie?"

"I'm out on that rig," I said quietly, "paying for this house."

"I didn't ask you to."

"You picked it. You said you wanted a dock. You said you wanted to watch the sunsets on Back Bay. I'm out there so you can have the sunsets."

She looked at me for a second, and I watched her decide that she didn't have a good answer for that, and I watched her go around it instead, the way water goes around a rock.

"You're just a grease monkey, Eddie," she said. "You've always been a grease monkey. You don't understand my world. Lamar understands it. He understands what it takes to get somewhere."

"I understand it fine," I said. "He took you to a hotel and the company paid for it, and then he gave you a promotion. I'm a grease monkey, Shannon, but I can read a receipt."

Her face went very still.

"What do you want?" she said.

"I want you out of my house. Thirty days. That's more than fair. Take the Lexus. It's in your name."

"Thirty days?" She laughed, shrill. "Fine. You know what? Fine. In thirty days I'll be living in a condo on the beach that the company pays for, with a man who wears a suit to work."

"Then you won't need all thirty."

She picked up her phone off the counter and her purse off the chair. At the door to the hallway, she stopped and turned around.

"And Eddie? Don't you dare make trouble. Lamar told me what you'd do. He said men like you always try to make trouble. And he said if you do, he knows people. He's got a cousin at your company. In Houston. He said one phone call and you'll never work offshore in the Gulf again."

She went down the hall and shut the guest room door, and locked it.

I stood there in my own kitchen with the lights on and the wine on the counter.

I've been threatened by men with wrenches, out on a rig at three in the morning, over overtime and who left the dog house a mess. I've never been threatened by a man's cousin in Houston. I didn't know what that was worth.

But I knew one thing. The envelope was still in my sport coat. And a threat is just a gauge reading. The paper log is what counts.

Tommy Ladner has been my best friend since we were roughnecks together in 2003, sharing a bunk room on a jack-up rig off Port Fourchon. He's fifty-three, a crane operator now, with a beard like Santa Claus and a laugh like a truck with a bad muffler. On our weeks home, we fish off the Biloxi Lighthouse Pier at dawn and lie to each other about the size of what we catch.

Sunday morning I told him everything, sitting on two overturned buckets with our lines in the water.

When I finished, he reeled in, looked at his empty hook, and baited it again before he said anything.

"'It's what ambitious people do,'" he said finally. "She said that? Out loud? In front of people?"

"In front of twelve people."

"Lord have mercy." He cast his line way out. "You know what that is, Eddie? That's a woman who thinks she already won. You don't say a thing like that unless you think nobody can touch you."

"Lamar says he's got a cousin at Gulfstar. In Houston."

Tommy laughed so loud a pelican flapped off the railing.

"Gulfstar's got twelve thousand employees. Everybody's got a cousin at Gulfstar. My ex-wife's got a cousin at Gulfstar. You know what a cousin in Houston can do to a man with twenty-three years, no write-ups and a union card? He can send you a Christmas card." He shook his head. "That's a man bluffing with a pair of twos."

I watched my line for a while.

"So what do I do?" I said.

"What do you want to do?"

"I don't want to do anything. I want her out of my house, and I want to go back out on the rig, and I want to never hear the name Lamar Duncan again as long as I live."

"Uh huh." Tommy cracked open a Coke. "And what's he going to do?"

"What do you mean?"

"I mean you think this is over because you told her to pack. It ain't over. He's got her a big title on his say-so, on a company card. He threatened your job through her. Next week she's a director, the week after that she's telling everybody at that casino you're a jealous drunk who beat on her, and the week after that, he's doing the same thing to some other girl in some other hotel." He took a swig. "Men like that don't stop, Eddie. Somebody stops them."

"Who?"

"Who's he married to?"

I didn't know. I'd never thought about Lamar Duncan having a wife. I'd never thought about Lamar Duncan at all, until Friday.

Tommy took out his phone with his big fat thumbs and started typing.

"Lamar Duncan, Silver Gulf," he muttered. "Duncan... Duncan... here we go." He turned the screen to me. It was a picture from the Sun Herald society page, from some charity ball. Lamar in a tuxedo, smiling his big smile, with his arm around a slim dark-haired woman in a green gown who wasn't smiling at all.

"Vivian Varnado Duncan," Tommy read. "Executive director, Silver Gulf Foundation." He scrolled. "Daughter of Ellis Varnado. Founder and majority owner of the Silver Gulf Casino Resort."

He looked up at me with his eyebrows all the way up in his hairline.

"Eddie," he said. "He married the boss's daughter. And then he took your girl to a hotel on the boss's credit card."

"I'm not going to send a man's wife a love note," I said. "That's low. That's not who I am."

"It's not a love note. It's a receipt." Tommy pointed his Coke can at me. "You're always telling me. Trust the paper. Well, that's paper."

My phone buzzed in my shirt pocket.

It was a number I didn't recognize, with a 228 area code. I almost let it go. Then I answered.

"Mr. Nash? Eddie Nash?" A young woman's voice, quiet, nervous. "This is Ashley Cuevas. I work with Shannon. I was at the dinner Friday. Navy jacket?"

The girl across the table who hadn't laughed.

"I remember."

"I got your number off the RSVP list. Shannon had me do the invitations." She took a breath. "I'm really sorry to call you on a Sunday. I just — I couldn't stop thinking about what she said. About ambitious people. And I think you should know some things, and I don't think anybody else is going to tell you."

I looked at Tommy. He set down his Coke.

"Go ahead," I said, and put her on speaker, low.

"I was up for the director job too," Ashley said. "Me and Shannon and a guy named Marco Fields, from the Gulfport property. I have an MBA. I've been there six years. Shannon's been there four. The job was supposed to go to whoever brought in the biggest numbers on the spring campaign." She stopped. "I brought in the biggest numbers. It's in the quarterly report. Lamar told me in his office that it was 'a close call.' The announcement came out the Tuesday after the Jackson conference."

"Was there a conference in Jackson?"

"There was a regional gaming conference, yes. Friday only. One day. Most of us drove back that night." She paused. "Shannon and Lamar didn't come back until Sunday."

Tommy whistled low.

"And Mr. Nash," Ashley said, "this isn't the first time. Two years ago there was a girl named Dawn in events. Twenty-six, really pretty. She got a promotion she wasn't ready for, and she rode with Lamar to a conference in New Orleans, and six months later she left the company crying, and we all heard she got paid to sign something and keep quiet. Nobody ever said anything out loud. Because of who his wife is."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're the first person I ever saw who knew and didn't laugh," she said. "And because Shannon called me into her new office yesterday and told me if I wanted to keep my job, I'd better be 'a team player' now. She did the air quotes and everything. And I thought about my mother, who cleaned rooms at the Beau Rivage for thirty years, and I thought, no."

After she hung up, Tommy and I sat on our buckets for a long time and didn't catch anything.

"Well," Tommy said finally. "There's your log book."

Monday morning, the third day, I sat at my kitchen table at six o'clock with a cup of coffee and a pad of yellow paper, and I tried to write a letter to Vivian Duncan.

I'm not a letter writer. I've written about four letters in my whole life, and three of them were to my mother from basic training. I tore up five tries. Every one was too long, too angry, too much about me.

In the end I wrote one line.

I think you should see this.

I didn't sign my whole name. I signed it Eddie Nash, Biloxi, with my cell phone number under it, because a man who sends a thing like that without his name is a coward, and I've never been one of those either.

I made copies of the card and the receipt at the UPS Store on Pass Road, front and back. I put the copies in a FedEx envelope with the note. I kept the originals in a gallon Ziploc bag in my gun safe in the bedroom closet, next to my daddy's shotgun and my birth certificate.

The Silver Gulf Foundation had an office on the second floor of a bank building on Howard Avenue in downtown Biloxi. It was on their website, with a picture of Vivian Duncan handing a big cardboard check to a children's hospital.

I paid twenty-six dollars to have it delivered by ten the next morning. Signature required.

When I got back to the house, Shannon's Lexus was in the driveway. She'd come home from work early.

She was standing in the kitchen, holding my truck keys.

"I needed to move your truck," she said. "You blocked me in. Again."

"Sorry. Forgot."

"Uh huh." She held up a crumpled piece of paper between two fingers. "What's this?"

It was the FedEx receipt. I'd left it on the passenger seat.

The back of my neck went cold. I didn't look at it. I kept my face where it was.

"FedEx," I said.

"I can see that. Who'd you send something to on Howard Avenue overnight for twenty-six dollars?"

"Rig company. Gulfstar's got a parts office downtown. I had to send back a pressure sensor. I've been carrying it around in the truck for a week."

She looked at me for a long time, the way she'd looked at me across the steakhouse table. She was trying to decide if I was smart enough to lie to her.

I whistled. I didn't mean to. That old tuneless whistle came out of me, and I stopped it as soon as I heard it.

Shannon crumpled up the receipt and tossed it in the kitchen trash.

"You're a terrible liar, Eddie," she said. "You know that? You've always been a terrible liar. It's the only thing I ever really liked about you."

She went down the hall to the guest room and locked the door.

I fished the receipt out of the trash and put it in my wallet.

Tuesday morning, at seven o'clock, before the FedEx truck had even left the depot, my phone rang. 228 area code again. A different number.

"Mr. Nash?" A woman's voice, low, very calm, very proper. Old Biloxi money. "This is Vivian Duncan."

I set my coffee down.

"Ma'am, the package isn't supposed to get there till ten."

"My assistant tracks every package addressed to me. She saw your name on the shipping notice at six this morning and looked you up. Then she called me." A small pause. "I'd like to see what you sent me before my husband does, Mr. Nash. Can you meet me at the Bayou Coffee House in Ocean Springs at eight-thirty?"

The Bayou Coffee House is a little place on Government Street in Ocean Springs, across the bay from Biloxi, with old wood floors and local art on the walls and a ceiling fan that wobbles. It was mostly empty at eight-thirty on a Tuesday. A couple of retirees doing a crossword. A college girl with a laptop.

Vivian Duncan was sitting at a table in the back with a cup of black coffee she hadn't touched. She was about forty-five, slim, with her dark hair pulled back tight and pearl earrings and a navy dress. She looked like the photo on the society page, except up close her eyes were red at the edges.

I sat down across from her.

"Mr. Nash," she said. "Thank you for coming."

"Yes, ma'am."

"My assistant intercepted the package at the FedEx depot in Gulfport at seven-fifteen. I've read it." She folded her hands on the table. "I'd like to see the originals."

I took the Ziploc bag out of my jacket pocket and slid it across the table.

She didn't open it. She looked at it through the plastic. The cream card with her father's company logo on the top. The Westin receipt, with the Amex number. She looked at the handwriting for a long time.

"That's my husband's handwriting," she said. "That's his 'L.' He makes it like a fishhook. He's done it since college." She touched the plastic with one finger, right over the receipt. "And that's my father's corporate card."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Do you know what 'client development' means, Mr. Nash?"

"I figure it means the company paid for it."

"It means my husband charged his affair to my father's business and called it work." Her voice was very steady, but her hand on the table wasn't. "Do you know how long I've known he was cheating?"

"No, ma'am."

"About a year. Since the Mardi Gras ball. I saw how he looked at her, and how she looked at me." She took a breath. "I hired an investigator in June. He got photographs of my husband's car outside a condo in D'Iberville. But photographs aren't proof of anything to my father. My father thinks Lamar walks on water. Lamar went to Ole Miss with my brother. My father loves him like a son." She tapped the bag. "My father doesn't care about my feelings, Mr. Nash. But he cares very much about his money. And about the company's code of conduct, which he wrote himself, in 1994, and which says, in Section Eight, that no supervisor may have a romantic relationship with a direct report. And in Section Eleven, that no employee may use a company card for personal expenses."

"So it's paper," I said.

She looked at me oddly.

"I'm a rig mechanic, ma'am. Gauges lie. You trust the paper log."

For the first time since I sat down, Vivian Duncan almost smiled.

"Yes," she said. "It's paper." She picked up the Ziploc bag and put it in her purse. "I'll return these to you when my father's lawyers have made copies. Today I'm going to my father's office with these and a code of conduct. And I'm going to ask the head of human resources to pull every expense report my husband has filed in five years."

"Ma'am, there's one more thing you ought to know. There was a girl named Dawn. Two years ago. In events."

Her face went still.

"How do you know about Dawn?"

"Somebody at that company who's tired of keeping quiet told me."

Vivian looked at me for a long moment. Then she nodded once, like she was writing it down somewhere inside her head.

She stood up and put a ten-dollar bill under her coffee cup.

"Mr. Nash," she said. "One more thing. Don't be at home tomorrow evening."

"Why not?"

"Because by tomorrow afternoon, my husband is going to know exactly where that envelope came from," she said. "And Lamar has never in his life been told no by a man who works with his hands."

I was at home Wednesday evening anyway. It was my house. I wasn't going to hide from a man in a gray suit in my own house.

I was out on the screened porch at a little after six, with a glass of iced tea and the Braves game on the radio, watching the light go orange over Back Bay, when a black BMW came down the street too fast and stopped crooked in my driveway, behind Shannon's Lexus.

Lamar Duncan got out. No jacket this time. White shirt with the sleeves rolled up, tie pulled loose, face the color of a boiled shrimp.

He came up the porch steps two at a time and yanked open the screen door.

"You," he said.

"Evening, Lamar."

"Don't you 'evening' me. Do you know what you've done?"

"I mailed some paper."

"You mailed some paper." He laughed, but it wasn't a laugh. "You mailed some paper to my wife. Who took it to her father. Who called me into his office at noon today with two lawyers and the head of HR, and put a copy of my own handwriting on the table in front of me, and asked me to explain 'client development.'" He pointed at me. "Twenty-two years I've given that family. I married into it. I've been the face of that casino since before you knew what a slot machine was."

"I've known what a slot machine was since I was nineteen," I said. "I lost a whole paycheck in one in 1993. Learned my lesson."

"You think this is funny?"

"No, sir. I think it's paper."

He stared at me. Then he took a step closer, close enough I could smell the bourbon.

"My daddy used to say there's two kinds of men in this world," he said. "Men who build things, and men who tear them down because they can't build anything themselves. You know which one you are, oil man?"

"I rebuild diesel engines a hundred and thirty miles out in the Gulf of Mexico in twelve-foot seas," I said. "I build plenty."

"You're a jealous little man who couldn't keep his woman, and you tore down a career because of it."

"Whose career?"

"Mine! And hers! Do you have any idea what you've done to Shannon? She worked her whole life for that title."

"She worked two nights at the Westin for that title," I said. "On your father-in-law's card. You didn't give her a promotion, Lamar. You gave her a receipt."

That one landed. I saw it land. His face went from shrimp to something darker.

Across the street, Mrs. Bertha Saucier had come out onto her own porch with a dish towel over her shoulder, pretending to water a fern that was already dripping.

"You think you're smart," Lamar said, low. "I'll make one call. My cousin—"

"At Gulfstar," I said. "In Houston. I heard. Go ahead and call him. I've got twenty-three years with no write-ups, a union card, and a supervisor who thinks I hung the moon because I kept a blowout preventer running through Hurricane Ida. Tell your cousin I said hey."

Lamar opened his mouth, and closed it.

"Here's what I know about paper, Lamar," I said. "Your wife has the original. Not a copy. The original, with your fishhook L on it, sitting in her purse or her daddy's safe. Every copy you can tear up, I'll make another. But that one's not mine anymore. It's hers. And it's her daddy's company."

He stood there breathing hard.

Then the screen door behind me creaked, and Shannon came out onto the porch in her work clothes, barefoot, with her phone in her hand.

"Lamar?" she said. "What are you doing here? What's going on? You haven't answered my texts all afternoon."

He looked at her. He didn't answer her. He looked at her like she was a piece of furniture somebody had left on the curb.

Then he turned around and went down my porch steps, and got in the BMW, and backed out of my driveway so fast he took out the corner of my mailbox post.

Shannon stood on the porch with her mouth open.

"Eddie," she said. "What did you do?"

"I told you Friday. I read a receipt."

"What did you do?"

"I sent the card and the receipt to his wife," I said. "Monday. That FedEx wasn't a pressure sensor."

Her mouth went tight and small. She grabbed the porch rail with one hand.

"You sent it to Vivian Varnado?"

"I sent it to Vivian Duncan. She took it to her father."

"Oh my God." She put her hand over her mouth. "Oh my God, Eddie. Oh my God. Do you know who her father is? Do you know what you've done to me?"

"I know what you did to me. I'm just the one who mailed it."

"It was business!" She was screaming now, there on the porch, with Mrs. Saucier across the street holding her dripping fern. "It was business, Eddie! Do you know how hard I worked? Do you know what it took?"

"Two nights at the Westin," I said. "It's on the receipt."

"You jealous, small, pathetic—" She stopped herself. "You did this because you couldn't stand me being more than you."

"I did it because you told twelve people it's what ambitious people do," I said. "And I figured if it was something to be proud of, his wife ought to know about it too."

She stared at me.

Then she did the thing I'd seen her do a hundred times in three years, when she'd lost an argument and knew it. She went around the rock.

"You want to know something?" she said. "I never loved you. Not really. I loved the dock. I loved the sunsets. I loved that you were gone fourteen days at a time and the bills got paid. That's all you were, Eddie. A paycheck that came home every two weeks and slept on the couch."

I thought about that for a second. I'll be honest. It hurt more than anything else she'd said.

"Then you got your money's worth," I said. "Now go pack."

She went in the house and slammed the door so hard the wreath fell off it.

Across the street, Mrs. Saucier set down her fern.

"Edward," she called over. "You want some gumbo? I made too much."

"Yes, ma'am," I said. "I believe I do."

I was halfway through the bowl, sitting on Mrs. Saucier's porch steps, when Tommy called.

"So I asked around," he said. "About the cousin at Gulfstar."

"And?"

"And his name's Dwight Duncan, and he works in Houston, all right." Tommy was already laughing. "He's a receptionist, Eddie. In the parking garage office. He validates tickets. If Lamar Duncan calls him, the worst thing he can do to you is make you pay eight dollars to park."

I laughed so hard I had to set the gumbo down on the step. Mrs. Saucier came to the screen door to see what was so funny, and I couldn't even tell her. I just waved my spoon.

"A pair of twos," Tommy said. "I told you."

Thursday morning, a little after nine, I was at the Home Depot on Cedar Lake Road buying a new mailbox post when my phone rang.

It was Shannon.

I let it ring three times. Then I answered.

"Eddie." She was crying. Not the show crying from the porch. Real crying, the ugly kind, hiccuping. "Eddie, I need you to come get me."

"Where are you?"

"The employee lot. At work. They — they took the Tahoe keys. HR called me in at eight. They said there's an investigation into the — into the selection process for the director position. They said the promotion is 'rescinded pending review.'" She choked. "Rescinded, Eddie. Like a bad check. They walked me back down to my old desk and gave me a box for my office things. And then they said the Tahoe's company property and I'm not authorized to drive it anymore, and my Lexus is at the house, and I don't have a way home."

"Call your sister."

"Darcy's in Mobile. She's at work. I don't have anybody else, Eddie. I don't have anybody." Her voice cracked. "Please."

I stood there in the garden center at Home Depot with a four-by-four post under my arm.

"I'm twenty minutes out," I said.

The employee lot at the Silver Gulf is around the back of the building, past the loading docks, a big slab of hot asphalt with a guard shack and the bay glittering past the chain-link fence. When I pulled in, I saw Shannon right away, standing by the guard shack in her work clothes with a cardboard box at her feet.

And I saw the rest of it.

Over by the executive parking spaces near the back door, there was a white Mercedes with the engine running and the driver's window down. Vivian Duncan was sitting behind the wheel in sunglasses, very still, watching the back door of the building.

On the loading dock steps, an old man in a seersucker suit and a straw hat stood with his hands folded on top of a cane. White hair, big white eyebrows, a face like a hickory nut. Ellis Varnado. I knew him from the billboards on Interstate 10.

And coming out the back door of the casino, carrying a cardboard box with a framed photo and a desk lamp sticking out of the top, was Lamar Duncan. A uniformed security guard walked on his left. Another walked on his right.

I parked my truck and got out and stood by the hood.

Lamar walked across the asphalt toward his BMW. He didn't look at the old man on the steps. He didn't look at the Mercedes. Halfway across the lot, he saw Shannon by the guard shack. And then he saw me.

He stopped.

"You," he said, across thirty yards of asphalt.

"Keep walking, Mr. Duncan," one of the guards said.

"Do you know what you did?" Lamar shouted at me. "Twenty-two years! Twenty-two years and I'm terminated for cause! For cause! No severance! Because of some greaseball with a FedEx account!"

"Keep walking, sir."

Then Lamar turned to Shannon, and his face changed, and he pointed at her with his free hand, the box sliding against his hip.

"And you," he said. "You stupid — you left it in your car? You kept it? I told you to throw it away! I told you at the Westin, throw it away!"

Shannon flinched like he'd slapped her.

"You said you wanted me to have something to remember it by," she said. "You said I was your rising star."

"Rising star," Lamar said, and laughed, an ugly sound. "You were a weekend. You were a weekend I put on the company card."

Over on the loading dock steps, old Ellis Varnado tapped his cane once on the concrete.

"Lamar," the old man said. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't have to. Every head in that parking lot turned. "Put that box in your car and get off my property. My daughter will be in touch about the house."

Lamar stood there for a second. Then he put the box in the BMW and got in and drove out past the guard shack, and the guard had to wave the arm up fast so he didn't hit it.

The white Mercedes rolled its window up, slowly. Vivian Duncan didn't look at me. But just before the glass closed, she lifted two fingers off the steering wheel, the way men in pickup trucks wave to each other on a country road.

Then she drove away too.

The back door of the casino opened one more time, and Ashley Cuevas came down the loading dock steps in her navy blazer with a cup of coffee in each hand. She walked straight across the lot to me and held one out.

"I figured you'd be here," she said. "I saw your truck from the third floor."

"Thank you." I took the coffee. "You all right?"

"They called me up to Mr. Varnado's office at eight-thirty. Him and two lawyers and the HR director." She glanced at Shannon by the guard shack, then back at me, and lowered her voice. "They asked me about the spring campaign numbers. I brought the quarterly report. They asked me about Dawn. I told them what I told you." She swallowed. "Then they asked me if I'd serve as acting director of marketing while they review the selection. And their lawyers are calling Dawn this afternoon. To ask her what she signed."

"Congratulations," I said. "I mean that one."

"I didn't get it in a hotel," Ashley said. "I want that on the record."

"It's on the record," I said.

She went back inside. She didn't look at Shannon at all.

Shannon stood by the guard shack with her box. Inside it, I could see her nameplate, Shannon Tullos, Marketing Manager, the old one, and a little plant, and a framed photo of the two of us on the dock at sunset that I'd forgotten she even had at work.

She looked at me with her mascara running down to her chin.

"Eddie," she said. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. Please. Can we just go home? We can fix this. I'll find another job. I'll make it up to you. I'll be good. I swear to God, I'll be good. Just take me home."

I picked up her box and put it in the bed of my truck. Then I opened the passenger door for her.

"I'll take you to your sister's in Mobile," I said. "Your Lexus will be in her driveway by Saturday. Tommy and I will bring your things."

She didn't get in. She stood there holding onto the door.

"That's it?" she said. "Three years, and that's it? That's all you've got to say to me?"

I looked at her for a long moment, standing there in that parking lot with the bay shining behind her, the rescinded director of marketing.

"Congratulations on your success, Shannon," I said.
