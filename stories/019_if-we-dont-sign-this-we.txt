"If we don't sign this, we won't get married."

My fiancée said it in a lawyer's office in Sioux City, Iowa, in May of 2001, with her mother, her lawyer and a notary sitting around the table watching me. She slid a stack of paper across to me with a pen on top, and she didn't smile.

The paper was a prenuptial agreement. Eleven pages. It protected her flower shop, the two hundred forty acres of farmland her grandfather had left her in Plymouth County, and, in clause seven, something else that made the notary raise her eyebrows.

I was thirty-four years old and I had eleven thousand dollars in a savings account and a 1994 Chevy pickup with a cracked windshield. There wasn't much of mine to protect.

I picked up the pen.

"Read it first, Milton," her mother said from the corner. Vera Lowell was holding a camcorder up to her eye, the big kind with the shoulder strap, because she filmed everything back then. Christmas, birthdays, the time the basement flooded. "Delia wants it on tape that you read it."

"I read it," I said. "Last night. Twice."

"Clause seven," Delia said. "Read clause seven out loud. For the tape."

So I did. I read it out loud, in a lawyer's office, to a camcorder.

"All money, prizes, winnings, awards and windfalls received by either party during the marriage, from any lottery, contest, game of chance or wager, shall remain the sole and separate property of the party in whose name they are received, regardless of the source of funds used to acquire the ticket or entry."

"Why's that in there?" I asked.

Delia tossed her hair. She was thirty-one and beautiful and she knew it.

"Because I buy scratch tickets every Friday," she said, "and if I ever hit, Milton Sparks, that money is mine. Every dollar I make, every dollar I inherit and every dollar I ever win stays mine."

Her mother laughed behind the camcorder. Her lawyer, an old man named Walt Easley, cleared his throat and looked at the ceiling.

I signed. Delia signed. The notary stamped it.

Twenty-four years later, my wife would be sitting at her own mother's eightieth birthday dinner, reading clause seven out loud, and her voice would crack on the word lottery.

What she didn't know was that the VHS tape her mother made that afternoon had been sitting in a shoebox on the top shelf of my closet for twenty-four years, right next to my high school yearbook, because Vera gave it to me as a wedding present.

My name is Milt Sparks. I'm fifty-eight now. I've managed the Missouri Valley Grain elevator on the north side of Sioux City for nineteen years. I spend my days weighing other people's corn and telling farmers what it's worth, and the thing that job taught me is simple: a scale doesn't care how a man feels about his load. It only tells you what's actually in the truck. Hold on to that.

On a Monday morning in March, I stopped at the Casey's on Hamilton Boulevard for coffee and a breakfast pizza, the same as every Monday. I handed the girl at the register two tickets from Saturday's Lotto America drawing that had been riding in my shirt pocket all weekend. I buy two every week. Five dollars. It's the only bad habit Delia ever let me keep, mostly because she has the same one.

The girl scanned the first ticket. Nothing.

She scanned the second. The machine made a sound I'd never heard it make before, a long rising beep, and the screen on her side lit up with a message she read twice.

"Sir," she said. "Um. Sir. It says to contact Lottery headquarters."

"How much?"

"It doesn't say." She was a little pale. "It says the prize is too big for the store to pay."

"Is that bad?" I asked.

"I don't think so." She looked around, then leaned over the counter and whispered, "My manager says when it does that, you should probably not tell anybody in the store. Just go."

So I took the ticket back, said thank you, left my breakfast pizza sitting on the counter, and went. I drove to the elevator, sat in my truck in the parking lot with the heater running, and pulled up the Iowa Lottery website on my phone with my thumbs shaking.

Saturday's Lotto America jackpot. One winning ticket, sold in Sioux City. Fifteen million dollars.

I checked the numbers four times. Then I checked them a fifth time, out loud, one at a time, like I was reading a scale ticket to a farmer who didn't trust me.

They matched.

I'm not a man who cries. I cried a little in that truck. Mostly I laughed. I laughed so hard my foreman, Gus Ortega, knocked on my window to ask if I was having a stroke.

"Gus," I said, "I'm taking the day off."

"You haven't taken a day off since 2016."

"Then I've got a few saved up."

I drove home to tell my wife.

We live in a split-level on the north side of Morningside, with a big sunroom off the back that Delia put in for her plants. Her van from the flower shop was in the driveway, which it never is on a Monday morning. I came in through the garage, quiet, because I wanted to see her face when I told her.

I heard her before I saw her. She was in the sunroom on the phone, with her back to the kitchen door, watering a fern.

"Rudy, stop. I told you April," she was saying. "The fifteenth. I'll file right after taxes so I can claim the shop loss one more year."

I stopped in the kitchen with the ticket in my hand.

"No, he won't fight it. He won't even understand it. He'll read that prenup and see he gets his truck and his little 401k from the elevator and that's it. The shop's mine. The farm's mine. My mother made sure of that." She laughed, low and warm, the way she hadn't laughed at me in maybe five years. "Then you and me and the lake place. I told you. Patience. Twenty-four years I've been patient with a man who weighs corn."

I put the ticket back in my shirt pocket.

Rudy Simmons. Her high school boyfriend. Sold crop insurance out of an office in Le Mars. I'd bought a policy from him myself in 2019, on the north eighty of her farm.

"I've got to go," she said. "Milt's at work. Love you. Bye."

She turned around with the watering can and saw me standing in the kitchen doorway.

The watering can tipped. Water ran all over the tile.

"Milt," she said. "You scared me half to death. What are you doing home?"

I looked at my wife of twenty-four years, with a fifteen-million-dollar ticket in my pocket and her boyfriend's phone call still warm in her hand.

"Forgot my reading glasses," I said.

Ruthanne Cowley has been practicing law in Sioux City for thirty-five years, in an office over a hearing-aid store on Fourth Street. She did my late father's will and my son's speeding ticket. I sat across from her desk Tuesday morning and put two things on it: the lottery ticket, sealed in a sandwich bag, and a copy of my prenup.

She looked at the ticket for a long time. Then she looked at me over her glasses.

"Milt," she said. "Have you signed the back of this?"

"Not yet."

"Good. Don't. Not until we talk about how you claim it." She slid it back to me. "Now tell me why you look like a man who just found out his dog died instead of a man who just won fifteen million dollars."

I told her about the sunroom. About Rudy, April fifteenth, the lake place, the man who weighs corn.

Ruthanne didn't say anything for a while. She picked up the prenup and started reading, and about halfway down page four, she started to laugh. Not a big laugh. A dry little chuckle, like somebody reading a joke in a church bulletin.

"Who wrote clause seven?" she said.

"She did. Well, her lawyer did. Walt Easley. But she told him what to put in it. She dictated it, pretty much. She plays scratch tickets."

Ruthanne read it out loud, slowly, every word.

"Regardless of the source of funds used to acquire the ticket." She set the paper down. "Milt, in Iowa, without this, a lottery prize won during the marriage would be marital property. A judge would split it down the middle, more or less, no matter whose name is on the ticket. With this?" She tapped the clause. "With this, it's yours. All of it. She wrote a fence around her own winnings, and she built it so tight it fences in yours, too."

"She'll say I bought the ticket with our joint debit card."

"I'm sure you did. Clause seven says that doesn't matter. She made sure of it."

"She'll say she signed it under pressure. Two days before the wedding."

"She'll try." Ruthanne smiled. "Who was pressuring who, Milt? Whose office was it in? Whose lawyer?"

"Hers. Her lawyer, her mother, her office."

"Then she'll have a hard time crying duress." Ruthanne leaned back. "Here's how this goes. Iowa is a no-fault state. Her boyfriend won't change the split of anything. What changes it is this paper. So you keep your mouth shut. You claim the prize the right way. You don't hide it, because hiding it is the one thing that could hurt you. You just don't announce it at the supper table, either."

"She'll find out."

"The Lottery posts winners' names. She'll find out in about ten days." Ruthanne folded her hands. "I'd like her to find out after she files. After she's sworn to a judge, in writing, that the prenup is valid and she wants it enforced. Because she will. She'll want it enforced to keep her shop and her farm."

I thought about that. Then I reached into my jacket and set one more thing on her desk. A VHS tape in a cardboard sleeve, with Vera's handwriting on the label in black marker. Delia and Milt, Prenup, 5/14/01.

Ruthanne picked it up like it was a holy relic.

"Is this what I think it is?"

"Her mother filmed the whole signing. Delia had me read clause seven out loud for the tape. Then she explained why it was in there. In her own words."

Ruthanne held the tape up to the window light.

"Milt," she said. "Get this digitized today. Two copies. One in my safe. One somewhere she's never been."

"Where's somewhere she's never been?"

"The grain elevator," Ruthanne said. "Has your wife ever once in twenty-four years been inside the elevator office?"

I thought about it.

"No," I said. "She says it smells like dust."

"Then that's where it goes."

Delia filed on April fifteenth, like she'd told Rudy. She didn't tell me first. She had a process server come to the elevator at ten in the morning, in the middle of the corn rush, with nine trucks lined up on the scale.

He was a skinny kid in a Hawkeyes jacket. He walked right into the scale house where I was standing with Gus and two farmers, and said, "Milton Sparks?"

"That's me."

"You've been served." He handed me an envelope and walked out fast, like he expected me to throw something.

The two farmers looked at each other. Gus looked at the envelope.

"Milt," he said. "Is that what I think it is?"

I opened it. Petition for Dissolution of Marriage, Woodbury County. Delia Ann Sparks, Petitioner. It said the marriage had broken down irretrievably. It said there was a valid premarital agreement dated May 14, 2001, and that Petitioner asked the court to "enforce said agreement in its entirety," awarding her Delia's Flowers, the Plymouth County farmland and the lake lot at Okoboji that she'd bought in 2016 in her own name.

She'd signed it at the bottom. Under oath.

"Enforce said agreement in its entirety," I read out loud.

"What's that mean?" one of the farmers asked.

"It means she likes her prenup," I said.

"I'm sorry you had to find out like this. It's been over for a long time. You'll be fine, you'll keep your truck and your 401k. Please don't make this ugly."

I typed back one word.

"Okay."

Gus was still staring at me.

"You're taking this pretty good," he said.

"Gus," I said, "weigh that next truck. He's been waiting twenty minutes."

On April twenty-third the Iowa Lottery put out a press release. They'd asked me if I wanted a photo with one of those giant cardboard checks, and I'd said no thank you, but by law they still had to say who'd won. So on the Lottery's website, under Big Winners, there was a little paragraph and a stock photo of the Sioux City skyline.

Milton Sparks of Sioux City claims fifteen-million-dollar Lotto America jackpot. Sparks, who manages a local grain elevator, said he plans to "keep going to work for now."

By noon it was on the Sioux City Journal website. By two, it was on Facebook in three different community groups. By three-thirty, my phone had twenty-nine missed calls from Delia, eleven from her mother and four from a number in Le Mars I'd never saved but knew anyway.

"I'm sure I will."

"It's true, Vera."

Two weeks later I came home from the elevator on a Thursday and found my closet on the bed.

Not some of it. All of it. My shirts were on the comforter in a heap, still on their hangers. My boots were on the floor. Delia was sitting in the reading chair by the window with her arms crossed. She'd moved out to her mother's three weeks before, but she still had a key, and she'd clearly used it.

"Where is it?" she said.

"Where's what?"

"Don't do that. Don't do the dumb farmer thing with me, Milton. The tape. Mama's tape. It was in that box for twenty-four years. I've seen it every time I put your sweaters away."

Vera Lowell turned eighty on the first Sunday in June. The family had booked the back room at the supper club on Hamilton Boulevard, the one with the dark paneling and the relish trays, where they've had every Lowell birthday since 1985. Vera called me herself to invite me.

"No ma'am. Harvest is in four months. Somebody's got to weigh the corn."

"Delia always said you'd never leave that place."

"Delia was right about that one."

At the end of the table, Delia was talking to nobody, folding and unfolding her napkin into smaller and smaller squares.

It went fine through the relish tray and the salad. Then, between the salad and the prime rib, Delia stood up and tapped her water glass with a spoon.

"I just want to say something," she said. "To Mama, happy birthday. And to everybody, because I know you've all heard things."

She looked down the table at me.

"Milt and I have had a hard few months. But I believe in fairness. And I believe Milt does too, deep down. So I've had my lawyer draw up a settlement. Something fair. Three million dollars. Not half, just a fair share for twenty-four years of marriage." She pulled a folder out of her purse and set it on the table. "I'd like him to sign it tonight, here, in front of my family. As a gift to my mother. So we can all be at peace."

Twenty-two faces turned to me. Somebody's fork hit a plate.

I didn't take the folder. I reached into my sport coat and took out another one, an old manila folder, soft and yellow at the edges, and laid it on the white tablecloth in front of my mother-in-law.

"Vera," I said. "You asked me to bring it. I brought the paper instead. The tape's at the elevator."

Vera put on her reading glasses. She opened the folder and turned to page four. She read for a while, her lips moving. Then she looked up the long table at her daughter.

"Delia," she said. "Read clause seven out loud."

"Mama, that's not—"

"Read it, honey. For the family."

Delia took the page. Her hands weren't steady. She read it the way a girl reads in front of a class she knows is going to laugh at her.

"All money, prizes, winnings, awards and windfalls received by either party during the marriage, from any lottery, contest, game of chance or wager, shall remain the sole and separate property of the party in whose name they are received…" She stopped. "Regardless of the source of funds."

"Who wrote that, honey?"

"Walt wrote it."

"Who told Walt what to write?"

Delia didn't answer.

"You did," Vera said. "I was there. I held the camera. You practiced it the night before in my bathroom mirror. And when it was signed, you bought everybody steaks." Vera took off her glasses. "I'm eighty years old today, Delia. I've buried a husband and a sister. I'm not going to sit at my own birthday and watch you ask a man to pay you three million dollars for the privilege of being cheated on."

"Mama!"

"Rudy Simmons," Vera said, to the whole table, "came to my house on Tuesday to ask if I'd co-sign a loan for his boat. I told him to get out."

Somebody at the far end, one of the Sioux Center cousins, laughed out loud and then covered her mouth with her napkin.

Delia turned to me. Her eyes were full.

"Milt," she said. "Please. Twenty-four years. I made a mistake. If we don't fix this, we lose everything we built."

I folded up the old prenup and put it back in its yellow folder. Then I slid her new folder, the one with the three-million-dollar settlement, back down the tablecloth to her, unsigned.

"Twenty-four years ago, you told me if we didn't sign this, we wouldn't get married," I said. "I signed it. Now you're asking me not to honor it. Then we never were."

I stood up, kissed my mother-in-law on the cheek, and handed her the little wrapped box from my pocket. Inside was a pair of pearl earrings and two Lotto America tickets for next Saturday's drawing.

"Happy birthday, Vera," I said. "If you hit, it's all yours. Clause seven."
