"This is your fault," he said, and came at me.

I'll say this for Logan Pierce. He was younger and he was taller. But he'd been drinking since happy hour, and I've spent thirty years in boiler rooms wrestling 200-pound pumps up ladders.

He got me with the first one. A big, wide, sloppy right that caught me on the cheekbone and rang my ears and spun my head. Kenzie screamed. Ozzie yelled something in Spanish that would have gotten him suspended from any school in the district.

I kept my feet.

He swung again, the same right, because men like Logan only have one punch. This time I saw it coming. I stepped inside it, caught his arm, turned with it, and put him down on the plank floor on his belly with his arm bent up behind him and my knee on his back. His cowboy hat rolled under the pool table.

"Get off me! Get off me, man!"

"You hit me first," I said. "In front of about sixty people. Stay down."

"My arm!"

"Then stop pulling on it."

The bouncer got there in about four seconds, a big kid in a black T-shirt who looked down at the two of us and said, "Sir, I saw the whole thing. You can let him up. I got him."

I let go and stood up and stepped back with my hands open. My cheek was throbbing and I could feel my left eye starting to swell shut.

"Cops are on the way," the bartender called. "He threw the first punch. I've got it on camera, and so do half these girls."

He was right. At least a dozen phones were up in the air, pointed at Logan on the floor.

Logan sat up, holding his shoulder. He looked at Tori.

"Baby. Baby, I'm sorry. I'll fix it. Come on. Let's go home."

Tori looked at him for a long time. Then she reached up and took off the little silver necklace she was wearing, a heart with an L on it, and dropped it in his lap.

"Home's my mom's in Kearney," she said. "You can mail me the crib."

She turned around and walked out to the Buick with the engine running. The woman in scrubs got out and helped her into the passenger side and slammed the door so hard the balloons over the bachelorette table swayed.

Two Lincoln police officers walked in about three minutes later. The bartender showed them the video on the bar's camera. A young officer with a buzz cut watched it twice, then looked at Logan and said, "Mr. Pierce, stand up and turn around for me."

"He's the one who put me on the floor!"

"After you hit him twice, sir. Turn around."

They cuffed him right there under the BRIDE balloons. He was still yelling as they walked him out the side door, something about lawyers and his shoulder and how none of this was fair.

The other officer took my statement and my phone number and looked at my eye with a little flashlight.

"You want to press charges?" she asked.

"Yes, ma'am."

"You want to get that looked at?"

"I've had worse from a steam valve."

She almost smiled.

Kenzie came over and looked at my face and winced. "Dad. Your eye looks like an eggplant."

"It'll go down."

"You took a punch from a guy in a cowboy hat at my bridesmaid party."

"Technically it's not your bridesmaid party anymore."

"Technically," Ozzie said, putting the cowboy hat on his own head, "this is the best bachelorette party in the history of Lincoln, Nebraska. I'm going to tell my grandkids about it. I'm going to tell the school board about it."

"Don't tell the school board about it, Oz."

"I'm going to tell the school board a little bit about it."


When I turned around, Brandi was standing a few feet away. The tiara was gone. The sash that said LAST NIGHT OF FREEDOM was crooked across her chest. Her friends had backed off and left her standing there alone, the way people do when they're not sure whose side they're on anymore.

"Rod," she said.

"Brandi."

"I didn't know. About her. About the baby. I swear to God I didn't know."

"I believe you."

"He told me he loved me. He told me he never stopped. He said I was making a mistake marrying you, that you were a nice guy but nice guys are boring and I'd be bored out of my mind in five years." She wiped her eyes with the back of her hand. "I just wanted to know. Before I married you. I just wanted to know if I was making a mistake."

"Now you know."

"Can we just..." She reached out and touched my sleeve, the same way she used to touch it in the truck when she wanted me to turn the radio up. "Can we just start over? Please. Call the venue Monday. Yvette said she'd hold the date if I called. It's still two weeks away. Nobody has to know. We can just pretend this week never happened."

Behind her, nineteen women in pink sashes held their breath. Kenzie had her hand over her mouth. Ozzie was holding Logan's cowboy hat, which he'd fished out from under the pool table, for reasons I never figured out.

I thought about the text. "Wedding's still on, but." Everything that mattered was in the "but." It always is.

"Nobody has to know?" I said. "Brandi, you told twelve people on Tuesday. Twenty more tonight. There's a pregnant girl in a Buick on her way back to Kearney who knows. The police know. Your father knows. He asked you Thursday night, and you told him."

"Then what am I supposed to do?"

I took her hand off my sleeve, gently, and gave it back to her.

"Do what you need to do," I said, and I walked out to my truck with my daughter on one side of me and Ozzie on the other, carrying a cowboy hat.
