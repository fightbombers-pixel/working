"Pack your things, Cedric. I'm with someone else now. You have until noon, or security will escort you out."

My wife said it at the seven o'clock production meeting, at the head of the long table in the main conference room of Capitol Seating Components, in front of eleven managers holding coffee cups. The guy from shipping set his cup down like it might go off.

Tonya slid a single sheet of paper across the table to me. It said Notice of Separation at the top, and her signature was at the bottom, as Director of Human Resources. Twenty-seven years I'd worked in that building. I'd started on the line as a welder at twenty-five, making nine dollars an hour, and I'd been running the whole plant floor for the last eleven.

I read the paper once. I chewed the piece of ice I had in my mouth until it was gone.

Then I unclipped my badge from my shirt, laid it on the table on top of her notice, put my keys next to it, and slid the thick blue binder I'd brought to the meeting, the one with every customer requirement we'd ever signed, into the middle of the table where anybody could reach it.

"Noon it is," I said.

Five days later, three members of the board of directors were sitting on my back porch in Pike Road, and the oldest one was holding his hat in both hands like he was in church.

What Tonya didn't know, standing at the head of that table, was whose name was printed on page four of our biggest customer's contract.

My name is Cedric Lane. I'm fifty-two, I'm the vice president of operations at a seat-frame plant in Montgomery, Alabama, and we build the steel skeletons that go inside the front seats of about four hundred thousand cars a year for the big auto plant up I-65. Twenty-seven years in a factory teaches you that the paper on the wall matters more than the man in the corner office. Whoever's name is on the paper is the one the customer calls.

Hold on to that.

Tonya stood there with her hand still flat on the table, waiting for me to argue. She's forty-nine, sharp, always dressed better than anybody in the building, and she has a habit of doing air quotes with two fingers when she says a word she doesn't respect. She'd been doing them at me for about six years.

"Do you understand what I'm telling you?" she asked.

"You're with someone else. I've got until noon."

"I'm sure you'd like to make a scene."

"I wouldn't, actually."

"Good. Because this is a business decision. The company is moving in a more" — her two fingers went up — "strategic direction."

"Who's the someone else, Tonya?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to. At the far end of the table, Marshall Greer adjusted the cuffs of his shirt and looked at the ceiling tiles like he was counting them.

Marshall is forty-one. Tonya hired him seven months earlier as our director of strategy, a job title nobody at the plant had ever heard of before he showed up. His LinkedIn says Harvard MBA, he wears loafers with no socks on a factory floor, and he says "let's circle back" about nine times in every meeting. The guys on second shift call him Loafers. Not to his face.

"Marshall will be taking over operations," Tonya said. "Effective at noon."

Lamont Fuller, our maintenance supervisor, was sitting two chairs down from me. He's sixty, bald as a doorknob, and he's kept every machine in that building alive since before Marshall was in middle school. He put both hands flat on the table.

"Excuse me," Lamont said. "Did Russ sign off on this?"

"Russ is the acting CEO, and Russ has approved the restructuring."

"Does Leon know?"

"Leon is recovering from heart surgery, Lamont. We're not going to bother a seventy-four-year-old man in the hospital with routine personnel matters."

Leon Whitfield founded the company in 1981 with a used welder and a bank loan. He'd hired me himself, off the line, when I was twenty-five. His son Russ had been running things for six weeks, ever since Leon's bypass, and Russ was a decent man who'd never once in his life read a document longer than a golf scorecard.

"Routine," Lamont said slowly. "Firing the VP of operations is routine."

"Lamont," Tonya said, "I'd be careful about how invested you are in this."

There was a little silence after that. Everybody at the table heard what she meant by it.

I stood up.

"It's all right, Lamont. Get the second shift on the robot cells by three. The welding tips on cell four are due."

"I know when the tips are due."

"I know you know." I looked at Marshall. "You know when the tips on cell four are due, Marshall?"

Marshall looked at me, and then at Tonya, and then at his phone.

"Let's circle back on that," he said.

I took my coffee cup with me when I left. It was mine. Tonya had bought it for me on our tenth anniversary, and it said World's Okayest Husband. I thought it was funny for nineteen years.

I spent the next four hours packing my office. There wasn't much. Twenty-seven years of work, and it fit into two copy-paper boxes. A picture of my mother. A plaque from the customer for zero defects in 2019. The first weld I ever made, a crooked little bead on a scrap of steel that Leon had kept and given back to me when I made VP. A jar of hard candy for anybody who came in to complain.

People came by. Not many at first, because nobody wanted to be seen doing it. Then more. A line lead from first shift came in and shook my hand and didn't say anything at all. A woman from purchasing cried. One of the forklift drivers took a piece of candy, said, "This is crap, boss," and I didn't know if he meant the candy or the situation.

At two minutes to noon, Dante came to my door.

Dante is twenty-four, our day security guard, and I'd hired him myself two years earlier when he was a skinny kid fresh out of the Army National Guard with no job and a baby on the way. He stood in the doorway in his uniform with his hands behind his back, and he looked like he wanted the floor to open up.

"Mr. Lane. I'm real sorry. She told me noon."

"It's not noon yet, Dante."

"I know. I just... I didn't want to be late, and I didn't want to be early."

I picked up the first box and handed it to him. I picked up the second one myself.

"Then help me carry these," I said.

He walked me out to my truck. The whole way across the plant floor, the machines kept running, because that's what machines do, but I could feel the people on the line watching us go. At the door, Dante put the box in my truck bed and stood there.

"Mr. Lane, they're going to need you."

"They've got Marshall."

"Sir," Dante said, "Marshall asked me yesterday where we keep the forklifts."

I laughed out loud right there in the parking lot. It came out of me like a cough. Dante grinned, a little scared.

I got in my truck and sat there for a minute with the engine running and the AC on high. My phone buzzed on the seat beside me.

It was an email to all staff, from Tonya. Subject line: Leadership Update.

Please join me in welcoming Marshall Greer to his new role as Vice President of Operations, effective immediately. Marshall brings a Harvard MBA and a fresh strategic vision to Capitol Seating. Cedric Lane is no longer with the company. Please direct all operations questions to Marshall.

Then, right under it, a text from Tonya.

I'm staying at Marshall's. Don't call me. I'll come by Wednesday for my things.

I read it twice. I put the phone face down on the seat.

Then I looked back at the plant, where the big bay doors on the shipping dock were open, and a truck from the auto plant up I-65 was backing in to pick up the morning's seat frames, the way it did every day at noon.

That truck had come every day at noon for eleven years, and every single one of those days, the paperwork in the driver's clipboard had my name on it.

Lamont came over that night with a twelve-pack of Miller Lite and a bag of ice, because he knows I chew ice, and he knows I don't keep enough in the house.

We sat at my kitchen table in Pike Road. Tonya had picked out the table. Tonya had picked out everything in that house, and I'd paid for most of it, and I'd never minded.

"Frankie Beverly," Lamont said, nodding at the speaker on the counter. "Good choice. Man gets fired, he's got to have Frankie Beverly."

"I always have Frankie Beverly."

"I know. That's why I said it." He opened two beers and pushed one across to me. "How you doing, Ced?"

"I'm chewing ice."

"I can hear that. The whole neighborhood can hear that."

"How bad was it after I left?"

Lamont rubbed his bald head with both hands.

"You want it straight?"

"I always want it straight."

"Loafers called a meeting at one o'clock. First thing he did was cancel preventive maintenance for the month. Said it was a" — and here Lamont did Tonya's air quotes with his big greasy fingers — "cost optimization."

"He canceled PM on the robot cells."

"On everything. Cell four's tips are three days overdue as of tonight. I told him. He told me to circle back on Friday."

I set my beer down.

"Lamont, the customer's quality audit is Thursday."

"I know that."

"Does Marshall know that?"

"Ced, I don't think Marshall knows what day it is." Lamont leaned forward. "I'm going to change those tips tonight anyway. Off the clock. I'm not letting bad welds go out that door with my name on them."

"He'll write you up."

"Let him. I'm sixty years old. I've got a pension and a bass boat. He can write me up on a napkin." He drank. "What are you going to do?"

"I'm going to call a lawyer."

"About the job or about Tonya?"

"Both, I imagine."

Lamont looked at me for a long moment over the top of his can.

"You know about him? About her and Loafers?"

"I know now."

"Everybody's known for about four months, Ced. They go to lunch together in his car. He parks it behind the paint building." He shook his head. "I should've told you. I didn't know how."

"Wasn't your job, Lamont."

"Didn't stop it from being the right thing to do."

The next morning I sat across from Adele Whitlock in her office on Dexter Avenue, a block from the capitol. She's sixty-ish, small, with silver hair cut short and a voice like a judge reading a sentence. A friend from church had used her for a custody fight in 2017 and said she was the meanest woman in Montgomery when she was on your side.

"Two different problems," Adele said, holding up two fingers. "Your marriage and your job. Let's do the marriage first, it's simpler."

"It's simpler?"

"In Alabama, adultery is grounds for divorce. And a judge can consider it when splitting property and deciding alimony. She told you, in front of eleven witnesses, that she's with someone else. She texted you that she's staying at his house." Adele tapped the printout of the text I'd brought her. "That's about as close to a confession as I get in this office without a priest."

"And the job?"

"That's where it gets interesting." She put on her reading glasses. "Did you ever sign a non-compete agreement with Capitol Seating?"

"No."

"Never? Twenty-seven years?"

"Leon never asked. He said a man who'd stay only because of a piece of paper wasn't a man worth keeping."

Adele looked at me over her glasses, and for the first time she smiled a little.

"I think I'd like your Mr. Whitfield," she said. "So you're free to work anywhere you want, starting today. Now. Did she give you a reason for the termination?"

"Restructuring. Strategic direction."

"In writing?"

"In writing."

"Then she owes you severance under the company's own policy. Twenty-seven years of service." Adele flipped through the employee handbook I'd brought. "Two weeks for every year, if it's a restructuring. That's fifty-four weeks of pay. Unless..."

"Unless what?"

"Unless she changes her story and says you were fired for cause."

My phone buzzed in my shirt pocket. I took it out.

It was an email from the HR department of Capitol Seating Components. Signed Tonya Lane.

Please be advised that, upon further review, your separation has been reclassified as a termination for cause, due to performance and conduct concerns. You are therefore not eligible for severance benefits.

I turned the phone around and slid it across the desk to Adele.

She read it. She took her glasses off, folded them, and set them on the desk very precisely.

"Mr. Lane," she said, "your wife just made a very expensive mistake. And she made it in writing."

Tonya came by the house on Wednesday afternoon, just like she said she would. She brought Marshall's Audi and left it running in the driveway with the air conditioner on, as if she wanted to be able to leave fast. My phone chimed in my pocket when she pulled in. Three years earlier, after somebody stole Lamont's bass boat right off its trailer in his driveway, I'd put a little camera up under the eave of my front porch, and it still told me every time a car turned in.

I was in the garage sharpening the blade on my lawnmower. Twenty-seven years of getting up at five, and you can't just stop on a Tuesday. You find something to sharpen.

She came in through the garage, which she never did, because she says it smells like gasoline.

"I'm here for my clothes," she said.

"They're where you left them."

"I need you to stay out of the bedroom while I pack."

"I'm in the garage, Tonya."

She stood there for a second, looking at me and the mower blade, and I could see she'd come in ready for something. A fight, maybe. Tears. Me begging. She'd built a whole drive over here around one of those three, and I wasn't giving her any of them.

"You could at least act like this matters to you," she said.

"It matters."

"You're sharpening a lawnmower."

"Grass doesn't stop growing because a man gets fired."

She did the air quotes. "Fired. You're making it sound so dramatic. You were" — the fingers again — "transitioned."

"For cause, your email said. Performance and conduct."

She blinked. "That's standard language."

"Twenty-seven years. Zero-defect plaque from the customer. Never been written up once. What conduct, Tonya?"

"I don't have to explain an HR decision to you."

"You're right. You'll have to explain it to a judge."

That stopped her for a second. She hadn't expected a judge. She'd expected me to sulk.

"You talked to a lawyer."

"I did."

"Already? You couldn't even wait a week?"

"You couldn't wait until I'd cleaned out my desk to send the email about Marshall."

Her face went tight. She came a couple of steps further into the garage.

"You want to know why, Cedric? You really want to know? Because you peaked. You peaked twenty years ago as a welder with a high school diploma, and you've been coasting on Leon's affection ever since. Marshall went to Harvard. Marshall has a vision. Marshall talks about the future. You talk about welding tips."

"Welding tips are the future, Tonya. Every single seat that goes out that door has got about forty welds in it. Every one of those welds holds a person in a car crash."

"See? That. That's exactly what I mean. You can't think bigger than a weld."

"Somebody has to."

She threw her hands up. "I'm going to pack."

"Go ahead."

She was halfway to the kitchen door when she stopped and turned around.

"One more thing. Where's the customer binder?"

"What customer binder?"

"Don't play dumb. The blue one. With all the customer contracts. Marshall says it's not in your office. You took confidential company property off the premises, and if it's not back on my desk by tomorrow morning, I'm calling the police."

I wiped my hands on a shop rag, slow.

"Tonya. I put that binder in the middle of the conference room table on Monday morning. In front of eleven managers. Right next to my badge and my keys. You were standing right there. You watched me do it."

She opened her mouth. Then she closed it. You could see her rewinding Monday in her head.

"Marshall said—"

"Then Marshall didn't look on the table. Or somebody moved it. Either way, ask the eleven people who watched me put it there."

"I'm going to look into this," she said.

"You should. While you're in there, you might want to read it."

"Why would I read it? I'm HR."

"Page four," I said. "Just page four. It's short."

She looked at me like I was speaking another language. Then she went inside and packed four suitcases while I sharpened the mower blade, and I heard her on the phone up there, her voice going up and down, and Marshall's name a few times.

She was carrying the last suitcase out to the Audi when my phone rang. The screen said Grace Tilley.

Tonya stopped in the driveway. She knew that name. Everybody at the plant knew that name.

Grace Tilley is the supplier quality manager at the auto plant up I-65. She's forty-five, she's audited our plant twice a year for eight years, and she has never once, in all that time, given anybody a compliment. If she says "fine," you go home and celebrate. If she says "interesting," you start updating your résumé.

I answered on speaker, because my hands were greasy and I wasn't trying to hide anything.

"This is Cedric."

"Cedric, it's Grace." No hello. Grace doesn't do hello. "I logged into our supplier portal this morning and somebody at your plant changed the management representative on your account to a Marshall Greer. I've never heard of a Marshall Greer. Did you quit?"

"No ma'am. I was terminated on Monday."

There was a long pause on the line. Tonya was standing in the driveway with the suitcase handle in her hand, not moving.

"Terminated," Grace said. "Who terminated you?"

"Our HR department."

"Your HR department is your wife."

"Yes ma'am."

Another pause.

"Cedric, I'm going to read you something you already know, because I need to be able to say I told you. Page four of our supply agreement, section six. 'The Supplier shall designate a Management Representative with full authority over production and quality at the supplying facility. The current Management Representative is Cedric Lane. Any change in Management Representative shall trigger a requalification review of the supplying facility, during which the Customer may place shipments on hold.'"

Out in the driveway, Tonya's hand slipped off the suitcase handle. The suitcase tipped over on the concrete.

"I know it, Grace."

"Your plant is sixty percent of our seat frames for two models. If I put you on hold, we have to pull from the backup supplier in Tennessee, and the backup supplier in Tennessee is going to charge us like they're printing money. My boss is not going to be happy. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"I'm coming down Thursday at nine for the regular audit anyway. I'm going to need to see who's running that plant." She paused. "And Cedric? I'd like you there. As the outgoing representative, you have to sign the transfer. That's procedure. Bring a visitor badge."

"I don't work there anymore, Grace. They won't let me in."

"They'll let you in," Grace said. "I'll make sure of it."

She hung up.

I put the phone in my pocket and went back to the mower. Behind me, out in the driveway, I heard Tonya pick up the suitcase, and then put it down again, and then pick it up, and then I heard her car door, and then I heard her on the phone before the door even closed.

"Marshall," she said. "Marshall, where is the blue binder? No. Listen to me. Where is the blue binder right now?"

Thursday morning at a quarter to nine, Russ Whitfield called me.

Russ is forty-four, Leon's only son, a good-hearted man who should have been a golf pro, and he's always been honest that he doesn't know which end of a welder is hot.

"Cedric. Hey. Look. Grace Tilley's here early. She's sitting in the lobby, and she says she won't start the audit until you're in the building."

"I don't work there anymore, Russ."

"I know. I know." He sounded like a man who hadn't slept. "Can you come in? Just for the audit? Tonya says it's fine, it's just a formality, you sign the transfer form and leave."

"Tonya says."

"Tonya says." He paused. "Ced, I signed what she put in front of me. She said it was a restructuring. She said Dad had talked about it before his surgery. I didn't check."

"Your dad never talked about it, Russ."

"I know that now." He cleared his throat. "Will you come?"

I got there at nine. Dante was at the front desk, and he handed me a paper badge that said VISITOR in big red letters, and he looked like it physically hurt him to do it.

"Sorry, Mr. Lane."

"Don't be. It's a nice badge."

Grace was waiting in the lobby in her steel-toed shoes and her safety glasses pushed up on her head, holding a clipboard against her chest. Next to her was Marshall Greer in his loafers, and next to him was Tonya, in a gray suit, with a smile on her face that didn't go anywhere near her eyes.

"Grace," Marshall said, putting out his hand. "Marshall Greer. So glad to finally connect. I've got a lot of exciting ideas I'd love to circle back with you on."

Grace looked at his hand. She looked at his loafers. She didn't shake.

"Where are your safety shoes, Mr. Greer?"

"I'm sorry?"

"You're the management representative of a facility with stamping presses and robotic welders. You're wearing loafers with no socks. Where are your safety shoes?"

Marshall looked down at his feet like he was seeing them for the first time.

"I'll get some," he said.

"Do that," Grace said, and wrote something on her clipboard.

We walked the floor. Grace went straight to the robot welding cells, because Grace always went straight to the robot welding cells. She pulled the maintenance log off the hook on cell four and flipped through it.

"Preventive maintenance was canceled on Monday," she said. "By whom?"

"That was a cost decision," Marshall said. "Part of a broader optimization initiative."

"And yet the welding tips on cell four were changed Monday night, a little before midnight. Off the clock. Signed by L. Fuller."

Lamont was standing about ten feet away with his arms crossed. He didn't say anything. He didn't have to.

Grace looked at Marshall.

"So your maintenance supervisor did maintenance you canceled, on his own time, without pay, so that bad welds wouldn't ship to my plant. Is that right?"

"I wasn't aware of that," Marshall said.

"Clearly." She wrote on her clipboard. "Mr. Greer, what's the first-pass yield on this cell?"

"Let's circle back—"

"No. What's the first-pass yield on this cell?"

Marshall looked at Tonya. Tonya looked at the floor. Grace turned and looked at me.

"Ninety-eight point six last month," I said. "Cell two runs a little lower because the fixture's older. Ninety-seven point nine. Lamont's rebuilding it in March."

Grace wrote it down. She didn't even check. That's eight years of trust, written down without checking.

At the end of the walk-through, we stood in the conference room, the same one, at the same long table. Tonya stood at the head of it, where she'd stood on Monday. Grace put her clipboard down in front of her and took her safety glasses off her head.

"Here's where we are," Grace said. "I'm placing Capitol Seating Components on a requalification hold, effective today. That means a ninety-day review. During the review, I'm cutting your volume in half and moving the other half to Tennessee."

Russ, who'd been standing quietly by the window, made a sound like somebody'd stepped on his foot.

"Half," Russ said. "That's... Grace, that's nineteen million dollars."

"About that, yes."

Tonya found her voice.

"This is retaliation. He told you to do this. He's been in contact with you, he's sabotaging the transition—"

"Mrs. Lane," Grace said, without raising her voice at all. "I called him. On Wednesday. Because somebody at this plant changed my supplier portal without telling me. That somebody was you. It's in the access log."

Tonya stopped talking.

"There is one other option," Grace said. "Under section six, if the original management representative is reinstated before the review formally opens, the review is waived. I open it Monday morning at eight. Monday, or ninety days." She picked up her clipboard. "Mr. Whitfield, I'll need your decision before I open that file. I'll also need a résumé for Mr. Greer. Our portal requires one for any new representative. Standard due diligence."

Marshall smiled at her for the first time all morning.

"Happy to send that over. Harvard's on there."

"I'm sure it is," Grace said.

She walked out. Russ went after her. Tonya stood at the head of the table, both hands flat on the wood, and she looked at me across the table with an expression I'd never seen on her face in nineteen years of marriage.

"You did this," she said. "You planned this."

"Tonya, I didn't plan anything. I just put the binder on the table. You were the one who didn't read it."

I unclipped my visitor badge and laid it on the table in the same spot where I'd put my real one on Monday. Then I walked out.

Friday morning at ten, Grace Tilley called me again.

"I'm not supposed to tell you this," she said. "But the portal still lists you as the management representative of record until Monday, so I'm going to tell you anyway. We ran Mr. Greer's résumé through our standard verification this morning."

"And?"

"He doesn't have a Harvard MBA."

I sat down at my kitchen table.

"He doesn't?"

"He has a certificate from a six-week online course called Harvard Business School Online. It's a real course. People take it on their lunch breaks. It's not an MBA, any more than my nephew's online welding video makes him a welder." She paused. "He listed it on his résumé as Master of Business Administration, Harvard University, 2019. That's on paper now, Cedric. On our portal. Under his name."

"Does Tonya know?"

"Mrs. Lane hired him. Seven months ago. I'd think she'd have checked."

"She's HR, Grace. She'd have checked."

"Then I'd think somebody might want to ask her why she didn't."

That evening, after dark, somebody knocked on my front door. Not the doorbell. A knock. Three of them, quick, the way you knock when you don't want the neighbors to hear.

It was Tonya. Alone. No Audi in the driveway. She'd driven her own car.

She'd been crying, and she'd fixed her makeup in the car, and she'd been crying again.

"Can I come in?"

"You can talk from the porch."

She took a breath.

"Russ is going to fire Marshall Monday. Grace sent him something about the résumé. Russ says he has to report it to the board."

"Sounds like Russ is doing his job."

"And if he fires Marshall, the board's going to ask who hired him. And who didn't check." She wiped her eyes. "Cedric, I need you to come back. Just for thirty days. As a consultant. Just until the customer calms down. If you come back, Grace waives the hold, and nobody looks too hard at anything, and we can all just... move on."

"We."

"The company. The company can move on."

"You can move on, you mean."

"Please. Please, Cedric. I'll put it in writing. Severance, the full fifty-four weeks. I'll reverse the for-cause. Whatever you want."

"Tonya, Monday you gave me until noon."

"I know what I did!"

"Wednesday you threatened to call the police on me for a binder you watched me leave on the table."

"I was upset!"

"Today you're upset again. And you're on my porch asking me to save the job of the man you left me for. Is that about right?"

She didn't say anything. She just stood there in the porch light.

"No," I said.

"No?"

"No."

Her face changed. The tears stopped like somebody'd turned off a faucet. And what was under the tears was something I'd been married to for nineteen years without ever really looking at it.

"Fine," she said. "Then here's how it's going to go. Monday morning, before Russ says one word to the board, I'm filing a formal complaint that you threatened me. Tonight. On this porch. You threatened me, and I was afraid for my safety, and HR is going to have to investigate it. And no board in Alabama is going to bring back a man under investigation for threatening a woman."

"I didn't threaten you, Tonya."

"Who's going to say you didn't?"

She turned around and walked to her car. She didn't run. She walked, slow, like a woman who'd just found her last card and was sure it was an ace.

I stood in the doorway until her taillights turned the corner. Then I looked up, past the porch light, at the little white camera I'd put up under the eave three years earlier, after somebody stole Lamont's bass boat off his trailer.

The little green light on it was on.

Saturday morning I was on my back porch with a cup of coffee and a bowl of ice when I heard car doors in the driveway. Three of them.

Leon Whitfield came around the side of the house first, slow, with a cane in one hand and his old straw fedora in the other. He's seventy-four, six weeks out from a triple bypass, and he'd lost about twenty pounds he couldn't spare. Behind him came Russ, holding his father's elbow, and behind Russ came Doris Akins, who's sixty-six and has sat on the Capitol Seating board since 1995, and who once made a bank vice president cry in a meeting by asking him the same question four times in a row.

I stood up. You stand up for Leon. Twenty-seven years, I'd never not stood up for him.

"Sit down, son," Leon said. "You'll make me feel old."

"You are old, Leon."

"That's why I need the chair."

Russ got him into the rocker. Doris took the swing. Russ stood by the porch rail with his hands in his pockets like a kid sent to the principal's office.

Leon sat there for a second getting his breath back. He was holding his hat in both hands on his knees, turning it around and around by the brim, like he was in the front pew at a funeral.

"I didn't know," Leon said. "I want you to hear that first. I was laid up with tubes in me, and nobody told me a word until Russ came to the house last night with that woman's audit report in his hand."

"I know you didn't know, Leon."

"Doesn't matter whether you know it. I had to say it." He looked at his son. "Russ."

Russ cleared his throat.

"I signed it, Ced. The restructuring. I signed whatever she put in front of me for six weeks. She said Dad had talked about bringing in new blood. She said you were burned out. She said you'd told her you wanted to retire early." He looked at the porch boards. "I didn't call you. I didn't call Dad. I just signed."

"I never said I wanted to retire."

"I know that now too."

Doris pulled a folded piece of paper out of her purse and unfolded it on her knee.

"Let me tell you where we are, Cedric, because Leon's going to get sentimental in a minute and Russ is going to apologize six more times, and I'd like to get to lunch." She read off the paper. "Grace Tilley opens the requalification file Monday at eight. If she does, we lose half our volume for ninety days. That's nineteen million dollars, and it means layoffs on second shift by Christmas. Three hundred and forty people work in that building. Most of them you hired."

"I know how many people work there, Doris."

"Then you know what I'm about to ask." She folded the paper back up. "Come back. Monday morning. Same job, same title, same office. We reverse the termination, we reverse the for-cause, we pay you for the week, and we put it in writing that it never should have happened."

Leon leaned forward in the rocker.

"Please," he said. "I'm asking. I don't think I've asked anybody for anything since 1981. I'm asking you."

I looked at the three of them. The founder with his hat in his hands. The son who couldn't look up. The toughest woman on the board, waiting.

I took a piece of ice out of the bowl and chewed it.

"I'll come in Monday at seven," I said. "To the production meeting. Same room. Same eleven managers. You three bring whatever paper you want to bring. And Tonya and Marshall are at that table."

Doris narrowed her eyes. "Why?"

"Because they sent me out of that room in front of eleven people. If I'm coming back, I'm coming back in front of the same eleven."

Leon started to laugh, which turned into a cough, which turned back into a laugh.

"That's my welder," he said. "That's the boy I hired."

"Leon, there's something else you ought to know before Monday."

I took my phone out, found the video from the night before, and handed it to him. Leon watched it with his reading glasses on the end of his nose. Doris leaned over his shoulder. Russ came around behind the rocker.

On the little screen, under the porch light, Tonya's voice came out thin and clear.

Monday morning, before Russ says one word to the board, I'm filing a formal complaint that you threatened me. Tonight. On this porch. Who's going to say you didn't?

Leon handed the phone back to me without a word. Doris took out her pen and wrote something on her folded piece of paper.

"Seven o'clock," Doris said, standing up. "Don't be late."

"I've never been late in twenty-seven years, Doris."

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm saying it."

Monday morning at five minutes to seven, I walked into Capitol Seating Components with a paper VISITOR badge on my shirt pocket and a cup of coffee from the gas station, because my World's Okayest Husband mug was still in a box in my garage.

Dante was at the front desk. He stood up when I came in. He didn't say anything. He just stood up, the way the line leads stand up when Leon walks the floor.

The conference room was full. All eleven managers, in the same chairs as the Monday before, like somebody had taken a photograph and put everybody back. Lamont was two chairs down from my old seat with his arms crossed. Leon was at the end of the table in a wheelchair Russ had borrowed from the hospital, with his hat on the table in front of him. Doris was beside him with a folder. Russ stood by the window.

Grace Tilley was on the speakerphone in the middle of the table. Her voice came out of it flat as a ruler.

"I'm on, Mr. Whitfield. I have the requalification file open on my screen. It's unsigned."

Tonya and Marshall came in last. Tonya was in a navy suit, and she was carrying a thin folder of her own, and she walked straight to the head of the table, where she'd stood the Monday before. Marshall was in his loafers. He'd found a pair of socks.

Tonya didn't sit. She put her folder down on the table and opened it.

"Before we begin," she said, "I'm required, as Director of Human Resources, to inform the board that a formal complaint was filed this morning against Cedric Lane. On Friday night, at his residence, he threatened me. I was in fear for my safety. Under company policy, any individual under active investigation for threatening conduct cannot be reinstated to a supervisory position until the investigation is complete." She looked down the table at me. "I'm sorry, Cedric. I take no pleasure in this."

Eleven managers looked at me. A couple of them looked at their shoes.

I didn't say a word. I took my phone out of my shirt pocket and slid it down the table to Doris, the way I'd slid the binder on Monday.

Doris pressed play and turned the volume all the way up.

Tonya's voice came out of the little speaker, under my porch light, into a room of eleven managers and three board members and one auto-plant quality manager on a speakerphone.

I'm filing a formal complaint that you threatened me. Tonight. On this porch. You threatened me, and I was afraid for my safety. And no board in Alabama is going to bring back a man under investigation for threatening a woman.

Then my voice. I didn't threaten you, Tonya.

Then hers. Who's going to say you didn't?

Doris stopped it.

"The porch," Doris said pleasantly. "That's who."

Tonya's hand was flat on her folder. She didn't move it. She was looking at the phone like it had bitten her.

"That's... That was a private conversation. That's illegal—"

"Alabama is a one-party consent state, Mrs. Lane," Doris said. "And it was his porch, and it was his camera, and you knocked on his door."

Lamont uncrossed his arms.

"While we're doing show-and-tell," Lamont said, "I've got one."

He reached under his chair and put the blue customer binder on the table with a thump. The whole table jumped.

"Thursday night, after the audit, I went in to fix the thermostat in the VP office. Marshall's office now, I guess. It was in the bottom drawer of his credenza. Under a gym bag." He flipped it open to a page marked with a yellow sticky note. "Page four. Somebody put a note on it."

He held it up. In blue ink, in neat block letters, the note said: GRACE — IRRELEVANT? CIRCLE BACK.

Everybody turned and looked at Marshall.

"I... I was going to read it," Marshall said.

"You took it off the conference room table last Monday," Lamont said. "Right after Ced walked out. I watched you do it. Then you told Tonya he'd stolen it."

"Tonya told me to!"

The room went dead quiet.

Marshall heard it come out of his own mouth a half second too late. You could see it land on him. He turned to Tonya with both hands out.

"Tonya, tell them. You said he'd never come back for it. You said nobody would ever look at page four. You said—"

"Shut up, Marshall."

"—you said nobody checks résumés at a seat-frame plant in Alabama!"

From the speakerphone in the middle of the table, Grace Tilley's flat voice said, "Somebody does."

Leon Whitfield picked his hat up off the table. He put it on his head. Then he took it off again and set it back down, and he looked up the table at the woman standing at the head of it.

"Mrs. Lane," Leon said. "You hired Mr. Greer seven months ago without verifying his education. You started a personal relationship with a man you hired and you never disclosed it, which is a violation of the policy you wrote yourself, in 2016, because I asked you to. You fired my vice president of operations under a restructuring I never approved. You changed our customer's portal without authorization. You reclassified a termination for cause with no cause. And this morning you filed a false complaint with this company's HR department, which is you."

He paused to catch his breath. Nobody filled the silence.

"Russ," Leon said.

Russ came away from the window. He took a sheet of paper out of Doris's folder and laid it on the table in front of Tonya. It said Notice of Separation at the top.

"Mrs. Lane," Russ said, "you're terminated for cause. Mr. Greer, so are you." His voice shook a little, but he got it out. "Pack your things. You have until noon, or security will escort you out."

Tonya stared at the paper. Then she looked down the table at me, and her face did something I'll remember for the rest of my life. It looked, for one second, exactly like the face I'd fallen in love with in 2005, before she learned the air quotes.

"Cedric," she said. "Please. Say something. Tell them it's a misunderstanding. Tell them we can fix this. Please."

"You gave me until noon," I said. "I was out the door with two minutes to spare."

Leon reached into Doris's folder and took out one more sheet of paper. He held it up toward me with a hand that shook a little.

"Reinstatement," he said. "Full title. Full back pay. Signed by every member of the board. I'm begging you, son. Don't make an old man ask twice. Don't leave."

I unclipped the paper VISITOR badge from my shirt and laid it on the table. Dante had already walked in from the hallway, and he put my real badge down beside it, the one I'd left on that table eight days before.

I clipped it back on.

"Grace," I said, toward the speakerphone. "This is Cedric Lane, management representative. Go ahead and close that file."

"Closing it," Grace said. "Cedric?"

"Yes ma'am."

"Fine," she said, and hung up.

Lamont started laughing so hard he had to hold on to the table.

Tonya picked up her folder, and the separation notice, and walked out of the conference room without looking at anybody. Marshall followed her in his loafers and his brand-new socks.

Dante stood in the doorway and waited for them to pass. Then he looked at his watch, and he looked back into the room at all of us, and he said it loud enough for the whole table to hear.

"Mrs. Lane? It's not noon yet. But I'm not going to be late."
