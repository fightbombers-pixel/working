"Just having coffee with a friend."

That was the text from my wife, and it landed on my phone at ten minutes to ten on a Saturday morning, while I was sitting at a folding table in the IBEW Local 242 union hall in Duluth with about forty electricians and a plate of pancakes. Frank Esposito was sitting across from me, and he saw my face, so he leaned over and read it upside down.

"Coffee with a friend," Frank said. "On a Saturday. Since when does Stacy drink coffee with anybody?"

"Since about September," I said.

Twenty-nine years of marriage, and my wife had never once in her life met a friend for coffee. Stacy drinks one cup at home, black, standing at the counter, and she complains about the price of it at the store.

I didn't text back right away. I scrolled up to the message above hers, the one that had come in four minutes earlier from a number I'd saved only nine days before. It was three photographs and five words. The photographs were of a silver Lexus parked behind the Amazing Grace bakery in Canal Park. My wife was in the passenger seat. A man with a good haircut was in the driver's seat, and her hand was on the back of his neck.

The five words were: Taken at 9:40. Liked them.

I typed my reply with one thumb, holding a fork in the other hand.

Ask him if his wife liked the new photos.

I pressed send, put my phone face up on the table next to the syrup, and cut a bite of pancake.

"Gil," Frank said. "What did you just do?"

"I answered her."

Then the phone started buzzing, and it didn't stop.

Twelve messages. I counted them out loud for Frank, because by the third one, half the table was listening.

One. What??

Two. What are you talking about

Three. Gil call me

Four. Who sent you something

Five. Gil I'm serious CALL ME

Six. It's not what you think

Seven. He's just a friend from work

Eight. Did someone send you pictures? Those could be anybody

Nine. Answer your phone

Ten. Gilbert Atwood answer your phone right now

Eleven. Please

Twelve. Is she there with you?

That last one I read twice. Is she there with you. Not who sent them, not what pictures. She knew exactly which wife I meant.

Twenty minutes later, a man named Neil Burch was standing in the parking lot behind the Amazing Grace bakery with his shirt untucked and his phone pressed to his ear, yelling at somebody who had just hung up on him. I know that because the woman who took the photographs was still sitting across the street in a green Subaru, and she took one more.

What my wife didn't know, sitting in that Lexus, was what was inside the gray accordion folder on the passenger seat of that green Subaru. Three years of bank records. And her own signature was on about two hundred pages of them.

My name is Gilbert Atwood. I'm 55 years old, and I've been a union electrician in Duluth, Minnesota, for thirty-three years. The first thing they teach you as an apprentice is that a wire is never dead because somebody tells you it's dead. You don't take anybody's word. Not the foreman's, not the homeowner's, not your own. You put the tester on it and you look for yourself.

Hold on to that.

Frank put down his fork.

"Gil. Who's 'his wife'?"

"Maribel Burch. She's an ER nurse at St. Luke's."

"And how do you know Maribel Burch?"

"Nine days ago she called this hall looking for me."

Frank looked at the other guys at the table, who were all suddenly very interested in their pancakes, and he lowered his voice.

"Come outside."

We stood by my truck in the parking lot with the wind coming off Lake Superior, which in November in Duluth feels like getting slapped with a frozen fish.

"Talk," Frank said.

"Stacy's boss is a man named Neil Burch. He owns Burch Insurance on London Road. You know it, the yellow sign with the lighthouse."

"Everybody knows it. They do half the boats in the marina."

She said she'd been going through her husband's payroll records, because she does the books for the agency on her days off, and she saw that the office manager was Stacy Atwood. She looked Stacy up on Facebook. Stacy's page says, 'Married to Gilbert Atwood, IBEW 242.' So she called the hall."

"Just like that."

"Just like that. She said, 'Mr. Atwood, I think my husband is sleeping with your wife, and I think it's been going on since Labor Day. I've had a private investigator on him for three weeks. I'd like to show you something.'"

Frank whistled through his teeth. "And you went."

"I met her at the Perkins on Central Entrance. She had photos from three different weeks. Stacy getting into his Lexus at the agency after closing. Stacy and him at a supper club in Superior. Stacy coming out of a motel in Two Harbors."

"Two Harbors," Frank said. "She told you she was at her sister's in Two Harbors."

"Twice."

He put a hand on the hood of my truck.

"Why didn't you say anything for nine days?"

"Because Maribel asked me not to. She said there's more to this than the motel. She said there's money involved, and the people who need to see it haven't seen it yet. She said if I blew it up too early, her husband would have time to clean up." I looked at my phone. It was buzzing again in my hand. "And then this morning her investigator called her and said they were at the bakery, and she sent me the photos, and Stacy sent me that text, and I just couldn't stand there and let her lie to me one more time."

"So you blew it up."

"I lit the fuse," I said. "That's not the same thing."

My phone buzzed. Not Stacy this time. Maribel.

Neil just called me screaming. He knows you know. He knows I know. He's on his way to the agency. Don't go home yet. Call me.

"Everything," I said.

"Over coffee."

It just sat there running with the garage door up for about five minutes, with my wife inside it, while I looked at her purse on my kitchen floor.

Her phone was in it. It buzzed. I didn't touch it.

It buzzed again. Then the car door opened and she came back in, snatched up the purse, and looked at the screen, and whatever she read made her mouth go thin.

Stacy slept in our daughter Kate's old room that night with the door locked. Kate's nineteen, a sophomore at UMD, and she lives in the dorms across town, so the room still has her horse posters on the wall. On Sunday Stacy left at seven in the morning without a word and didn't come back until dark.

Monday I took a personal day and went to see Paula Ferraro.

Paula has an office in the old Medical Arts building downtown, and she's been doing divorces in St. Louis County for twenty-five years. She has the voice of a woman who used to be a high school basketball coach, which she was.

"Minnesota's a no-fault state," she said before I'd even sat down all the way. "I want you to hear that first, Gil. The judge does not care who was in the Lexus."

"I figured."

"What the judge cares about is money and property. House?"

"Ours. Bought in '98 in Lakeside. Paid off two years ago."

"Kate. She's nineteen. She's on her own mostly."

You don't touch that man's records. Whatever that nurse shows you, you look at it, and you keep your hands in your pockets."

"I'm an electrician, Paula. I always keep my hands where I can see them."

She almost smiled.

Maribel Burch met me Tuesday at six at the same Perkins on Central Entrance, in the same back booth. She came straight from a twelve-hour shift, still in maroon scrubs, with her hair pulled back and her eyes rimmed red. She was forty-eight, small, very calm, the kind of woman who's spent twenty years telling people their kid is going to be fine while she works on the kid.

She had the gray folder with her. She put it on the table and kept her hand on it.

"I'm sorry about Saturday," she said. "I shouldn't have sent you those."

"I'm glad you did."

"Doug, my investigator, was very upset with me. He said I tipped Neil off. He was right. Neil went straight to the agency and found out the bank statement binder was gone. I took it two weeks ago and made copies and put it back, but on Friday I took it again, because I wanted to show you the originals." She shook her head. "That was stupid. But I'm tired, Mr. Atwood. I've been tired for a year."

"A year?"

"I found out about the first one a year ago. A woman from his Rotary club. That's when I started doing the books myself. That's when I found this."

She opened the folder.

I'm not an accountant. I know amps and volts and conduit fill. But I could follow what she showed me.

When you buy insurance through a local agency, a lot of the time you write the check to the agency, and the agency sends the money on to the insurance company. That money isn't the agency's. It goes into a special account called a premium trust account, and the law in Minnesota says the agency has to keep it separate and send it on. You can't touch it. It's like your customer's money sitting in your truck. You just drive it where it's going.

"For three years," Maribel said, "Neil has been holding back part of the premiums and moving it out of the trust account into the agency's operating account, and then out of the operating account into a brokerage account in his own name. About three hundred and eighty thousand dollars so far. He covers it by delaying payments to the carriers and moving money around between customers. Every month he robs Peter to pay Paul."

"Does he know you know?"

"He does now." She turned a page. "But here's the part you need to see."

It was a transfer authorization form from the bank. One of dozens. At the bottom, in the space for the authorized officer, was a signature I'd seen on every birthday card I'd gotten for twenty-nine years.

Stacy Atwood, Office Manager.

"She signs them," I said.

"Her name's on all of them. Two hundred and eleven transfers. And every online transfer goes through a banking login in her name."

"So she's in on it."

Maribel looked at me for a long time. She has very dark eyes, and they didn't blink much.

"I don't know," she said. "And I need you to hear that, because I'm not going to lie to you. I don't know if your wife knows what she's signing. Neil puts papers in front of people and they sign them. He did it to me for twenty years. But I know this. If the state comes in and opens these books, the first name they're going to see is hers. Not his. And I don't think that's an accident."

"He's setting her up."

"He's had her name on everything since the week he hired her. Six years ago. Long before he was sleeping with her." She closed the folder. "He picks people he can use, Mr. Atwood. He picked me. He picked her."

"I filed a complaint with the Department of Commerce a month ago. They have an investigator named Ray Donnelly. He's been building a case quietly. My lawyer says he's ready to execute a warrant on the agency's records, and he wants to do it when Neil's there and the books are there and nobody's had time to shred anything." She took a breath. "The agency is having its thirtieth anniversary open house this Saturday. Customers, cake, the mayor's supposed to stop by. Neil will be there all day."

"That's a week to the day from the coffee."

Thursday night, a little after two in the morning, I woke up because the motion light over the garage had come on and it was shining through the gap in the bedroom curtains. That light has a sensor on it I installed myself. A raccoon won't trip it. A deer won't trip it unless it walks right up to the door.

I lay there and listened. Then I heard it. The side door of the garage, the one I never lock because the deadbolt's been sticking since August. The little scrape it makes on the threshold.

I got up in my T-shirt and my boxers, and I took the big Maglite off the dresser, and I went down the hall in the dark. I passed Kate's room. The door was closed. No light underneath.

The door from the kitchen to the garage was open about an inch. I never leave it open. Stacy always does.

I pushed it with two fingers.

There was somebody in my garage. A man in a dark ski mask and a Carhartt coat, crouched in front of my tool chest with a little penlight in his teeth, jerking on the bottom drawer. It was locked. He had a pry bar. He was about to do something terrible to a twelve-hundred-dollar tool chest.

I turned on my Maglite and put it right in his eyes.

"That drawer's locked," I said. "Can I help you find something?"

He came at me again. He was big, and he was scared, which is worse than big. "In November?"

He kept smiling. He kept his hand out. I didn't take it.

Stacy came in from the kitchen carrying a stack of paper plates. She saw me. She saw Neil. She put the plates down on the table very carefully.

At ten minutes after eleven, three people came up the porch steps who didn't look like customers. A gray-haired man in a parka with a folder of his own. Sergeant Ann Mitchell in uniform. And behind them, small and calm in a long black coat, Maribel Burch.

The gray-haired man walked right up to Neil in the middle of the front room.

"Mr. Burch, I'm Ray Donnelly with the Minnesota Department of Commerce, Insurance Fraud Unit. I have a warrant to search these premises and seize the agency's financial records, including the premium trust account records and the computers."

The whole front room stopped. The councilman put his coffee down.

Neil looked at the warrant, and then at his wife in the doorway, and I watched him think fast. He was good at it. I'll give him that.

"Mr. Donnelly," he said, loud enough for every customer in the room, "I'm glad you're here. I've been trying to figure out where that money went for months. You should talk to my office manager. Every transfer out of that account has her signature on it. She has the login. She does the banking. I trusted her."

Sixty heads turned to look at my wife, standing by the paper plates.

Stacy opened her mouth and nothing came out.

So I stepped in.

"Mr. Donnelly, my name is Gilbert Atwood. I'm her husband. I'm also in the middle of divorcing her, so I'm not here to do her any favors." I took the gray folder out from under my arm and handed it to him. "These are copies Mrs. Burch gave me for safekeeping. On page forty there's a transfer from October 12th, fifty-one thousand dollars, made through my wife's login."

"Thank you, Mr. Atwood. We'll get to all of it."

"On October 12th, my wife was in the bleachers at a college volleyball game in Minneapolis, sitting next to me, from noon to five." I held up my phone with the GO KATE #11 picture on it. "There are about two hundred other parents who saw her there, and about thirty pictures on my phone with the time on them. So whoever made that transfer, it wasn't her."

Ray Donnelly took my phone and looked at it.

"The bank will have the IP address for that transfer," he said, mostly to himself. Then he looked at Neil. "Mr. Burch, where were you on the afternoon of October 12th?"

"I... I don't remember."

"I can help you with the first half of it," I said. "You were in the top row of those same bleachers in Minneapolis, in a blue fleece, until about the second set. Then you left. My daughter saw you. She thought it was weird that an insurance man would drive a hundred and fifty miles to watch somebody else's kid play volleyball."

A woman by the coffee urn, one of the marina customers, said "Oh, my Lord" under her breath, not quite quietly enough.

Neil's face went a dark, blotchy red from the collar up.

"So you drove back," I said. "And you stopped at the office. And somebody logged in as my wife."

"Here," Maribel said quietly from the doorway. "He called me at four from the road and said he was stopping at the office to catch up on paperwork. It was a Saturday. He walked in our door at six."

Neil turned on her. "You set this whole thing up."

"You set it up, Neil," she said. "Six years ago. The week you hired her. You told me she'd be perfect because she signs whatever you put in front of her."

Sergeant Mitchell stepped forward then.

"Mr. Burch, I'd also like to talk to you about a burglary on Tuesday Avenue on Thursday night. Your agency courtesy truck is on the homeowner's camera, and we've got blood on a broken window. Would you mind unwrapping that hand for me?"

He didn't unwrap it. He put it behind his back like a little kid with a cookie.

"I want a lawyer," Neil said.

"That's your right," Sergeant Mitchell said, and she took the cuffs off her belt. "Neil Burch, you're under arrest for burglary in the first degree and assault. Turn around."

He turned around. Sixty customers watched him do it, under a sheet cake that said 30 YEARS OF KEEPING DULUTH COVERED. Somebody's phone was already recording. The councilman was already halfway to the door.

They walked him out. Maribel stepped aside to let them pass, and he looked at her, and she looked right back at him, and she didn't say anything at all.

Stacy was still standing by the paper plates. Ray Donnelly walked over to her, very polite.

"Mrs. Atwood. We're going to need a statement from you. Today, if you can. You'll want a lawyer of your own."

She nodded. Then she came across the room to me, in front of all of them, and stood close.

"You didn't have to do that," she said. "You could've let me go down for it. After what I did to you, Gil, you could've just stood there and let me go down."

"I test the wire, Stacy," I said. "Even when it's yours."
