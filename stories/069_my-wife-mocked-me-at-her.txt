"He fixes air conditioners. I make six figures!"

My wife said it into the head table microphone at her sister's wedding reception, in front of a hundred and eighty guests, right after the best man asked who'd be paying for the open bar. She laughed when she said it. A few people laughed with her, the awkward kind of laugh where everybody looks at their plate.

I was sitting two seats down from her in a rented tux, and I had just walked her baby sister down the aisle, because their daddy had been gone eleven years and Kiana asked me to.

I just smiled, and I lifted my glass of sweet tea toward her, like she'd made a toast.

Six figures. Alana had said that number at every dinner party for three years, ever since Valley Claims Group made her director of sales. A hundred and twelve thousand a year. She liked the sound of it. She didn't like the sound of what I did for a living, which was climb on roofs in Columbus, Georgia in August and fix the units that keep everybody else cool.

Down at the end of the head table, her boss, Ted Lambert, raised his glass to her like she'd scored a touchdown. Ted was the bride's boss too. That's how he got an invitation. He was forty-six, had his sunglasses pushed up on his head indoors, and he laughed louder than anybody.

Nineteen hours later, my wife would be sitting in her company's chief financial officer's office on a Sunday afternoon, with the door closed, trying to explain why the man who owned their building had my last name.

What she didn't know was what was written in a green logbook on the night guard's desk on the ninth floor of that building. Seven months of it. Tuesdays and Thursdays.

My name is Isaiah Whitlow. I'm fifty-five years old. I own a commercial heating and air company with nine technicians and eleven trucks, and I still go up on the roof myself when it's a hard call. Twenty-nine years in this trade taught me one thing above all. Every building tells you what's wrong with it if you know where to put your hand. The air always tells you the truth before the people do. Hold on to that.

About forty minutes after the toast, the air conditioning in the reception hall quit.

It was the last Saturday in September, and it was still eighty-nine degrees outside on the Riverwalk. You could feel it go. The hum in the ceiling stopped, and inside of ten minutes the room started getting heavy. Women were fanning themselves with the programs. The cake was starting to lean.

The hall manager came hurrying over to the head table, a young guy with a clipboard and sweat on his lip.

"I'm so sorry, folks. The unit's down. I've got a call in to our service company, but they say it might be two hours on a Saturday night."

"Two hours?" Kiana looked like she might cry. "My cake."

Ted Lambert leaned back in his chair. "Hey, Alana. Isn't your husband an AC guy? Maybe he brought his toolbox." He did the air quotes with two fingers. "'Professional.'"

People laughed again.

Alana didn't. She just looked at me with her mouth tight, like she wanted me to disappear into the floor.

I stood up and took my jacket off and hung it on my chair.

"Where's your roof access?" I asked the hall manager.

"Sir, you don't have to—"

"Where is it?"

He showed me. It was a pull-down ladder in a storage closet off the kitchen. I keep a small tool bag behind the seat of my truck, every truck I've ever owned, and I went out to the lot and got it. It took me four minutes to get up on that roof in my rented shoes, and about six more to find the problem. The contactor on the condensing unit was pitted and stuck. I had a spare in my bag. Most guys in my trade do.

When I came back down the ladder, the hum was back in the ceiling and cold air was coming out of the vents. A cheer went up in the hall. Kiana ran over and hugged me, and I got sweat on her dress, and she didn't care.

"You saved my cake," she said.

"Your cake saved itself. I just fixed a contactor."

When I got back to the head table, the best man handed me the microphone. Somebody had decided it was time for my toast.

I'd written it on an index card that morning. I didn't need the card.

"Kiana," I said, "I've known you since you were thirteen and you had braces and a crush on a boy named Trevon who couldn't spell his own name."

People laughed. Real laughs this time.

"Your daddy can't be here. So I'll tell you what he told me the day I married your sister. He said, 'Isaiah, a marriage is a lot like a house. You don't see the work that keeps it standing. You only notice it when it stops.'" I raised my glass. "To Kiana and Marcel. Keep the air running."

They clapped. Kiana cried. Marcel shook my hand.

Alana didn't look at me. She was looking down the table at Ted Lambert, and Ted was looking back.

Around ten o'clock, the band played "September," and everybody over forty got up to dance. I stayed at the table. Ted asked Alana to dance. She said yes. They danced through Earth, Wind and Fire and then through a slow Anita Baker song, and at the end of it his hand was flat on her lower back, and he leaned down and said something in her ear that made her close her eyes.

Kiana sat down next to me in her wedding dress, holding her shoes in one hand.

"Isaiah," she said quietly. "Can I tell you something? And you won't be mad at me?"

"I can't promise that till I hear it."

She watched her sister on the dance floor.

"Ted told the whole billing department Friday that we're moving offices. In January. Out of your Broadway building and over to Riverwalk Commons, that new place by the river." She twisted her shoes in her hands. "He said the landlord won't renew our lease. He said the landlord's being impossible."

I didn't say anything. I'd been the landlord at 1140 Broadway since 2016. I'd bought it after servicing its chillers for eleven years, when the old owner wanted to retire and couldn't find a buyer. Every lease, every renewal, every key went through my property manager. And my property manager had sent Valley Claims Group a renewal offer three times since August, at the same rent they were already paying.

"And Alana," Kiana said. "She says once the move's done, Ted's making her vice president. She told me in the bathroom an hour ago. She said, 'Riverwalk is my promotion, baby sister.'"

"Riverwalk," I said.

"Isaiah, what's going on? You own that building. Doesn't she know you own that building?"

I looked across the hall at my wife with Ted Lambert's hand on her back.

"No," I said. "She's never asked."

The next morning was Sunday, and I called Lucius Garvin at seven.

Lucius has managed my building for nine years. He's sixty-three, wears a bow tie on weekdays and a Braves cap on weekends, and he keeps every piece of paper that's ever crossed his desk in a three-ring binder with tabs. He met me in the lobby of 1140 Broadway with two coffees and the Valley Claims binder already under his arm.

"You're not going to like this," he said.

"Start at the beginning."

We sat down at the guard desk in the lobby. Lucius opened the binder.

"August fourth. Renewal offer, five years, same rent as now, twenty-six dollars a square foot. Sent to Mr. Theodore Lambert, because he's the designated lease contact on their file. No reply." He turned the tab. "September second. Second letter. Same offer, plus we'd repaint all three floors for free. No reply. October first, third letter, sent certified mail. Signed for by T. Lambert. No reply."

"He signed for it."

"Signed his own name." Lucius tapped the green card. "So I did what I'd do with any tenant. I called their finance office Friday afternoon and left a message for their CFO, Mrs. Della Strickland. I said we'd been trying to reach them about renewal and hadn't heard a word, and could she call me."

"And?"

"And she called me back at nine o'clock last night. On a Saturday. She sounded confused. She said their VP of sales told their board in August that the landlord flatly refused to renew. She said they're voting Monday morning on a lease at Riverwalk Commons." He took a sip of coffee. "Isaiah, I'm sorry. I didn't know it was going to get personal. She asked who the owner was, for the record. I told her. Whitlow Properties LLC, Isaiah Whitlow, managing member. And then she got real quiet and said, 'Is that any relation to our Alana Whitlow?'"

"What'd you say?"

"I said I'd have to ask the owner." He looked at me over his reading glasses. "So I'm asking the owner."

"Tell her the truth," I said. "Every word."

The elevator dinged behind us. The doors opened, and Elroy Haywood stepped out, finishing his overnight shift.

Elroy is sixty-eight, retired Army, three tours, and he's guarded my building at night for six years. He walks every floor twice a shift, and he writes every after-hours sign-in in a green logbook in handwriting so neat it could be printed. He saw me and stopped short.

"Mr. Whitlow. On a Sunday?"

"Morning, Elroy."

He looked at Lucius, and then at me, and something in his face changed. He set his thermos down on the guard desk very slowly.

"Sir," he said. "I've been trying to figure out how to say something to you for about four months."

"Then say it."

He went behind the desk and came back with the green logbook. It was thick, one of those ledger books from the office supply store. He opened it to a page marked with a paper clip.

"Ninth floor," he said. "Valley Claims. After-hours sign-ins. Policy is, anybody in the building after eight p.m. signs in with me, name and floor and time out." He ran his finger down the page. "T. Lambert, nine. A. Whitlow, nine. Tuesday. T. Lambert, nine. A. Whitlow, nine. Thursday."

He turned the page. And the next one. Same two names, Tuesdays and Thursdays, all the way back to May.

"Always the two of them," Elroy said. "Never anybody else from that floor. Never earlier than nine, never later than eleven." He cleared his throat. "Back in June, Mr. Lambert handed me a fifty-dollar bill and told me I didn't need to bother writing his name down anymore. Said it was a hassle."

"What'd you do?"

"I gave him the fifty back and wrote his name down." Elroy looked me in the eye. "It's your building, sir. It's your log. I wasn't going to lie in your log."

I read the pages for a long time. I'd spent most of those Tuesdays and Thursdays at the Fort Moore hospital project on a twelve-hour shift, which Alana knew, because she'd made a point of asking every Monday what my week looked like.

My phone rang. It was a number I didn't have saved. I answered it.

"Mr. Whitlow? This is Della Strickland. I'm the chief financial officer at Valley Claims Group. Your property manager gave me this number. I hope that's all right."

"It's all right."

"I'm going to be honest with you, because I've had a very long night." She took a breath. "I'm going into the office this afternoon. I've asked your wife to come in at a quarter after two. I'd very much like you to be there at two. I have some questions about my lease, and I think I'm going to have some more questions after that."

I looked at the green logbook open on the guard desk.

"I'll be there at two," I said. "And Mrs. Strickland, I'll bring my own paperwork."

Valley Claims Group takes up the seventh, eighth and ninth floors of 1140 Broadway. A hundred and sixty people who process medical bills for hospitals in three states. On a Sunday afternoon the floors were dark except for one corner office on the eighth, where the blinds were open and a woman with silver hair cut short as a man's was sitting behind a desk covered in paper.

Della Strickland stood up when I knocked on the glass. She was about sixty, wore reading glasses on top of her head and had the handshake of someone who'd fired a lot of people and never enjoyed it.

"Mr. Whitlow."

"Isaiah."

"Isaiah. Sit down. Coffee? It's terrible."

"Then no, thank you."

That almost got a smile out of her. She sat back down and turned her monitor toward me.

"Let me show you what I've got, and then you show me what you've got." She clicked. "August twelfth. A memo to our board, signed by Ted Lambert, vice president of sales, and co-signed by Alana Whitlow, director of sales. Quote: 'After multiple attempts, the current landlord at 1140 Broadway has declined to offer renewal terms. We recommend relocation to Riverwalk Commons.' End quote." She clicked again. "Riverwalk Commons wants thirty-four dollars a foot. We pay you twenty-six. On sixty thousand square feet, that's four hundred and eighty thousand more a year, for ten years."

"Plus moving costs."

"Plus moving costs. Plus downtime. I had it figured at about five and a half million dollars over the life of the lease." She pushed her glasses down onto her nose. "And I signed off on it. Because my VP of sales told my board the landlord refused. Because my director of sales co-signed it. And because I never thought to pick up the phone and call the landlord myself, which is something I'm going to think about for a long time."

I opened Lucius's binder on her desk and turned it around so she could read it.

"August fourth," I said. "September second. October first, certified. Same rent. Five years. We offered to repaint all three floors. Every letter went to Ted Lambert. He signed the green card on the last one himself."

She read all three letters. She looked at Ted's signature on the certified mail card for a long moment. Then she took her reading glasses off and set them down very carefully, like they might break.

"He hid them," she said.

"He hid them."

"Why?"

"I've got a guess. Who owns Riverwalk Commons?"

She turned back to her screen and typed. It took about thirty seconds.

"Pitts Development Group. Garland Pitts, president." She frowned. "That name's familiar."

"It ought to be. Garland Pitts is married to Ted Lambert's sister. I've known Garland twenty years. I put the units in his first strip mall in 2004."

Della sat very still. Then she picked up her desk phone, dialed an extension, and said, "It's Della. I need IT to pull Ted Lambert's company email for the last six months, and anything with the words Pitts or Riverwalk in it. Yes, on a Sunday. Yes, I'm authorizing it. Company server, company policy. Thank you."

She hung up and looked at me.

"Section 12 of our employee handbook," she said. "Every email on our server belongs to the company. Ted signed that handbook. So did your wife."

There was a soft knock on the glass door.

Alana was standing outside it in a cream-colored blouse and the gold earrings I'd given her for our twentieth anniversary. She had her work bag on her shoulder and a smile ready for Della, the six-figure smile.

Then she saw me.

The smile didn't fall off her face so much as slide off it, slow, like ice off a windshield.

Della waved her in. "Come in, Alana. Close the door. Have a seat."

Alana came in. She didn't sit down.

"Isaiah, what are you doing here?"

"Mrs. Strickland asked me to come."

"Why on earth would Della ask you to—"

"Alana," Della said. "Sit down, please. I have a question for you, and I need a straight answer."

Alana sat on the edge of the chair next to mine like the seat was hot.

"Our landlord's property manager called me Friday," Della said. "And again last night. He asked me why we'd never answered three renewal offers. Then he told me who owns this building." She folded her hands on the desk. "Alana, why was my landlord asking about your husband? Your husband, who owns the building?"

Alana laughed. It was a short, high laugh, like a hiccup.

"That's not, that can't be right. Isaiah fixes air conditioners. He has a little service company. Eleven trucks."

"Eleven trucks," I said. "And one building."

She turned in her chair and stared at me. I watched her try to put it together and fail, and try again.

"Since when?"

"March of 2016. I bought it from Raymond Callaway when he retired. You were at the closing dinner. You said the steak was too salty."

"You said it was a business thing!"

"It was a business thing. Whitlow Properties LLC. I told you I bought a building. You said, 'That's nice, honey,' and asked me if we could finally redo the guest bathroom."

"You never said this building!"

"You never asked which one. Alana, you've worked on the ninth floor of this building for six years. My name is on a brass plaque in the lobby next to the elevators. It says 'Whitlow Properties, Managing Agent.' You walk past it every morning."

She opened her mouth and closed it.

"I thought that was a different Whitlow," she said finally, very quietly.

"How many Whitlows you know in Columbus?"

Della let that sit. Then she slid the August memo across the desk.

"Alana. You co-signed this. It says the landlord declined to offer renewal terms after multiple attempts. Did you see any of those attempts?"

"Ted handled the lease. I just co-signed because he asked me to. I'm the director of sales. It was a sales memo."

"It was a lease memo."

"Ted said the landlord was being impossible."

"The landlord," I said, "offered to repaint your office for free."

Alana's eyes went to Lucius's binder, open on the desk, and to the certified mail card with Ted's signature on it. I saw her read the date.

"Ted said," she started, and then stopped. "Ted told me we'd get Riverwalk because it was better space. That's all. That's all I knew."

"Did he tell you who owned Riverwalk?" Della asked.

"No."

"Garland Pitts. Ted's brother-in-law."

Alana looked at the floor. Della's desk phone buzzed. She picked it up, listened, said "Send them to me now, please," and hung up. Her computer chimed with an email. She opened it, read for a minute, and her mouth got thin.

"Well," she said. "Here's an email from Garland Pitts to Ted Lambert, dated July nineteenth. Subject line, 'your four percent.' Quote: 'As discussed, four percent of first-year rent as a leasing consultation fee, paid to TL Advisory, on execution.' End quote." She looked up at Alana. "Four percent of first-year rent at Riverwalk is about eighty-one thousand dollars."

"I didn't know about that."

"I believe you," Della said. "On that one."

Alana flinched like Della had slapped her. Then she stood up, very straight, and picked up her bag.

"I'm not going to sit here and be treated like a criminal on a Sunday because my husband decided to play landlord," she said. "I'll talk to Ted. There's an explanation."

"There's a board meeting at nine tomorrow," Della said. "Ted's scheduled to sign the Riverwalk lease at eight-thirty, before the board even votes, because he told me Garland needed it signed by Monday or the space goes to somebody else. I'd strongly suggest you don't call Ted tonight."

"You can't tell me who I can call."

"No," Della agreed. "I can't."

Alana walked out. The glass door swung shut behind her, and we heard her heels all the way down the dark hallway to the elevator.

Della sat back in her chair and looked at me for a long time.

"Isaiah," she said. "Is there something else I should know? Something about Ted and your wife that isn't about real estate?"

I put my hand on the green logbook in my lap. I didn't open it.

"Tomorrow," I said. "There's a man who can tell you better than I can."

Alana didn't call Ted from the car. She called him from our kitchen, with the door half open, and she didn't bother to lower her voice. I was out on the back porch fixing the hinge on the screen door that she'd been asking me about since May. I heard every word.

"He owns it, Ted. The whole building. Della knows everything about the letters. She's got Garland's email. The four percent." A pause. "No, I didn't tell her anything. What would I tell her? I didn't know about the four percent, Ted. You never told me about any four percent."

Another pause, longer.

"Fine. Fine. Eight-thirty. I'll be there. But you'd better have an explanation for Della, because I am not going down for your brother-in-law."

She hung up. She came out onto the porch and stood in the doorway with her arms crossed.

"How long have you known?" she said.

"Known what?"

"Don't. Don't play dumb. You've been playing dumb for nine years, and you're not going to play dumb with me tonight."

"I've known about the letters since this morning."

"And Ted?"

I set the screwdriver down on the porch rail.

"What about Ted, Alana?"

She looked at me. I looked back. The ceiling fan on the porch turned slowly over us.

"Ted's my boss," she said.

"He's your boss on Tuesdays and Thursdays between nine and eleven at night too?"

That one landed. I watched it land. She actually took a step back into the doorway.

"Who told you that?"

"The building told me."

"What is that supposed to mean? You put cameras in your building? You've been spying on me?"

"No cameras. A man with a pen. Every person in that building after eight p.m. signs a log. You signed it, Alana. Your own name in your own handwriting, every Tuesday and every Thursday since May." I picked the screwdriver back up. "The air always tells you the truth before the people do."

"Oh, don't you dare quote your little sayings at me." Her voice went up. "You want to talk about truth? You hid a building from me for nine years. You let me walk past your name every morning like a fool. You let me tell everybody at Kiana's wedding that you fix air conditioners, and you sat there and smiled. You knew. You let me humiliate myself."

"You humiliated me, Alana. Into a microphone."

"Because you let me! Because you're always so quiet and so humble and so, so small. Do you know what it's like being married to a man who never wants anything? Ted wants things. Ted has plans. Ted was going to make me a vice president."

"With Garland Pitts's money."

"With his own talent!"

"Then why'd he hide three letters?"

She didn't have an answer for that. She stood in the porch doorway breathing hard, and the screen door I'd just fixed swung shut on its new hinge without a sound.

"I want a divorce," she said.

"Okay."

"And I want half of that building. It's marital property. I looked it up on the way home. Georgia. You bought it while we were married."

"You're right," I said. "It is marital. My lawyer will tell yours the same thing. You'll get your fair share of what it's worth, and I won't fight you on that. I don't need to win the building, Alana. I never did."

She stared at me like I'd spoken another language.

"Then what do you want?"

I thought about it. I thought about the head table, and the microphone, and the hundred and eighty people looking down at their plates.

"I want to be there at eight-thirty," I said.

I didn't sleep much. Around eleven, I called the one lawyer I trust, Marguerite Oakes, who did the closing on the building in 2016 and has done every contract for Whitlow Mechanical since. She picked up on the second ring, because she's seventy-one and says she quit sleeping in the Carter administration.

"Isaiah. It's Sunday night. Somebody had better be dead."

"Nobody's dead. My wife wants a divorce."

There was a pause on the line. "Well. I'm sorry. Are you?"

"I'm a lot of things right now, Marguerite."

"Fair enough." I heard her pick up a pen. "Tell me what you need."

I told her. The building. The wedding. The letters. The logbook. She listened all the way through without saying a word, which is how I knew she was taking it seriously.

"The building's marital," she said when I was done. "Bought during the marriage. Georgia's equitable distribution, so she'll get a fair share of what it's worth. Not half automatically, but a fair share. Can you live with that?"

"I can live with that."

"Good. Then don't do anything stupid tomorrow. Don't threaten anybody. Don't use that building to punish her company. Don't raise their rent. Don't cut off their air. A judge would look at that and see a spiteful man, and you're not one." She paused. "You're not, are you?"

"No, ma'am."

"Then go in there and tell the truth and let the paper do the work. My granddaddy was a carpenter. He used to say you let the level tell you if the wall is straight. You don't argue with the level."

At a quarter to eight the next morning, I was standing in the lobby of 1140 Broadway next to the brass plaque with my name on it, waiting for Della, when the front doors opened and Kiana came in.

She was still tan from the reception, still had a little bit of glitter in her hair from the send-off, and she was wearing her work lanyard. She worked in billing on the seventh floor. Her honeymoon didn't start until Thursday.

"Isaiah." She grabbed my arm. "She called me at midnight. Alana. She was crying so hard I could barely understand her."

"What'd she say?"

"She said Ted called her back after she hung up on him. She said Ted told her if Della pushes, he's going to say the whole thing was Alana's idea. That she's the one who wanted out of your building so you wouldn't find out about them. That she wrote the memo and he just signed it." Kiana's grip got tighter. "Isaiah, he's going to put it all on her. He's going to say she was hiding from you."

I looked up at the plaque. Whitlow Properties, Managing Agent.

"She co-signed it, Kiana," I said. "Her name is on it."

"I know. I know she did. And she said something awful about you at my wedding, and I'm still mad at her about it, and I'll be mad at her at Christmas." Kiana's eyes filled up. "But she's my sister. And Ted Lambert is a snake. Please don't let him put it all on her."

The elevator dinged. Della Strickland came out of it, saw the two of us, and raised an eyebrow.

"Mrs. Hart," she said. "I didn't know you were part of this."

"I'm not," Kiana said quickly. "I'm just the bride."

"Then go on up to billing, honey," Della said. "This one's going to be loud."

Kiana squeezed my arm once more and went to the elevator. The doors closed on her worried face.

Della looked at me. "Did she tell you something I should know?"

"She told me Ted's going to blame my wife for everything."

Della pushed the elevator button again. "Then let's go see," she said, "if Ted can do math."

The big conference room at Valley Claims is on the ninth floor, at the end of the hall, with windows that look across Broadway at the old Springer Opera House. At twenty after eight on Monday morning, it had a pitcher of ice water, a tray of pastries from the bakery downstairs and a lease on the table with yellow sign-here flags sticking out of it like little tongues.

Garland Pitts was already there. He was sixty, heavyset, in a golf shirt with his company's logo on it, and when I walked in behind Della Strickland he stood up so fast his chair rolled into the wall.

"Isaiah? What are you doing here?"

"Morning, Garland."

"I didn't know you knew these folks."

"I'm their landlord," I said. "For nine more years, if Della wants me."

Garland looked at Della. Then at the lease on the table. Then at the door, where his brother-in-law was just walking in with a leather folder and a coffee from the place on the corner, laughing at something on his phone.

Ted Lambert saw me, and the laugh stopped in the middle.

Alana came in right behind him. She'd changed clothes since last night, a navy suit, hair pulled back tight. She didn't look at me. She sat down at the far end of the table and put her hands flat in front of her, like she was holding the table down.

Lucius Garvin came in last, with his binder. And behind Lucius, still in his guard's uniform with his thermos in one hand, came Elroy Haywood. He'd come back after going home to sleep for three hours. I'd asked him to.

"Della," Ted said. He set his coffee down and put on a big friendly voice. "Didn't know we were having company. We're just doing the signing before the board meeting. Garland's got a tight window on that space."

"Sit down, Ted."

"Sure. Sure." He sat. He pushed his sunglasses up on his head. "So what's this? Isaiah, good to see you. Great job on the AC Saturday night. Real lifesaver."

"Thanks, Ted."

"So what brings you to—"

"I own the building," I said.

Ted laughed. Nobody laughed with him.

"Right," he said. "Okay. Good one."

Della slid a copy of Lucius's three letters down the table to him. They stopped in front of his coffee cup.

"August fourth," she said. "September second. October first. Three renewal offers from our landlord, at our current rent. All addressed to you. You signed for the last one personally. Ted, why did you tell my board in August that the landlord refused to renew?"

Ted looked at the letters. He looked at them for a long time.

"I don't remember getting these," he said.

"Your signature's on the green card."

"Somebody else must've signed for me. Our mail room—"

"Your mail room is three women who've worked here for a combined sixty years," Della said. "I asked them this morning. None of them signed it. They said you came down personally on October second and asked whether anything had come certified for you."

Ted's ears were turning red.

"Okay. Okay, look. Maybe I got the letters. I'd have to check. But the terms weren't good for us, Della. That building's old. The HVAC's terrible."

"The HVAC," I said, "is maintained by Whitlow Mechanical. On a contract with the building. You've never put in a single service ticket in six years. I checked last night."

He turned to me. "You don't get a say in this."

"Sure he does," Della said. "He's the landlord. Ted, are you saying the terms weren't good? Same rent, five years, and he'd repaint all three floors?"

"I'm saying I made a judgment call."

"A judgment call to pay thirty-four dollars a foot instead of twenty-six?"

"Riverwalk's better space."

"Riverwalk's your sister's husband's space," I said.

Ted stood up. "Okay, this is ridiculous. Garland and I have a family relationship, sure, but Garland gave us the best deal in town—"

"Ted." Garland's voice was flat. He was still standing over by the wall where his chair had rolled. "Sit down."

"Garland, I'm handling this."

"No, you're not. Sit down." Garland looked at Della. "Ma'am, I want to say something before this goes any further. Ted told me Valley Claims couldn't renew at Broadway. He said the landlord was a stubborn old man who wouldn't return calls. I had no idea the landlord was Isaiah Whitlow. If I'd known that, I'd have picked up the phone myself. Isaiah put the air in my first strip mall in 2004. He returns calls."

"And the four percent?" Della said.

Garland's face got red. He didn't answer right away.

Della turned her laptop around and set it in the middle of the table, so everybody could read the screen. Garland's email from July nineteenth. Your four percent. TL Advisory. On execution.

"That's a consultation fee," Ted said quickly. "It's standard. TL Advisory is my consulting company. I do consulting on the side. It's allowed."

"Section 9 of the handbook," Della said. "Any outside compensation related to a company transaction must be disclosed to the CFO in writing. Did you disclose it?"

"I was going to."

"After you signed a ten-year lease on the company's behalf, with your brother-in-law, that cost this company five and a half million dollars more than staying put?"

"That's not fair. You're making it sound—"

"I'm making it sound like what it is."

Ted looked down the table at Alana. It was a quick look, a man looking for help.

Alana didn't give it. She was staring at the laptop screen like it was a snake.

"You told me it was just better space," she said to him. Her voice was very quiet. "You told me the landlord was impossible. You said I'd be vice president after the move."

"Alana, not now."

"You let me co-sign a memo, Ted. My name is on that memo."

"Because you wanted that title more than anything, babe. I didn't hear you complaining."

Babe.

The word sat in the middle of the conference room table like a dropped glass. Della heard it. Garland heard it. Lucius stopped turning pages in his binder.

Alana went very still.

"Mr. Haywood," Della said, without taking her eyes off Ted. "Mr. Whitlow told me yesterday there was a man who could tell me something better than he could. I assume that's you."

Elroy set his thermos down and stood up straight, the way a man does when he's spent twenty years in the Army.

"Yes, ma'am."

"What can you tell me?"

He opened the green logbook and laid it on the table, open to the June page with the paper clip.

"Building policy, ma'am, anybody on any floor after eight p.m. signs in with me. Since May fourteenth, two people from this company have signed in after hours on the ninth floor, every Tuesday and every Thursday. Always the same two. Mr. Lambert and Mrs. Whitlow. Always between nine and eleven." He paused. "In June, Mr. Lambert offered me fifty dollars to stop writing his name down. I didn't take it."

Ted's face had gone a deep, blotchy red. "That's a lie."

"No, sir," Elroy said. "That's my handwriting."

Della closed her laptop.

She sat there for a second with both hands on the lid. Then she looked down the table at Ted Lambert, and when she spoke, her voice was calm and very tired.

"Ted, you're terminated for cause, effective now. You hid three lease offers from your employer. You took an undisclosed fee from a vendor on a transaction you negotiated for this company. And you tried to bribe a security officer in our landlord's building to falsify his records. Security will walk you to your car. Your badge, please."

"You can't do this. I have a contract."

"Your contract has a for-cause clause. I wrote it." She held out her hand. "Badge."

Ted looked at Garland. Garland looked out the window.

"Don't look at me, Ted," Garland said. "I'm going to have to explain this to your sister tonight. You're on your own."

Ted unclipped his badge and threw it on the table. It slid across and bumped Lucius's binder. Then he grabbed his leather folder and his coffee and walked out, and the door closed slowly behind him on its hydraulic arm. I'd adjusted that arm myself in 2019.

Alana was crying now. Not loud. Just tears running down and dripping onto her navy jacket, and she didn't wipe them.

"Della," she said. "I didn't know about the money. I swear I didn't."

"I believe you didn't know about the money, Alana. I told you that yesterday." Della took off her reading glasses. "But you co-signed a memo to my board that said our landlord refused to renew, when you had no idea whether that was true, because a man promised you a title. And that landlord was your own husband. I can't keep a director of sales who signs whatever she's handed." She folded the glasses. "I'm sorry. Effective today, you're terminated. You'll get two weeks' severance. That's more than Ted's getting."

Alana turned to me. Her face was wet and blotchy, and for a second she looked like the girl I'd married at twenty-four, scared of everything.

"Isaiah," she said. "Please. Tell her. Tell her I'm good at my job. You know I am. One call from you. You're the landlord. She'll listen to you."

I thought about it. I want to be honest. I thought about it.

"Alana," I said. "Saturday night, a man at the head table asked what I do. You told a hundred and eighty people I fix air conditioners. You were right. I do. I fix what's broken." I shook my head. "I can't fix this one."

She stared at me. Then she picked up her bag, and she walked out the way Ted had, and the door closed slowly behind her too.

The room was quiet. Garland Pitts cleared his throat.

"Isaiah," he said. "I'm sorry. I'll tear up the Riverwalk paper myself. I never wanted business like this."

"I know you didn't, Garland."

Della reached under the stack of yellow-flagged Riverwalk pages and pulled out a different document. A plain white one. It was the five-year renewal Lucius had sent on August fourth, the one Ted had stuck in a drawer.

"Isaiah," she said. "Is this offer still good? Same rent? Paint?"

"Same rent. Paint."

She signed it on the last page and slid it across the table to me, along with her pen.

I uncapped the pen, signed my name on the landlord's line, and handed the lease back to her across the table in my own building.

"Welcome home, Della," I said. "Call me if the air ever quits."
