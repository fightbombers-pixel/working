"Clueless is carving. Twenty minutes, garage. Perfect cover, like always."

That was my wife's text, glowing on a cheap black flip phone I had never seen in my life, at four in the afternoon on the first Sunday of November, while fourteen members of my family sat in my living room in Billings, Montana watching the Broncos lose. I'd gone out to the garage for two more folding chairs. The phone was buzzing inside my late father's old survey tripod case, the one I hadn't opened since we buried him.

Under her text was the answer, from a number with no name, sent eleven seconds later.

"Fifteen years and he still hasn't figured it out. Coming. Bring the good wine."

I knew who it was before I scrolled up. I knew it from the "Coming." My brother Ramon has signed every text he's sent since 2006 with that one word, like a man who thinks he's a cowboy in a movie.

Fifteen years. Our anniversary had been three weeks earlier. I'd bought Dana a $340 necklace from a jeweler on Grand Avenue, and she'd worn it to dinner, and she was wearing it right now in my living room, passing the dip to my mother.

I stood there in the cold garage with the phone in my hand. Then I took my own phone out of my coat, photographed the screen, closed the flip phone, set it back in the case exactly the way it had been lying, face down, under the strap, and picked up two folding chairs.

I walked back into the house, set the chairs down by the TV, went to the kitchen, picked up the carving knife and started carving the roast like a man who didn't have a single thing on his mind.

Twenty-five days later, my brother would be reading his own words out loud at our mother's Thanksgiving table, and his hands would be shaking so hard the paper rattled.

What neither of them knew was that I'd seen what else was in that tripod case, folded twice and tucked behind the phone. One corner was sticking out, and on that corner, in capital letters, were the words QUITCLAIM DEED.

My name is Leland Ochoa. I'm fifty-two, I've been a land surveyor for Yellowstone County for twenty-four years, and my father was a surveyor before me. There's one rule he drilled into me before I could drive. You never trust a corner pin you didn't find yourself. You dig until you hit iron. Hold on to that.

"Lee, you're butchering it," my mother called from the couch. "Thin slices. Thin! Your father cut it like paper."

"Yes, Mom."

"And where's Ramon? He said he was getting more ice."

"Probably on the phone with a customer," my Uncle Tito said from the recliner, where he'd been asleep since kickoff. "That boy sells tires in his sleep."

"He sells them in church," my Aunt Pilar said. "Father Benny caught him handing out coupons after Mass."

"Garage, maybe," Dana said, and she smiled at me across the kitchen island, a nice warm smile, and salted her plate of roast before she'd tasted a bite, the way she has every single night for fifteen years.

I carved. I put the slices on the platter. And when everybody had a plate, I picked up my beer and tapped it with my fork.

"To family," I said.

Fourteen people raised their glasses. My mother raised her iced tea. Dana raised the good wine.

Ramon came in through the garage door five minutes later with a bag of ice and a big laugh about the Broncos' defense, and he laughed too loud at his own joke, the way he always does, and nobody else laughed.

He didn't look at Dana. Dana didn't look at him. They'd had fifteen years of practice.

I waited until the fourth quarter. Then I said I needed some air, and I went back out to the garage, and I knelt down by the tripod case, and I unbuckled the strap.

I'd just touched the folded deed when the garage door from the house opened behind me.

"Need a hand, big brother?"

I didn't jump. Twenty-four years of standing on highway shoulders with semis going by at seventy teaches you not to jump. I just kept my hand where it was, on the strap, and looked up at him.

Ramon was leaning in the doorway with a beer. He's forty-eight, four years younger than me, with our father's shoulders and our mother's eyelashes, and he's been the favorite since the day he was born. He runs Ochoa Tire and Lube on King Avenue, which Dad helped him buy, and he talks to everybody like he's selling them a set of all-seasons.

"Dad's old case," he said. "What are you doing with that?"

"Thinking about selling it. Somebody on Facebook Marketplace wants a vintage tripod."

"You'd sell Dad's tripod?"

"I haven't opened it since the funeral, Ramon. It's just sitting out here collecting dust."

His eyes went to the case. Just for a second. Then he laughed, too loud.

"Don't sell it, man. Mom would kill you. Come on, it's the two-minute warning."

"Right behind you."

He waited. He actually stood there in the doorway and waited, so I buckled the strap, and stood up, and walked past him into the house. He shut the garage door behind us like a man shutting a barn on a horse.

I watched the rest of that game without seeing a single play.

You'd think a man would explode. Walk back into that living room and turn the coffee table over. I thought about it. I thought about it the way you think about stepping off a cliff when you're standing at the edge, just because it's there. But my father's voice was in my head, the same as it was on every job I ever ran. You never trust a corner you didn't dig up yourself.

I had one text. I didn't have the iron yet.

That night, Dana went to bed at ten like always. I said I had some plats to finish. At a little after midnight, I went out to the garage in my socks, unbuckled Dad's case, and took out the flip phone.

It wanted a four-digit code.

I sat on an overturned bucket in the cold and thought about it. Dana uses her birthday for everything, the ATM, the garage door, the Netflix. I tried it. It buzzed at me. I tried Ramon's birthday. Nothing. I had one more try before it locked, and I sat there for a long time, my breath fogging up, and then I typed in the date of my own wedding.

One, zero, one, two. October twelfth. The one date in the world she knew I'd never think to try, because it was mine.

It opened.

I sat on that bucket for three hours that first night. I came back the next night, and the night after that. I didn't take the phone. I didn't copy anything off it. I took pictures of the screen with my own phone, one at a time, three hundred and eleven of them, while my feet went numb and the furnace kicked on and off behind me.

The thread went back to the day they bought the phone, four years ago. But they talked about the old days all the time, the way people do when they think they got away with something. That's how I learned about my wedding day.

"Remember the morning of his wedding?" Dana had written, two Octobers ago. "The bridal suite at the Northern. You came up to give me the 'family welcome.'"

"Best morning of my life," Ramon wrote back. "Watching you walk down that aisle an hour later in that dress. Knowing."

"I married the wrong brother."

"You married the right one for the job. He's the perfect cover. Nobody looks at the wife of the nicest guy in Billings."

And then, a year later, after a Christmas at my mother's: "Clueless gave me a card again. He writes the same thing every year. 'To my favorite person.' I almost felt bad."

"Don't. He's Clueless. That's what makes it work."

I keep every card anybody's ever given me. They're in a shoebox in my closet. Every one Dana gave me in fifteen years says the same thing, too. "Love you, D." I'd never noticed that before.

On the third night, I scrolled to the newest texts, the ones from the last few weeks, and I stopped being sad and started being something else.

"Ponderosa called again," Ramon had written. "They'll do two point one for the whole ranch. But they want both signatures by Dec 1 or they walk."

"He'll never sell. That ranch is his dad's whole life."

"He doesn't have to sell. He has to sign one paper that gives me his half. Then I sell. Mom asks him at Thanksgiving, it's for her, it's to keep things simple for the estate, he signs. He always does what Mom asks."

"And then?"

"And then a million and change, and you leave him, and we go to Arizona. Clueless gets to keep his tripod."

"After he signs at Thanksgiving, I tell him. Not before."

I put the phone back in the case. I buckled the strap. I went inside and stood at the kitchen sink and washed my hands for a long time, longer than a man needs to, and then I sat down at the kitchen table and broke down crying, alone, at three in the morning, with my face in a dish towel so Dana wouldn't hear me upstairs.

Then I made a pot of coffee and waited for it to be eight o'clock.

Ruth Canfield's office is above a western wear store on Montana Avenue. She's sixty, she wears turquoise rings on every finger, and she did my father's will, which is why she knew more about the ranch than I did.

"Let me see if I've got it," she said, turning my phone slowly with one ringed finger, one photo at a time. "Your wife and your brother. Since the morning of your wedding. A secret phone, in your garage, in your father's tripod case. And a plan for your mother to get you to sign a quitclaim deed at Thanksgiving dinner."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Lee, I've practiced law in this town for thirty-four years. I've never been this offended on a Wednesday." She set the phone down. "First, the boring part. Montana is a no-fault state. The cheating, by itself, won't change much in a divorce. A judge won't care that she called you Clueless. I care. A judge won't."

"I figured."

"Second, did you take the phone?"

"No. I took pictures of it. It's in my garage. In my father's case. Which Dad left to me."

"Good. Don't take it. Don't hide it. Don't text anybody from it. You're a man who looked in his own property and found something. Keep it that way." She opened a drawer and pulled out a thick file with my father's name on it. "Third, the ranch. Your father left the Ochoa place to you and Ramon, half and half. Seven hundred acres on the Clarks Fork. Neither one of you can sell the whole thing without the other one signing."

"That's what the deed's for."

"A quitclaim deed gives away whatever you've got. Your half, for ten dollars and love and affection, to your brother. Sign that, and he owns all seven hundred acres, and he can sell to anybody he wants on December second." She tapped the file. "What's Ponderosa?"

"I was hoping you'd know."

Ruth turned to her computer, typed for a minute, and her mouth went thin.

"Ponderosa Land Company. Developer out of Bozeman. Subdivisions. And look at this." She turned the screen. "Two weeks ago, your brother signed an option agreement with Ponderosa on the Ochoa ranch and recorded a notice of it with the county. The whole ranch. All seven hundred acres. He signed it as if he already owned your half."

"He can do that?"

"He can sign anything he wants. He can't deliver it. Not without you." She sat back. "Lee, an option agreement means Ponderosa paid him money to hold the deal. Usually real money. If he can't deliver on December first, he owes that money back, and maybe more. Your brother isn't just in love with your wife. Your brother is in a hole."

"How deep?"

"That," Ruth said, "is a question for somebody who reads his bank statements."

I didn't have to wait long to find out who that was.

The following Monday, a little before five, I was in my office at the county building finishing up a plat for a subdivision out in Lockwood when the receptionist buzzed me.

"Lee? There's a Lucy Ochoa here to see you. She says she's your sister-in-law."

Lucy is forty-six. She's a teller at the First Interstate branch on Grand, and she keeps the books for Ramon's tire shop on the side, because Ramon can't add without a calculator and a prayer. She's always been the quiet one at family dinners, the one who clears the table before anybody asks.

She came into my office in her bank blazer and shut the door behind her, and she didn't sit down.

"Lee. I'm just going to ask you, because I'll lose my nerve. Do you know?"

"Know what, Lucy?"

"Don't." Her voice cracked. "Don't do that to me. I'm not stupid. I keep his books. There's a charge on his business card every month for twenty-five dollars. A prepaid phone company. For four years. He doesn't have a second phone, Lee. Not one I've ever seen."

I looked at her for a long moment. Then I opened the photos on my phone, scrolled down to one I'd picked out on purpose, one of the gentler ones, and turned it around.

She read it standing up. Then she sat down after all, in the chair across from my desk, with her bank blazer still buttoned.

"Dana," she said.

"Since my wedding."

"Since your wedding." She laughed, a horrible little laugh. "I was a bridesmaid at your wedding. I held her bouquet while she signed the license."

We sat there for a while, the two of us, in my little county office with the plats on the wall.

"There's something else," Lucy said finally. "Something you need to know about the money. The tire shop is underwater, Lee. Ninety thousand behind with the supplier. And three weeks ago, fifty thousand dollars came into his business account from a company in Bozeman. Ponderosa something. He told me it was a loan. And it's already gone. He paid the supplier and the truck note and..." She stopped. "And a deposit on a condo in Scottsdale. I thought it was a surprise. For me."

"It's not for you, Lucy."

"No. I'm starting to get that."

My phone buzzed on the desk between us. It was Dana.

"Where are you? Ramon drove past the county lot and says your truck's still there. It's almost six. Is something wrong?"

Lucy saw it. She looked at the screen, then at me.

"He's checking on you," she said.

"He's checking on you," I said. "He knows your car."

I drove home that night by the long way, out past the Rims, with the city lights coming on below me. When I pulled into the garage, Dana's car was there and the house was dark except for the kitchen.

I didn't go inside right away. I knelt down beside my father's tripod case, the way I'd done every night for a week, and I unbuckled the strap.

The flip phone was gone. So was the deed.

I stood there in the garage with the empty case open at my feet, and for one bad minute I thought I'd lost it all. Then I remembered the three hundred and eleven photographs on my own phone, and the copies Ruth had made of every one of them on Wednesday morning, and I breathed again.

Somebody had gotten nervous. That was all. Somebody had noticed a man asking about a tripod.

I went inside. Dana was at the kitchen counter with a glass of wine, scrolling her real phone.

"You're late," she said.

"Long day. Subdivision out in Lockwood."

"Ramon said your truck was at the county lot."

"That's where I work, Dana."

She looked at me over her wine glass for a second too long. Then she smiled.

"Your mom called. She wants you to call her back. She sounded worried about you."

My mother is seventy-eight and lives alone in the house in Laurel where Ramon and I grew up, twenty minutes west of Billings, with a rosary on every doorknob and a freezer full of tamales. I called her from the porch.

"Mijo. Are you okay?"

"I'm fine, Mom."

"Ramon says you're drinking. He says you've been sitting in the garage at night by yourself. Dana told him. They're worried about you, Leland."

I stood on my own porch and looked at the Rims, the long dark line of the cliffs over the city. They'd already started. They were laying the groundwork, the way you lay a control line before you survey a lot. Clueless Leland, who drinks alone in the garage.

"I'm not drinking, Mom. I'm sitting in the garage looking at Dad's old things."

"Oh, mijo." Her voice softened. "That's even sadder." Then she cleared her throat, the way she does before she asks for something. "Leland, at Thanksgiving, I want you to sign something for me. Ramon had a lawyer draw it up. It's about the ranch. To keep it simple, after I'm gone. So there's no fighting. Will you do that for me?"

"What does it say, Mom?"

"I don't know, honey, it's lawyer words. Ramon explained it. It's what your father would have wanted."

"I'll read it at Thanksgiving," I said.

"You'll sign it?"

"I'll read it, Mom."

Four days later, on a Friday, Ramon found me at work.

I was up on the Rims north of the airport with my party chief and a rodman, staking out a property line for a man who was building a house with a view he'd paid too much for. It was cold and clear and the wind came across the top of the cliffs like it had a grudge. Ramon's tire shop truck came bumping up the gravel road and stopped about twenty feet from my instrument.

He got out and slammed the door.

"We need to talk."

"I'm working, Ramon."

"I don't care. Mom says you won't commit to signing. She says you said you'd 'read it.' What's that mean, 'read it'?"

"It means read it. It's a thing people do with documents."

My rodman, a kid of twenty-three named on the county roster as a seasonal tech, stopped pretending to hold the rod straight. My party chief, a gray-bearded man who'd worked with me for eleven years, took out his phone and leaned on the truck.

"Don't be cute," Ramon said. "It's for Mom. It's so there's no fighting after she's gone. You want her to worry? She's seventy-eight."

"Then why are you the one up here sweating, Ramon?"

"I'm not sweating."

"It's thirty-one degrees and you're sweating."

He came closer. Close enough that I could smell the tire shop on him, rubber and coffee and the fake leather cleaner he uses on the waiting room chairs.

"You've been weird for two weeks. Dana says you're weird. You're out in the garage every night. You're asking about Dad's tripod. What are you playing at?"

"I'm not playing at anything. I'm a surveyor. I'm surveying."

"Just sign the damn deed Thursday, Lee."

"Is that what it is? A deed? Mom said she didn't know what it was. Lawyer words."

His face changed. He'd said one word too many, and he knew it.

"Sign it," he said, low. "Or I swear to God."

"Or what, Ramon? What are you going to do? Call me Clueless?"

He swung.

It was a big, wild right hand, the kind he used to throw in the yard when we were kids and he was losing at basketball. I'd been ducking it since 1984. I leaned back, the fist went past my nose, and I caught his wrist with my left hand and his elbow with my right and turned him, and he went down on the frozen gravel on his side with a sound like a sack of feed hitting a truck bed.

I stepped back with my hands open.

"Don't get up yet, Ramon. You'll slip."

He lay there breathing hard. My party chief, still leaning on the truck, still holding his phone up, said in a very calm voice, "Got all of it, Lee. From when he pulled in."

Ramon got up slowly. He had gravel stuck to his cheek. His eyes were wet, from the wind or from something else.

"You said Clueless," he said. "Why'd you say Clueless?"

I didn't answer him. I picked up my field book and went back to the instrument.

He stood there another minute. Then he got in his truck and spun the tires on the gravel and drove off down the Rims too fast.

"Who's Clueless?" my rodman asked.

"Hold the rod straight, son," I said.

That night Dana was waiting for me in the living room with all the lights on.

"Ramon came to the house," she said. "He has a scrape all down his face. He says you attacked him at work."

"Ramon swung at me. My party chief filmed the whole thing. You want to see it?"

"I don't want to see your little video."

"Okay."

"What is going on with you? What is this about? Is this about your brother? Because Ramon is my friend, Leland. He's been my friend for twenty years. He's family. You can't be jealous of your own brother."

"I'm not jealous of my brother."

"Then what? You're sitting in the garage at night, you're attacking people, your mother is worried sick, and you won't sign one stupid paper for her. What's wrong with you?"

"What paper, Dana?"

"The... the ranch paper. The one she wants you to sign."

"How do you know about it?"

"Your mother told me."

"Mom told me she didn't know what it was. Lawyer words. Ramon explained it to her. Who explained it to you?"

She opened her mouth. She closed it.

"You're paranoid," she said. "You know that? You're paranoid and you're clueless, Leland. You've been clueless your whole life. You walk around this town like everybody loves you, the nice surveyor, the nice brother, and you have no idea what anybody really thinks about you. None."

There it was. To my face, in my own living room, in her own voice.

"You're right," I said. "I had no idea."

She stared at me. Something flickered in her eyes, just for a second, like a woman who'd stepped on a stair that wasn't there. Then she picked up her wine glass and went upstairs, and I heard the bedroom door lock.

I slept on the couch. I didn't mind. I had plans to make.

The FedEx Office on 24th Street opens at seven. On the Tuesday before Thanksgiving, I was there at five after, with a thumb drive and a cup of coffee, and the young woman behind the counter looked at my file and then at me and then at the file again.

"Three hundred and eleven pages," she said. "Times twenty-two copies."

"Black and white is fine."

"That's six thousand eight hundred and forty-two pages, sir."

"I know. I did the math in my truck."

"And you want them in..."

"Twenty-two manila envelopes. With names on them. I'll write the names myself."

She scrolled to the first page to check the margins, and stopped, and read it, and then she looked up at me in a different way.

"Sir," she said quietly, "do you want me to get the manager to run these? So I don't have to look at them?"

"That'd be kind of you."

It took two hours. I sat in my truck in the parking lot while they printed, and Lucy came and sat in the passenger seat at eight, in her bank blazer, with a folder of her own on her knees.

"I made a copy of this at the bank," she said. "It came to the tire shop by FedEx yesterday, addressed to Ramon. I sign for his mail. I've always signed for his mail."

It was a letter on Ponderosa Land Company letterhead, from Bozeman. It was short. It reminded Ramon that under the option agreement, closing on the Ochoa ranch, all seven hundred acres, was scheduled for December first, at a price of two point one million dollars, and that if he could not deliver clear title to the full property by that date, the fifty-thousand-dollar option payment would be due back in full, plus Ponderosa's costs.

"He can't pay it back," Lucy said. "It's gone. I checked this morning. There's eleven hundred dollars in the business account."

"And the condo?"

"Scottsdale. Two bedrooms. Deposit's in his name and..." She took a breath. "And Dana's. They put down the deposit together. I found the email."

We sat there with the heater running.

"What are you going to do Thursday, Lucy?"

"I'm going to sit next to my husband," she said. "Like I always do. And then I'm going to hand your mother this letter. I'm going to need you to go first, though, Lee. I don't think I can go first."

"I'll go first."

The young woman from the counter knocked on my window and held up a cart stacked with boxes.

"That's all of them, sir," she said. And then, before she turned away: "I hope it goes how you want it to."

My phone buzzed while I was loading the boxes into the back seat. It was a text from Dana. The first one in two days.

"Thursday you're going to sign that paper for your mother. If you don't, I'm going to tell her and everybody at that table that you put her favorite son on the ground. In front of witnesses. Let's see who they believe."

I put the phone in my pocket and loaded the last box.

Ramon showed up at my house the night before Thanksgiving, a little after ten. Dana was at her sister's in Red Lodge, or said she was. I opened the door and he was standing on my porch in his tire shop jacket with no hat, and his breath was all beer.

"Can I come in?"

"You can stand right there."

He stood right there. The scab on his face had gone dark. He looked at his boots for a while, the way he used to when Dad caught him in a lie.

"Lee. Is there something in the garage I should know about?"

"You tell me, Ramon. It's not my garage you've been sneaking into."

He flinched. Then he laughed, the too-loud laugh, but there was nothing behind it.

"Okay. Okay, look. I'm going to be straight with you. I'm in trouble with the shop. Real trouble. I need that ranch deal, Lee. Not for me. For Lucy. For the guys who work for me. Eleven guys. You sign tomorrow, I sell, and I'll cut you in. Twenty thousand. Cash. Nobody has to know."

"Twenty thousand for my half of a two-million-dollar ranch."

"Twenty-five, then. Thirty. Come on, Lee. You don't even go out there anymore. You don't hunt, you don't run cows. It's just dirt to you."

"It's Dad," I said. "It's the only corner of this family I ever found myself."

"Don't do that. Don't do the Dad thing."

"Ramon, where's the phone?"

He went completely still. Two brothers on a porch in November, and one of them had just been asked a question he couldn't answer without lying, and both of us knew it.

"What phone?"

"Okay," I said, and started to close the door.

He put his hand flat on it. Not hard. Just enough.

"Lee. Whatever you think you know. Mom's seventy-eight. Her heart's not good. Whatever you're planning for tomorrow, you think about what it does to her."

"I've thought about it every night for twenty-five days, Ramon. You had fifteen years to think about it."

He took his hand off the door. He stood there on the porch a minute longer, and I watched him decide not to say whatever it was he was going to say. Then he walked back down the steps to his truck and sat in it in my driveway with the engine running for a long time before he backed out.

I called Ruth Canfield at eleven at night, because she'd told me to call her anytime, and she answered on the first ring with the television on behind her.

"He offered me thirty thousand," I said.

"For a half worth a million and change? Your brother negotiates like he sells tires." She turned the TV down. "Lee, listen to me. Tomorrow you don't sign anything. Not the deed, not a napkin, not a birthday card. If anybody puts a pen in your hand, you put it down."

"I won't sign."

"And Lee? Whatever happens at that table, you don't raise your voice. Let the paper talk. Paper doesn't get tired."

Thanksgiving at my mother's house in Laurel starts at two o'clock, which means it starts at a quarter to three, because my Aunt Pilar is always late and nobody eats until Pilar has kissed every single person in the house. There were twenty-two of us that year. My mother's sisters, their husbands, a bunch of cousins, a couple of their kids home from college, old Father Benny from St. Anthony's who comes every year for the pie. Ramon and Lucy. Dana and me.

The table was two tables pushed together with a sheet over the crack, the way it's been since I was a boy, with a turkey at one end and a ham at the other and Mom's tamales in the middle, because the Ochoas have never had a holiday without tamales.

Before anybody came in, I'd gone around the table and put a manila envelope under every plate. Twenty-two of them. Every one with a name on it, in my handwriting.

Nobody noticed at first. People don't look under their plates.

Ramon came in with a scab down the side of his face and told everybody he'd slipped on ice at the shop. Dana came in on my arm, smiling, and kissed my mother's cheek. They didn't look at each other. They'd had fifteen years of practice.

We sat. My mother sat at the head, in my father's chair. Father Benny said grace, which took a while, because he thanks the turkey personally. And then, before the first plate got passed, my mother took a folded paper out of her apron pocket and laid it on the tablecloth next to her plate.

"Before we eat," she said. "Leland, mijo. I want you to sign this for me. Today. So we don't have to talk about it anymore."

The table went quiet. Ramon put his fork down. Across from me, Dana lifted her wine glass and didn't drink.

"Of course, Mom," I said. "Can I read it first?"

"Lee," Ramon said. "Come on. It's Thanksgiving."

"I just want to read it. You never trust a corner you didn't find yourself. Dad's rule."

My mother smiled at that. She slid the paper down the table to me.

I unfolded it. It was the deed from the tripod case, the same one, with my name typed on the line marked Grantor and my brother's on the line marked Grantee, for ten dollars and love and affection.

"Mom," I said. "This gives Ramon my half of the ranch."

"It's to keep it simple. That's what Ramon said."

"Did Ramon tell you he's already agreed to sell the whole ranch to a developer in Bozeman for two point one million dollars? On December first?"

"Leland, that's a lie," Dana said, loud.

"That's a lie," Ramon said, louder. "Mom, he's been drinking, he's been... he attacked me, Mom, look at my face..."

"He did, Teresa," Dana said, turning to my mother with her eyes already wet. "He put Ramon on the ground. At work. In front of witnesses. Ask him."

"I did," I said. "After Ramon swung at me. My party chief filmed it from the second his truck pulled in. Mom, it's on my phone. Ramon swings, I duck, he lands on the gravel. You want to watch it right here, before the pie?"

My mother looked at Ramon. Ramon looked at his plate.

"I slipped on ice," he said, to nobody.

"That's three stories now, mijo," my mother said. "The ice, the attack, and the slip."

"Everybody," I said, "look under your plate."

Twenty-two people looked under their plates. I watched my Aunt Pilar pull out an envelope with her name on it, and Father Benny, and my cousin's college kid, and my mother.

"Leland, what is this?" my mother asked.

"It's what I found in Dad's tripod case, Mom. In my garage. On a phone that isn't mine."

Ramon had his envelope open. He was staring at the first page. I knew what was on it, because I'd put it there on purpose. The text from the first Sunday in November.

"Clueless is carving. Twenty minutes, garage. Perfect cover, like always."

"Read it out loud, Ramon," I said.

He didn't move.

"Read it, Ramon. You wrote half of it."

My Aunt Pilar was reading hers already, her lips moving, her hand slowly going up over her mouth. Father Benny had his glasses on and was turning pages very carefully, like they might bite.

Dana stood up so fast her wine went over on the tablecloth, dark red, spreading across the sheet over the crack.

"These are fake. He faked these. He's sick, he's been sick for weeks, everybody knows..."

"Fifteen years," I said. "Since the morning of our wedding. The bridal suite at the Northern Hotel. The 'family welcome.' It's on page four. Your own words, Dana."

"Ramon," my mother said.

Ramon looked up from the paper. His hands were shaking so hard the pages rattled against his plate.

"Mom, I can..."

"Read it," my mother said. "Read me what you wrote. Out loud. In your father's house."

And my brother, God help him, did. Because he has always done what Mom asks.

"You married the right one for the job," he read, in a voice like a little boy's. "He's the perfect cover. Nobody looks at the wife of the nicest guy in Billings."

Nobody said anything. The only sound in that dining room was the furnace and my Aunt Pilar crying.

Lucy stood up then. She was shaking, too, but she stood up straight in her nice holiday sweater and walked down the length of the table to my mother, and put a single sheet of paper next to her plate.

"Teresa," she said. "I'm sorry. This came to the shop on Monday. I sign for Ramon's mail."

My mother read the Ponderosa letter. She read it slowly. She read the part about two point one million dollars, and December first, and all seven hundred acres. She read the part about the fifty thousand dollars, and the part about clear title.

Then she looked at her younger son.

"You were going to sell your father's ranch," she said. "To a developer. For houses. And you were going to use me to steal your brother's half."

"Mom, it's not stealing, it's..."

"You put your father's name in my mouth. 'It's what your father would have wanted.' You made me say that."

"Mom, please. Please. I owe them fifty thousand dollars. They'll sue me. I'll lose the shop, I'll lose everything..."

"And the condo in Scottsdale?" Lucy said quietly. "The one with your name and Dana's on the deposit? Will you lose that too, Ramon?"

Ramon turned and lunged across the table for the envelopes, I don't know why, as if he could gather up six thousand pages and stuff them back in the tripod case. He got a handful of my Aunt Pilar's and knocked the gravy boat into the tamales, and one of my cousins and Uncle Tito grabbed him by both arms and sat him back down in his chair hard enough to crack the seat.

"Sit," Uncle Tito said. "You sit there, boy."

Dana was already moving toward the front door with her purse. She stopped by my chair. She bent down, close, and for a second she was the girl I'd married, fifteen years ago, in a white dress at the end of an aisle.

"Leland," she whispered. "Lee, please. Not like this. Not in front of everyone. We can go home. We can talk. I'll explain everything. I'll tell you everything, I swear. Please don't do this."

I looked up at her.

"You told me I had no idea what anybody really thought of me," I said. "Now I do."

She straightened up. She looked around the table, at twenty-one faces, at Father Benny and my aunts and the college kids and my mother. Not one of them looked back at her.

She walked out. The storm door banged behind her.

Lucy took Ramon's truck keys off the hook by the door, put them in her purse, and sat back down at the table next to her husband, very straight, and folded her hands.

My mother picked up the deed. She looked at it for a long moment. Then she tore it in half, and in half again, and in half again, and dropped the pieces into the gravy boat.

"Leland," she said. "Carve the turkey. Thin slices. Like your father."

I stood up and picked up the carving knife.

And from the far end of the table, through his glasses, still holding page four of his envelope, Father Benny said the last thing anybody said before we ate.

"Well," he said, "I believe I'll have the pie first."
