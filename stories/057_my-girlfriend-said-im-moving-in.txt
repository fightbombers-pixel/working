"I'm moving in with Marcus. We're in love."

My girlfriend said it standing up, with a glass of iced tea in her hand, at her MBA capstone lunch at the University of Memphis alumni center, in front of her whole cohort, two professors and the guest speaker. About thirty people. The guest speaker was Marcus.

I was sitting at table four with my sport coat on, the one I wear to funerals and graduations. Forty-eight hours later, she was standing on my front porch with mascara down to her chin, ringing the bell with one hand and holding her phone in the other, and she was still wearing the same dress.

My name is Stuart Cain. I'm 54, and I run the overnight dock at a freight hub out by the Memphis airport. Twenty-eight years of moving other people's boxes teaches you one thing. The label tells you where a box is going. It doesn't tell you what's in it. Hold on to that.

She'd find her own way.

Sunday evening, at a little after seven, the doorbell rang.

That was forty-eight hours, almost to the minute, from the moment she stood up at that lunch and told thirty people she was in love.

I opened the door, and there she was. The green capstone dress, wrinkled now, with a coffee stain on the hip. Mascara streaked down to her chin. Her phone in one hand. No coat, in December. Her sister's Ford Escape was idling at the curb with nobody in it.

"I'm working at Dr. Alvarado's office in Cordova," she said. "Front desk. Thirty-two hours a week."

"That's good."

"It's the same job I had when we met. Did you know that? Six years later and I'm right back where I started."

"It's honest work."

"It is." She wrapped both hands around the coffee cup like she was cold. "Stuart, I talked to the school. If I get the tuition paid by August, they'll let me back in for the fall. I'd only lose one semester. And I found a private lender that'll do it. But they need a co-signer. Somebody with good credit."

"You want me to co-sign a loan."

"Not pay it. Just sign. I'll pay every dime. You'd never have to touch it."

"That's what a co-signer is, Tasha. Somebody who never has to touch it until he does."

"I know how it sounds."

"How does it sound?"

She took a breath. The linemen two tables over had stopped even pretending to eat.

"It sounds like I'm asking you for money again," she said. "And I am. I'm not going to lie to you anymore. I'm asking."

"Thank you for not lying."

"So?"

"No."

She closed her eyes for a second.

"Just like that."

"Just like that."

"You didn't even think about it."

"I thought about it for twenty-two months."

Mr. Tolbert at the counter coughed into his napkin. I'm pretty sure it was a laugh.

Tasha leaned across the table and lowered her voice, but in a Waffle House there's no such thing as lowering your voice. The grill was quiet and the jukebox was off.

"You know what everybody's saying? In the cohort? They say you planned it. That you knew about Marcus all along and you were just waiting to pull the money at the worst time."

"Is that what you think?"

She was quiet a long moment.

"No," she said. "No. Renata told me what you said at Panera. That you laughed. That you didn't even know he was broke till she told you." She shook her head. "You didn't plan anything. You just stopped. You just stopped doing everything you were doing, and it all fell down."

"That's all I did."

"Do you know how much worse that is?"

"No."

"Because it means it was all you. Everything. The house and the bills and the tuition and the car insurance. I stood up in that room and told everybody I'd found somebody who was going somewhere." Her voice wobbled. "And the only one in my whole life who was actually holding anything up was sitting at table four in his funeral jacket."

"I told you once what my job taught me."

"The label thing." She laughed, wet. "The label tells you where it's going, not what's in it."

"You read his label. He read yours."

"And nobody read yours." She pressed the heel of her hand against one eye. "You were just a plain brown box with no return address, and I never once opened it to see what was inside."

I didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say to that. She'd said it all herself.

The waitress came by and topped off my coffee without asking.

"Can I tell you something?" Tasha said.

"Go ahead."

"Marcus and I were never in love. I know I said it. I stood up and said it in front of thirty people. But we weren't. We were just two people looking at each other's cars."

"I figured."

"I don't think I was ever in love with him." She looked up at me, and her eyes were wet, and for once there wasn't a drop of mascara to run. "I think I'm still in love with you, Stuart. I think I always was. I just didn't know it till you said that word and walked out."

The whole Waffle House was waiting. The waitress with the coffee pot. Mr. Tolbert with his stool turned. The two linemen. A trucker at the end of the counter who didn't know either of us from Adam and had put his fork down anyway.

I wiped my mouth with my napkin and folded it next to my plate. I took two twenties out of my wallet and laid them on the check, enough for my All-Star and her coffee and a good tip. Then I picked up my keys, the ones with the little floating bobber on the ring, and slid out of the booth.

She watched me stand up. She knew. I think she knew before I did.

"Understood," I said.
