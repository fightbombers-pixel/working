"It's nothing personal, Mark. I just saw an opportunity."

My girlfriend said that to my face in the employee break room at Man o' War Toyota in Lexington, Kentucky, at a quarter past four on a Tuesday afternoon, with three people standing close enough to hear every word. My master tech had a sandwich halfway to his mouth. The girl from the parts counter was frozen at the microwave. A young service advisor stood in the doorway holding two cups of coffee.

Two hours earlier, the general manager had called me into her office and told me that Sonia Prieto had filed a formal harassment complaint against me, and that starting tomorrow I was on paid administrative leave while an outside investigator looked into it.

Nineteen years I'd worked at that dealership. I'd run the service department for eleven of them, forty-two employees, the highest customer satisfaction scores in the region three years running. And two months ago, I'd walked Sonia's application into the GM's office myself and said, "Hire her. I'll vouch for her."

I didn't say a word.

I picked up a blank repair order off the stack by the coffee maker, clicked a pen, and wrote down exactly what she'd said, word for word, with the date and the time. Then I turned it around and held it out to my master tech. He looked at it, looked at me, put down his sandwich and initialed it. The parts girl initialed it next. I folded it, put it in my shirt pocket, and walked out of that break room without looking back.

Twenty-three days later, Sonia was standing outside the glass doors of the service drive at five minutes to seven in the morning, swiping her badge over and over at a reader that kept blinking red, while eleven technicians watched her through the shop windows.

What she didn't know, standing in that break room telling me it was nothing personal, was where I'd been on the fourteenth of March.

My name is Mark Delgado. I'm 46, and I'm the service director at a Toyota dealership. Oil changes, brake jobs, transmission rebuilds, recall work, three thousand repair orders a month. The thing you learn running a service department is that every repair order has a time stamp. The technician clocks on, the technician clocks off. The computer records it to the minute. A customer can tell you their car's been making that noise for a week, and the repair history will tell you whether it's true. Hold on to that.

Sonia and I met fourteen months ago at a steakhouse on Richmond Road where she was waiting tables. She was funny and quick and she remembered that I liked my coffee black with my steak, which nobody ever remembers. She was 38. She'd been a server for fifteen years and she was tired of it. Six months later, she moved into my house off Tates Creek Road. Ten months later, she told me she wanted a real job with benefits, and I told her the dealership was looking for a service advisor, and that she'd be great at it, because she was great with people.

She was great at it. Customers loved her. She upsold more cabin air filters in her first month than anybody in the department.

I'd been proud of her.

Dee Ann Rucker, our general manager, had explained it to me that afternoon with her office door closed and a box of tissues on her desk that she didn't offer me.

"She says you made comments about her body," Dee Ann said. "Repeatedly. In front of customers. She says on one occasion you cornered her in the parts storage room and grabbed her arm and wouldn't let her leave. She gave a date. March fourteenth. Around two in the afternoon."

"That's not true."

"Mark, I've known you nine years. I don't think it's true either. But she put it in writing, and she sent a copy to Clay, and she sent a copy to Toyota corporate's employee hotline. So it's out of my hands. We have to bring in an outside investigator. That's the law and that's the policy."

"I understand."

"You'll be on paid leave. You can't contact her about the complaint. You can't contact any witnesses about the complaint. If you do, it's retaliation, and that's worse than anything she's accused you of."

"I understand."

Dee Ann looked at me for a long moment. "You're very calm."

"What would you like me to do?"

"Most men would be yelling by now."

"Would yelling help?"

"No."

"Then I'll be calm."

She rubbed her eyes. "There's one more thing, and I'm only telling you because you're going to hear it anyway. She sent Clay a second email. Separate from the complaint. She said that if you're removed from your position, she'd like to be considered as your replacement. She said she's been 'effectively running the front counter' since she started."

"She's been here eight weeks."

"I know."

"She doesn't know what a torque converter is."

"I know, Mark."

That was two hours before Sonia said it was nothing personal. I'd gone to the break room to get my coffee mug, the one that said WORLD'S OKAYEST SERVICE MANAGER that my daughter gave me when she was twelve. Sonia was in there talking to Becca from the parts counter. She stopped talking when she saw me.

She didn't look scared. That was the first thing I noticed. A woman who's been cornered in a storage room is scared of the man who did it. Sonia looked at me like a poker player looks at the pot.

"You talked to Dee Ann," she said.

"Why, Sonia?" I said. It was the only thing I asked her, and the last thing I said to her for twenty-three days.

"Look," Sonia said. "I know this is awkward. But it's nothing personal, Mark. I just saw an opportunity. You'd have done the same thing."

That's when I picked up the repair order.

He stared at me. Then he understood, and his face went dark.

I didn't say anything.

"That's what the paper was for." He looked at my shirt pocket. "You wanted us to sign what she said."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

He shook his head. "You're a strange man, Mark Delgado."

"I've been told."

Sonia came home at seven-thirty with her sister.

Remember that steakhouse. I did.

I looked at her across the kitchen.

I didn't say a word.

She stood there for a long moment with her hand on the doorknob. For the first time all day, she looked a little unsure, like a woman who had planned for a fight and gotten an empty room instead.

"Fine," she said, and left.

"Ms. Prieto," Clay said, very calmly, "on the fourteenth of March, at two in the afternoon, Mark Delgado was in a classroom in Plano, Texas. Toyota has his badge records. American Airlines has his boarding pass. And your own sister has a pizza receipt."

Sonia's face changed. It was like watching a car roll back on a lift that hadn't been locked.

"Paloma," she said.

"She told the investigator the truth," Clay said. "And so did Jarrod, who showed her the text where you asked him to lie for you. And so did the three people standing in the break room the day you told Mark you saw an opportunity."

Sonia turned around and looked at the shop. Dewayne. Becca. Jarrod. Jarrod looked at the floor. Dewayne didn't.

"That was a joke," Sonia said. "That was obviously a joke."

"Mark wrote it down," Clay said. "With the time. Funny joke to write down."

Her phone buzzed in her hand. She'd been calling me the whole time, I realized, and it had gone to voicemail and she'd hung up and dialed again without even looking. My pocket buzzed. Becca, beside me, checked my screen and murmured, "Forty-seven."

Clay turned away from her then, the way a man turns away from a bill he's already paid, and looked at me.

"Mark," he said, loud enough for the whole shop. "I've wanted to say this for twenty-three days and couldn't. I'm sorry. I'm sorry this company ever made you sit at home while we checked whether a man who's given us nineteen years was who we already knew he was." He put his hand on my shoulder. "We break ground on the Lexus store in Georgetown in October. I want you running the service side. Your pick of who comes with you. Dee Ann's going to draw up the paperwork today."

Somebody in the back of the shop whistled. Becca clapped once, then stopped, embarrassed.

Sonia stood there with her phone in her hand. The job she'd filed a false complaint to get had just been handed to the man she'd filed it against, in the same service drive, in front of the same people, at twenty-five past seven on a Friday morning.

Sonia looked at me then. Straight at me, across the service drive, past the coffee machine and the old man in the John Deere cap and the eleven technicians.

"Say something," she said. Her voice cracked. "Twenty-three days and you haven't said one word to me. Not one. You just sat there and let it happen. You could have stopped this, Mark. You could have called me. We could have worked it out. You could have taken the deal and walked away and nobody would have had to know about any of it. Why didn't you say anything?"

The whole shop waited.

"Mark," Clay said gently. "You can talk now. Investigation's over."

I took the folded repair order out of my shirt pocket. Corinne Bales had mailed it back to me in a manila envelope with a note that said, "Thank you for the record." I unfolded it and looked at Sonia's words in my own handwriting, and Dewayne's initials, and Becca's.

Then I looked up at her.

"It's nothing personal, Sonia," I said. "I just saw an opportunity."
