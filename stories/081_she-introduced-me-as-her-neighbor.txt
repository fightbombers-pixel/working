"Dorian, this is Pedro. He's my neighbor."

My wife said it on the patio of a lake resort in Horseshoe Bay, at the welcome dinner of her company's spring retreat, with sixty of her coworkers holding margaritas within earshot. Her boss's hand was resting on the small of her back while she said it, and it stayed there.

Neighbor. We had been married thirty-one years. I had paid for the dress she was wearing, the earrings she was wearing, and about four hundred dollars of the room she was planning to sleep in.

Dorian Fairbanks put out his free hand. He was 51, tan in March, with a watch that cost more than my first house.

"Pedro. Good to meet you. You live near Dani?"

"Real near," I said.

I shook his hand. Then I reached into my shirt pocket, pulled out one of my business cards, and pressed it into his palm.

"Castillo Drywall," he read. "Huh. You do residential?"

"Mostly. Your wife's kitchen, actually. About four years back."

His smile didn't move, but his hand came off Daniela's back to put the card in his pocket.

Twenty-seven hours later, Dorian Fairbanks was standing in that resort's parking lot reading a text message from his wife, and when he looked up from his phone, he couldn't find Daniela in the crowd fast enough.

What none of them knew was that my name and phone number were already sitting in Marisa Fairbanks's contacts, right between her pool man and her pastor.

My name is Pedro Castillo. I'm 59 years old, I've been hanging and finishing drywall in San Angelo, Texas, for thirty-four years, and I own a handful of rental houses I fixed up one at a time. Drywall teaches you one thing above everything else. A wall is only as good as what's behind it, and most people never look behind it. Hold on to that.

"Pedro does such good work," Daniela said, laughing a little too loud. "He's always helping out around the street."

"Always," I said.

A young woman with a name tag that said Renata walked up with a tray of shrimp, saw Daniela, saw me, and stopped like she'd stepped on a rake. Renata Gallo. I knew her because she'd been to our house twice for Daniela's Christmas party, and both times she'd sat in my kitchen and asked me about my roses.

"Mr. Castillo," she said. "You came."

"Mr. Castillo is my neighbor," Daniela said, before I could answer. "Isn't that funny, Renata? Small world."

Renata looked at Daniela, then at me, then at Dorian's pocket where my card had gone. She set the shrimp down on a table that didn't need shrimp.

"Real small," she said.

I should tell you why I was even there.

Three weeks earlier, a letter came to our house on company letterhead. Concho Valley Billing Services, annual leadership retreat, Horseshoe Bay Resort, Friday through Sunday. Spouses and partners welcome. Daniela had been the director of billing there for six years. She left the letter on the kitchen counter under a bag of oranges like she hoped it would rot there with them.

"You'd hate it," she told me. "It's all PowerPoints and golf."

"I don't hate golf."

"You've never played golf."

"Then I can't hate it yet."

She laughed, but it was the kind of laugh you give a waiter. And then she said a thing I didn't think about again until that patio.

"Honestly, Pedro, it's better if you don't. It's kind of a work-work thing. People don't really bring their husbands."

The letter said spouses welcome. I read it twice. So the Friday of the retreat, I finished a job early, ironed a shirt, ironed my jeans too, because my father ironed his jeans and so do I, and I drove the two and a half hours to Horseshoe Bay without telling her I was coming. I figured I'd surprise her.

I surprised her, all right. She saw me walk out onto that patio, and her face did something I'd never seen it do in thirty-one years. It went looking for an exit. And then, when there wasn't one, it found Dorian.

That night we had separate beds. She'd booked a room with two queens, which I noticed and decided not to notice. She came in a little before midnight, smelling like tequila and somebody else's cologne, and she whispered at me in the dark.

"Why are you here?"

"The letter said spouses."

"You embarrassed me."

"By driving three hours to see my wife?"

"By showing up in boots. Everybody's wearing loafers."

"So that's why I'm the neighbor."

She didn't answer that. She rolled over toward the wall, and inside of ten minutes she was asleep, and I lay there looking at the smoke detector blink green until the sun came up.

Saturday was the boat day.

The company had rented two pontoon boats and a ski boat, and by ten in the morning, half of Concho Valley Billing was out on Lake LBJ drinking beer in koozies with the company logo on them. I sat at the back of the second pontoon with a Dr Pepper and my phone. Daniela sat up front, in the ski boat, next to Dorian.

I'm not a spy. I don't know how to do anything sneaky with a phone. But I know how to zoom, and I know how to hold still, because when you're setting a corner bead on a twelve-foot ceiling, holding still is the whole job.

So I held still.

At a quarter past ten, Dorian's hand on her lower back as she climbed down into the ski boat. Click. At eleven, the two of them under the canopy, his arm along the seat behind her, her head tipped against his shoulder for just a second too long. Click. At noon, when the boats rafted up in a cove and everybody was swimming, he handed her a towel and kept one hand on her hip while she dried her hair. Click.

"You know you're doing that out loud, right?"

Renata Gallo had climbed onto my pontoon from the raft. She sat down across from me, wet hair, sunglasses on her head, holding a bottle of water in both hands like a little kid.

"Doing what?"

"Taking pictures. Your phone's making the shutter noise."

I looked at my phone. She was right. I'd never turned the sound off, because I'd never needed to.

"Huh," I said. "Well. Nobody's listening to me anyway. I'm just the neighbor."

Renata didn't laugh.

"Mr. Castillo, can I tell you something? You can be mad at me after."

"I won't be mad at you."

"Everybody knows."

I put the phone down on my knee.

"Everybody knows what?"

"About them. Dorian and Dani. Since March of last year. They go to Dallas once a month for a 'client summit.' There is no client summit. I book the hotel. One room, on the company card, under Dorian's name. Twelve times now."

"Twelve," I said.

"Thirteen next week. It's already booked."

The boat rocked a little. Somebody up front cannonballed into the lake and everybody cheered.

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because you came to our Christmas party and you asked me about my mother's surgery. Twice. You remembered her name." Renata looked down at the water bottle. "Dorian's never once asked me anything that wasn't about a spreadsheet. And last night I watched Dani call you her neighbor while his hand was on her, and everybody at that table laughed, and I didn't. I felt sick."

"Who laughed?"

"Everybody. The CFO. The two vice presidents. Kelly from payroll. I can give you a list."

"I don't want a list. I'm not making a list."

"Then what are you going to do?"

I picked my phone back up, found the settings, and turned off the shutter sound.

"Nothing today," I said. "Today I'm on a boat."

She looked at me for a long moment.

"There's one more thing," she said. "And it's the thing that made me throw up in my bathroom last month."

"Go ahead."

"Dorian isn't the owner. Everybody acts like he is, but he isn't. The company was his father-in-law's. Mrs. Fairbanks inherited it when her dad died. She chairs the board. Dorian runs it because she votes him in every year." Renata pushed her sunglasses down over her eyes. "If she ever found out, he'd be gone in an afternoon. And Dani's in his car every month. On the company card."

I didn't say anything. Out across the cove, Dorian was laughing at something, his head thrown back. Daniela was laughing with him. She'd never laughed at one of my jokes like that, and I've got some pretty good ones.

"Renata," I said, "does Mrs. Fairbanks still have that blue tile backsplash with the little roosters?"

She frowned. "How would I know that?"

"I hung the wall behind it. She wanted roosters. I told her roosters would date it. She said her daddy loved roosters, and she was right, and I was wrong."

"You know Marisa Fairbanks?"

"I know her kitchen," I said. "That's not the same thing. Not yet."

Saturday night was the awards banquet. Daniela got Director of the Year, a glass star on a wooden base, and Dorian handed it to her at the podium and kissed her on the cheek, and the room applauded, and I applauded too, because that's what you do in a room. She didn't look at me once. When she came back to the table, she set the glass star between us like a fence.

"You could at least try to look happy for me," she said.

"I'm clapping."

"You're clapping like a man at a funeral."

"Well," I said, "I know a lot of people in this room who are about to be at one. They just don't know it yet."

She stared at me.

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means the brisket was dry. Pass the salt."

After the banquet, the photographer took everybody out onto the lawn under the string lights for the group photo. He lined everybody up by height. Daniela stood in the middle of the front row. Dorian stood behind her. I stood off to the side near the hedges with the other three spouses who'd come, and when the photographer said, "Partners, squeeze in!" Daniela looked straight at me and gave the smallest shake of her head.

So I didn't squeeze in.

After the photo, the young crowd headed for the bar. Daniela had told me that morning that on Sunday she'd be riding back to San Angelo with a coworker because she had "a few things to wrap up with the leadership team" first. I said fine. I went back to our room.

She was already in there. Sitting on the end of the bed with her phone, still in her banquet dress, her glass star on the dresser.

"I thought you were wrapping things up with the leadership team," I said.

"I am. Tomorrow. I'm leaving at three."

"With who?"

"With a coworker. I told you."

"Is the coworker's name Dorian?"

She put the phone face down on the bedspread. That's a thing people do. Watch for it.

"Don't start, Pedro."

"I'm not starting. I'm asking."

"Yes. Dorian's driving me. He lives four streets over from us. It makes sense."

"He lives in Santa Rita. We live off Sherwood Way. That's not four streets, that's three miles and a golf course."

"Oh my God." She stood up. "Is this what you drove three hours for? To count streets?"

I took out my phone. I opened the photos. I held it up so she could see the first one, his hand on her back as she stepped into the ski boat.

"I drove three hours to see my wife," I said. "And I spent three days watching a man keep his hand on her like he was holding her place in line."

She looked at the phone for about two seconds. Then she smiled. Not a nice smile. A smirk, the kind a cashier gives you when you try to use an expired coupon.

"It's just corporate politics, Pedro. Don't be so insecure."

"Corporate politics."

"Yes. Dorian's a touchy person. He's like that with everyone. I'm the director of billing. I need him on my side. You don't understand how a real company works. You've been working for yourself in a dusty truck your whole life."

"You introduced me as your neighbor."

"Because I didn't want to explain you!"

That one hung there in the room for a while. She heard it too. I watched her hear it.

"I didn't mean it like that," she said.

"Yeah, you did."

"You're blowing this up into something it's not."

"Then tell me what it is."

"It's nothing. It's work. It's a hand. You're seriously going to stand there and act like a jealous teenager over a hand?" She picked up her phone again and turned it over and looked at the screen, and whatever was on it made her soften her voice. "Look. Go to sleep. Tomorrow I'll be home by six. We'll get Chinese. We'll talk."

"Okay," I said.

She blinked. "Okay?"

"Okay. I'll see you."

I didn't argue. There wasn't anything to argue about. You can't argue with a woman who's already decided you're a neighbor.

She went back down to the bar to find the leadership team. I zipped up my bag. I set my room key on the dresser next to her glass star. And then I sat in my truck in the resort parking lot with the engine running and the air on high, and I did two things.

First, I sent five photos to my father-in-law, Armando Reyes, with one line under them. "Armando, I'm sorry you have to see this. I wanted you to hear it from me first."

Then I scrolled back four years in my invoice app, to a kitchen remodel on Country Club Road, and found the phone number of Marisa Fairbanks. I sent her the same five photos, and under them I typed, "Mrs. Fairbanks, this is Pedro Castillo. I hung your kitchen walls in 2022. My wife is the woman in these pictures. I thought you deserved to know what I know. I won't contact you again unless you want me to."

Then I put the truck in drive. On my way out of the lot I passed Dorian standing by his black BMW with his phone in his hand. He was reading something, and then he looked up and turned in a full circle, scanning the lawn and the lobby doors like a man who had lost his kid at the fair. He didn't even notice my truck go by.

I pulled out of Horseshoe Bay with the lake going dark behind me, and I didn't wait for six o'clock tomorrow.

I was forty minutes outside of Brady, on the long empty stretch of Highway 87, when my phone lit up on the seat beside me.

It was a text. Not from Daniela, and not from Armando.

"Mr. Castillo. Yes, I remember you. You were wrong about the roosters, by the way. Come to my house Monday at seven in the morning. Don't tell anyone. Especially not my husband."

I didn't go home that night. I own a duplex on Pecan Street that was between tenants, with a mattress in the back bedroom and a coffee maker on the counter, and I slept there with my boots by the door.

My phone kept count for me. By midnight, Daniela had called four times. By one in the morning, eleven times. By the time I turned the lamp off, the screen said nineteen missed calls and thirty-two unread texts, and the last one, which I read because it showed up on the lock screen, said, "You sent pictures to my FATHER??"

I didn't answer. Some questions answer themselves.

I spent Sunday hanging board in a garage conversion across town, because hands that are working don't text anybody back. At seven on Monday morning, I parked on Country Club Road in front of a big limestone house with a three-car garage, and Marisa Fairbanks opened the front door before I could knock.

She was 52, short gray hair, reading glasses on a beaded chain, and she was wearing a bathrobe over jeans, like she'd started getting dressed and then changed her mind halfway. She looked at my truck, then at me.

"You still drive the same truck," she said.

"It still runs."

"Come in. Dorian's at the gym until eight-thirty. He goes every Monday. Or he says he does."

Her kitchen was exactly how I left it. Blue tile backsplash, little white roosters marching across it in a row. She saw me look at them.

"You told me they'd date it," she said.

"I did."

"They didn't."

"No, ma'am. They didn't. I was wrong."

"Well, that's one man in Texas who admits it." She poured me coffee without asking how I took it and set it down in front of me black, which happens to be how I take it. "Sit."

I sat. She sat across from me with her phone face up on the table between us, the five photos on the screen.

"I need to ask you something, and I need you to be honest," she said. "Did your wife put you up to this? Is this some kind of game between the two of you to get money out of me?"

"No, ma'am."

"How do I know that?"

"Because this weekend my wife called me nineteen times, and none of them were to say thank you."

She almost smiled. Almost.

"Tell me what you saw. All of it. Not what you think. What you saw."

So I told her. The patio and the word neighbor. The boat and the towel. The banquet and the glass star. The group photo where I got shaken off to the side like a fly. I didn't tell her what Renata told me, because that was Renata's to tell, and I wasn't going to put a 31-year-old girl's job on a stranger's kitchen table.

When I finished, Marisa took her glasses off and let them hang on the chain.

"Dallas," she said.

I didn't say anything.

"He goes to Dallas once a month. For a client summit. I asked him last summer why our billing company needs a summit in Dallas when all our clients are in West Texas, and he told me I wouldn't understand, that it was about networking." She tapped her fingernail on the table. "I've been on the board of this company since my father died in 2015. I understand perfectly well what networking costs. I just never looked at what it cost."

"Can you look?"

"I can look at anything I want. I chair the board. It's my father's company. Dorian runs it because I vote him in every January, and every January he brings me flowers the day before." She put her glasses back on. "I'm going to pull every card statement for the last eighteen months. And I'm going to do it quietly."

"What do you need from me?"

"Nothing. That's what I need from you. Don't call Dorian. Don't post anything. Don't go shouting at my office. Can you do that?"

"I've been doing it for three days."

"Your wife. Does she know you sent these to me?"

"She knows I sent them to her father. I don't think she knows about you."

"Keep it that way." She stood up. The meeting was over. At the door she put her hand on my arm.

"Mr. Castillo. Why did you send them to me? You could have just gone home and filed for divorce. Nobody would have blamed you."

"Because I spent three days watching your husband put his hand on my wife in front of sixty people who all knew," I said. "And every one of them was laughing at me. I figured somebody ought to be laughing at me who also got lied to. Then at least there'd be two of us."

She nodded slowly.

"Our board meets on the 20th," she said. "Don't make any plans that morning."

I was backing out of her driveway when my phone rang. Daniela. Call number twenty-six. I picked up this time.

"Where are you?"

"Working."

"Where did you sleep?"

"Somewhere quiet."

"You sent pictures to my father, Pedro. To my father. He's seventy-four years old. He has a heart condition. Do you know what you did to him?"

"I told him the truth before somebody else could tell him a lie."

"Well, here's some truth for you. I went to HR this morning. I filed a complaint. I told them you followed me to a company event uninvited, stalked me for three days and took pictures of me and my coworkers without consent. They're opening an investigation."

"The letter said spouses welcome."

"You weren't welcome. I told you not to come."

"You told me people don't bring their husbands. Three of them did. I stood next to them in the picture."

"Nobody is going to care about that. They're going to care that a man in work boots was creeping around a retreat taking pictures with his phone. Do you know how that looks?"

"Like a man taking pictures of his wife."

"Like a stalker." She was breathing hard now. "And when my lawyer gets done with you, I'm going to have the house, and half of every one of your stupid duplexes, and you'll be paying me every month until I'm dead."

"Okay," I said.

"Stop saying okay!"

"Okay."

She hung up.

On Wednesday evening, Armando called and asked me to come by the house on Pecan Street. Not my duplex. Their house, a block down the same street, where Daniela grew up, where we'd had our wedding reception in the backyard in 1995 under a string of Christmas lights in June.

When I pulled up, I knew it wasn't going to be just Armando. Daniela's white Camry was in the driveway. So was my mother-in-law's Buick.

Armando was sitting on the porch swing with a glass of iced tea. He's 74, a retired lineman, with hands like baseball mitts, and he had his reading glasses pushed up on his forehead and my five photos printed out and stacked on his knee.

"Mijo," he said. "Sit."

I sat on the porch steps. The screen door banged open, and Daniela came out, and Consuelo came right behind her like a tugboat.

"He doesn't get to sit," Consuelo said. "He doesn't get to sit on my porch after what he did."

"Consuelo," Armando said.

"He spied on our daughter. Like a criminal. With a camera."

"With a phone," I said.

"Don't you correct me."

"Mom, let me handle it," Daniela said. She stood at the top of the steps with her arms crossed, looking down at me. "So. Are you happy? Daddy hasn't slept in three days. HR is investigating. Everybody at work is talking."

"Everybody at work was talking before I got there," I said. "That's why they stopped when I walked onto the patio."

"Nobody was talking!"

"Then why did you have to introduce me as the neighbor?"

"Because it was easier!"

"Than explaining why my husband shows up to a corporate event in Wranglers and a belt buckle! Everybody there has a husband who's a lawyer or a surgeon or owns a dealership. And I've got you. Mud on your boots. Dust in your hair. You don't fit, Pedro. You never fit."

"So you made me a neighbor," I said.

"So I made it simple."

"Thirty-one years, and I was simple to get rid of in one word."

"Oh, don't be dramatic."

"I'm the least dramatic man you've ever met. You told me that on our honeymoon."

"And it's true. You're boring. You're a boring man with a boring truck who takes boring pictures. And now you've got my father looking at them like they mean something."

Everybody turned.

"Daddy, it's not..."

"Yes."

Consuelo's mouth fell open. Daniela turned and went inside and let the screen door slam behind her, and the whole porch shook.

"It's my party. I decide what's a good idea."

On Friday I sat in the office of Bianca Ybarra on Beauregard Avenue with a paper cup of coffee going cold in my hand. Bianca's maybe five feet tall, she's practiced family law in Tom Green County for twenty-six years, and she talks like she's charging by the word, which, to be fair, she is.

"Texas is community property," she said. "Anything you two earned or bought during the marriage, a judge splits. Your rentals bought after 1995 are on the table."

"All four?"

"All four. But a Texas judge can give one side a bigger slice for fault. Adultery counts. So does spending community money on a lover, though in this case it sounds like she spent the company's money, which is the company's problem, not yours." She tapped her pen. "Your photos help. A witness would help more."

"I'm not putting anybody's job on the line."

"Noble. Expensive, but noble." She slid a letter across the desk to me. "Also, this came for you this morning. Addressed care of my office, which means somebody's already talking to a lawyer."

It was on the letterhead of a Dallas firm. It said that on behalf of Concho Valley Billing Services and its chief executive officer, Mr. Dorian Fairbanks, they demanded I immediately cease distributing "manipulated, misleading and defamatory images" of company employees, delete all copies, and sign the enclosed nondisclosure agreement within ten days, or face legal action.

"Manipulated," I said. "I can't even make my phone stop clicking."

Bianca laughed out loud, which I'd never heard her do.

"Truth is a defense to defamation in Texas," she said. "In plain words, you can't be sued for saying something that happened. And a picture of a hand on a back is not an opinion." The offices of Concho Valley Billing Services take up the third floor of a brick building on Chadbourne Street downtown. On the morning of the 20th, I sat on a padded bench in the hallway outside the boardroom in a pressed shirt and my good boots, with my phone on my knee and a cup of water from the cooler that I didn't drink.

People walked past me. A couple of them I recognized from the patio in Horseshoe Bay. One of them, a man in a golf shirt who I'm pretty sure was the CFO, slowed down when he saw me, then sped up again like he'd remembered an appointment.

"All in favor."

Five hands went up. Marisa's was the last, and she held it up the longest.

Dorian stood up so fast his chair rolled back and hit the wall.

"This is insane. This is a setup. She came on to me!" He pointed down the table at Daniela. "She's the one who kept booking Dallas. She's the one who said her husband was a nobody who'd never find out. I told her this was a bad idea."

Daniela came out of her chair.

"You liar. You told me you were leaving her by summer. You said you were going to run this company without her."

The room got very quiet.

Marisa took her glasses off.

"Without her," she said. "Well. That's one thing he got right."

She held out her hand, palm up.

"Your badge, Dorian. And the keys to the BMW. It's a company car. Toni will have somebody drive you home. Your things will be on the porch."

He stood there for a long moment with his mouth working. Then he pulled a key fob out of his jacket and set it in her hand, and he set his badge on top of it, and he walked out past me without looking at me. I heard his shoes going fast down the hall and the stairwell door bang.

"Mrs. Castillo," Toni said. "Regarding your complaint."

Daniela turned around slowly.

"Our retreat letter invited spouses. Your husband was listed on the RSVP that the company received from your home email address on March 2nd." Toni looked up. "Did you RSVP for him?"

Daniela didn't say anything.

"The complaint is closed as unfounded. Separately, you were the approving director on eleven of the twelve Dallas expense reports. That's a violation of section twelve as well."

"I was just signing what he told me to sign."

"Then you'll understand," Marisa said, very gently, "why we can't have you signing things anymore. Your last check will be mailed. Toni will walk you out."

Daniela looked at me then. Across the whole length of that table. And I'll be honest with you, I'd waited three weeks to see that look, and when it came, it didn't feel like anything. It felt like watching a wall come down that you already knew was rotten behind the paint.

I took my phone back from Toni and walked out ahead of her.

She caught up with me in the parking lot. She was carrying a cardboard box with a plant in it and the glass star from the retreat sitting on top.

"You did this," she said.

"I drove three hours to see my wife. Everything else, you did."

"You went to his wife!"

"I went to the woman who was paying for your hotel room."

"You think you won? You think this is over?" Her voice cracked. "Saturday is my parents' anniversary. Everybody's going to be there. My aunts, my cousins, the whole church. And I'm going to tell every single one of them what kind of man goes behind his wife's back to get her fired."

"Your father invited me."

"Then come," she said. "Come and hear it."

She put the box in the back of her Camry so hard the glass star cracked down the middle.

Saturday evening the backyard on Pecan Street looked just like it had in 1995. Christmas lights strung through the pecan trees, folding tables with white paper tablecloths, a keg in a trash can full of ice, and George Strait coming out of a speaker on the porch rail. About sixty people. Half the Reyes family had driven in from Del Rio and Eagle Pass.

I came in through the side gate with a gift bag and walked straight to Armando and Consuelo at the head table. Armando got up and hugged me. Consuelo didn't get up, but she didn't turn her face away either, which for Consuelo is practically a parade.

Lucia found me by the drinks.

"Dad. You came."

"Your grandpa told me to."

"Mom's here."

"I saw the Camry."

"She's going to do something. She's been in the bathroom with her cousin Yesenia for twenty minutes writing on an index card." Lucia pulled me a little way off, by the fence. "Dad, I need to tell you something before she does."

"Okay."

"Mom stayed at my place Thursday night after... after work. She left her iPad on my couch. It was open. I didn't go looking. I swear. A message popped up and I saw your name. Well. Not your name."

"What did it say?"

She took a breath.

"It was from Dorian. Back in October. It said, 'How's the neighbor?' And she wrote back, 'Still mowing.' With a laughing face." Lucia's eyes were wet. "Dad, there were dozens. All year. 'Is the neighbor home?' 'Neighbor's out of town, come over.' That's what they called you. For a year. It wasn't something she came up with at the patio. It was a joke they had."

I looked across the yard at the pecan trees and the lights.

"Okay," I said.

"Don't say okay. Please, Dad. Say something real."

"Something real?" I put my hand on the back of her neck the way I used to when she was little and scared of thunder. "Thank you for not picking a side until you knew. That's real."

That's when somebody tapped a fork on a glass.

Daniela stood up at the end of the head table with a plastic cup of wine and an index card. She was wearing the turquoise earrings I bought her in Santa Fe for our 25th.

"Hi, everybody," she said. "Thank you for coming. I want to make a toast to my parents. Forty-five years. Forty-five years of loyalty, of standing by each other." She looked at the card. "And I want to say, because you're all family, that this has been the hardest month of my life. Because loyalty is something not everybody understands. Some people, when they're jealous and insecure, they'll go behind your back to people you work for, and they'll cost you your career, and then they'll come to your parents' party and smile like nothing happened."

A few of the aunts looked at me. One of the cousins from Del Rio shook his head.

"So to my parents," Daniela said, raising her cup. "Who never had to wonder who was on their side."

"Mija."

Armando was standing up. He's slow getting up since the knee, but he was up, with one hand on the table.

"Sit down, Daniela."

"Daddy, I'm making a toast."

"You made it. Now sit." He turned to the yard. "My daughter is right about one thing. This has been a hard month. She's right that somebody went behind somebody's back." He looked at his daughter. "Tell them what you called your husband at that retreat."

"Daddy..."

"Tell them, or I will."

She stood there with the cup in her hand. The lights hummed in the trees.

"I called him my neighbor," she said finally. "It was a joke."

"A joke," Lucia said, from beside me.

Everybody turned. My daughter doesn't speak up in crowds. She stood next to me with her arms crossed and her chin up, and she looked exactly like her grandmother.

"It was a joke for a year, Mom," Lucia said. "'How's the neighbor?' 'Still mowing.' I saw it on your iPad. You and Dorian. All year." Her voice shook. "Dad was paying for the house you were sneaking him into. He was mowing the lawn. You were laughing about it."

Consuelo put her hand over her mouth.

Daniela's face went the color of the salsa on the buffet.

"Pedro," she said, and her voice wasn't the toast voice anymore. It was small. "Pedro, say something. Tell them it's not like that. Tell them I'm still your wife."

I set the gift bag down on the head table in front of Armando and Consuelo. Inside was a framed photo from 1995, this same backyard, these same Christmas lights, the two of them dancing.

"Happy anniversary," I said. "Forty-five years, and neither of you ever had to introduce the other one as somebody who lives down the street."

"Pedro, please."

"Goodnight, Daniela."

I turned to go back to the side gate. Armando caught my arm with his big slow hand and pulled out the empty chair next to him at the head of the table, the one with the paper place card that said Pedro in Consuelo's careful handwriting. I hadn't even seen it.

"No," the old man said, loud enough for every table in the yard to hear. "You sit up here, mijo. Family sits up here." He looked down the table at his daughter, standing alone with her plastic cup. "The neighbors can sit by the fence."
