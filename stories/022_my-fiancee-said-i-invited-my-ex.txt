"I invited my ex to the wedding. If you loved me, you'd understand."

My fiancée said that at a cake tasting in a bakery on Mill Mountain, in front of our wedding planner, her younger sister and the man who was about to sell us a four-tier lemon cake. Three people heard it, and all three of them stopped chewing.

I had a fork in my hand with a little square of raspberry filling on it. Seven weeks out. I had already written checks for $28,000 to the Hotel Roanoke, the florist, the band and this bakery, because Deborah wanted a real wedding this time, and so did I.

I set the fork down on the plate. There was a printed guest list on the table between the cake samples, with a pen clipped to it. I picked up the pen.

"I do understand," I said.

Then I found my own name on the list, Wendell Cross, groom, and right next to it, in my neat shop-teacher capital letters, I wrote PLUS ONE.

Forty-eight days later, at the rehearsal dinner, Lyle Garner's champagne flute slipped out of his hand and shattered on the parquet floor of the Regency Room, and forty people turned to see what he was staring at.

What Deborah didn't know was what Lyle's wife kept in a shoebox under the counter of her bakery in Lynchburg.

My name is Wendell Cross. I'm 61, and I've taught shop class at William Fleming High School in Roanoke, Virginia, for 34 years. I lost my first wife, Carol, to cancer eight years ago, and I didn't think I'd ever do this again. Thirty-four years of teenagers and table saws taught me one thing. When a joint doesn't fit, you don't beat it with a hammer. You stop, and you find out why. Hold on to that.

Deborah laughed a little, like I'd made a joke she didn't get.

"Plus one? Wendell, you're the groom. You don't get a plus one."

"Why not?"

"Because you're... because I'm your plus one."

"You invited a guest of your own. I figure I'm allowed one too."

Nicole Varner, our wedding planner, looked down at her iPad and suddenly got very busy scrolling. The baker, a big guy with flour on his forearms, quietly started stacking the sample plates. Deborah's sister Paige sat very still with her coffee cup halfway to her mouth.

"Who on earth would you invite?" Deborah asked.

"An old friend."

"What old friend? I know all your friends. Ray and the guys from the school and your cousin in Bedford."

"Not this one."

She stared at me. Deborah is 56, a real estate agent with her face on bus benches all over Roanoke County, and she has a way of tilting her head when she's not sure if she's winning a negotiation. She tilted it.

"Wendell, is this about Lyle? Because Lyle is ancient history. We dated in college. That was 1988. He's married. He's happily married. He just wants to wish me well."

"Then he'll wish you well."

"So you're fine with it?"

"I said I understand."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," I said. "It's not."

I went back to the cake and ate the raspberry square. It was very good. I told the baker so, and he smiled at me the way men smile at each other at a funeral.

Deborah picked the lemon with buttercream. She chattered the rest of the tasting about the color of the ribbons and whether the cake topper looked cheap, and she kept glancing at me like she was trying to read a price tag from across the room. I kept my face plain. I iron my jeans with a crease, a habit my dad drilled into me in the Army, and I keep my face the same way. Neat and flat.

Out in the parking lot, Deborah got in her Lexus to go show a house in Salem and blew me a kiss through the windshield. I waved. Then I heard footsteps on the gravel behind me.

Paige. She's 52, a nurse at Carilion, and she's always been the quiet one in that family. She had her car keys in her fist like she was ready to use them.

"Wendell. Can I say something without you ever telling her I said it?"

"Go ahead."

"Did you look at the invitation list? The real one, the one Nicole mailed?"

"Not closely."

"Look at Lyle's line." She bit her lip. "It just says Mr. Lyle Garner. No 'and guest.' No 'Mr. and Mrs.' Deb addressed it herself. She told Nicole his wife couldn't make it." Paige looked over her shoulder at the bakery door. "His wife never got invited, Wendell. Nobody ever asked her."

I met Paige the next morning at the Texas Tavern downtown, the little hot dog counter on Church Avenue that's been open since 1930 and has ten stools and no booths. I like it because you can't have a long, sad conversation at the Texas Tavern. There's no room for one.

We sat at the end of the counter and ordered two cheesy westerns and two coffees.

"You can't tell her I'm doing this," Paige said again.

"I won't."

"I mean it. She's my sister. I love her. But she's done this her whole life. She gets something good and then she has to go poke at it with a stick to see if something better jumps out."

"Tell me about Lyle."

Paige sighed. "Lyle Garner. Virginia Tech, class of '88. He was the big man on campus, played intramural everything, laughed at his own jokes louder than anybody else did. He dumped her right before graduation for a girl from Richmond. Deb cried for a year. Then she married Gary, then she divorced Gary, then she met you."

"And now?"

"Three years ago, Lyle found her on Facebook. Just a 'Hey stranger, you look great.' And then they started having lunch. Every few months. He drives over from Lynchburg, they meet at the Roanoker Restaurant, he tells her she was the one that got away." Paige stirred her coffee with a little red straw. "She told me it was harmless. She said it made her feel 25."

"Did you believe her?"

"I believed she believed it." Paige put the straw down. "Wendell, she keeps every card anybody ever gives her. You know that? She's got a drawer. Birthday cards, Christmas cards, sympathy cards from when Mom's dog died in 1997. She can't throw away a card."

"I know. She's got the one I gave her when I proposed."

"She's also got three from Lyle. Last three birthdays. I saw them in the drawer at Christmas when I was looking for tape." Paige looked at me. "He signs them 'always.' Just 'always, L.'"

The cook slapped our cheesy westerns down on the counter. I didn't touch mine.

"What's his wife's name?"

Paige hesitated. "Why?"

"Because somebody ought to ask her if she understands."

Paige was quiet a moment. Then she said, "Rosalind. Everybody calls her Roz. She owns a bakery in Lynchburg. Sweet Roz, on Rivermont Avenue. Deb made fun of it once. Said Lyle married a woman who smells like cinnamon rolls." She took a bite of her hot dog and chewed. "I always thought that sounded kind of nice."

"So did I," I said.

I drove to Lynchburg on Saturday. It's an hour east on 460, past the Peaks of Otter and a lot of cows, and I spent the whole drive with my hands at ten and two and the radio on the oldies station out of Lynchburg, which played "Born to Run" just as I came over the last hill. I took that as a sign, though I couldn't have told you of what.

Sweet Roz was a narrow brick storefront with a striped awning and a line out the door. I waited in it like everybody else. When I got to the counter, the woman behind it was about 57, with flour on her glasses, her gray hair pinned up with a pencil, and a face that looked like it laughed a lot and hadn't lately.

"What can I get you, hon?"

"Are you Roz?"

She wiped her hands on her apron. "Who's asking?"

"My name's Wendell Cross. I'm marrying Deborah Wills in seven weeks."

I watched it go across her face. It didn't go fast, and it didn't go loud. She just set the tongs down very carefully on the glass, and she called back into the kitchen without taking her eyes off me.

"Teresa, take the counter. I'm going on my break."

We sat out back on two milk crates by the dumpster, in the shade of a big maple, with two cups of her coffee. Roz lit a cigarette and then didn't smoke it, just held it.

"Lyle told me this weekend was a golf trip," she said. "The weekend of the twentieth. With the boys from the Rotary. Up at the Homestead."

"It's not a golf trip."

"No. I didn't think it was." She tapped the ash. "How'd you find me?"

"Her sister. And your awning."

She almost smiled. "So she invited him. To her wedding. Without me."

"She said if I loved her, I'd understand."

Roz laughed then, a short, rough laugh with no fun in it. "That's funny. Lyle says the same thing to me. Every time. 'Roz, if you trusted me, you'd understand.'"

She stood up, went inside, and came back with a Nike shoebox. She set it on the milk crate between us and took the lid off.

Cards. A whole stack of them, with a rubber band around them. Birthday cards, Valentine's cards, a Christmas card with a cartoon reindeer on it. She pulled the top one out and handed it to me.

It was a sappy card with a sunset on it. Inside, in a woman's loopy handwriting I'd seen on a hundred grocery lists on my own refrigerator, it said:

"Happy birthday to the one that got away. Every lunch is the best day of my month. Always yours, D."

"Found them in his golf bag in the spring," Roz said. "In the zipper pocket where he keeps his tees. I never said a word. I didn't know who D was. I just kept them. I figured someday I'd need to know." She took the card back from me and put it on top of the stack. "Now I know."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. You didn't write them." She looked at me hard. "So what is it you want, Wendell Cross? You drove an hour to show me you know about my husband. You didn't have to."

"I want you to come to the rehearsal dinner. As my plus one."

She looked at me for a long, long time. A delivery truck backed up somewhere behind the building, beeping.

"Why?"

"Because she invited your husband to my wedding and decided you didn't get a say. I figure you ought to get a say."

Roz put the lid back on the shoebox. She tapped it twice with her fingernail.

"I'm going to need a new dress," she said.

"I'll pay for it."

"You will not. I own a bakery." She stood up. Then she stopped with her hand on the screen door. "Wendell. Do you want to know whose idea the invitation actually was?"

"I figured it was Deb's."

"It wasn't." She pulled her phone out of her apron pocket and showed me a credit card statement on the screen, the joint one she and Lyle shared. One line was highlighted. Hotel Roanoke and Conference Center. Two nights. Friday and Saturday. The weekend of my wedding. Booked three months ago. "Lyle booked a room for both nights. A king suite. Did Deborah tell you where she's spending the night before her wedding?"

"At the hotel. In the bridal suite. Alone. It's a tradition, she said."

Roz nodded slowly, like I'd just confirmed a recipe.

"I called the front desk last week and asked which floor that king suite is on," she said. "Fourth floor. Same as the bridal suite. Two doors down."

I didn't say a word to Deborah for four weeks. Not one.

I'm not going to pretend that was easy either. I went to work. I taught a bunch of sophomores how to square up a cut on a miter saw without losing a thumb. I went to her open house on Sunday and stood in the kitchen of a split-level in Cave Spring handing out cookies to strangers. I picked out a tie. I let her pick out a different tie.

Every few days, she said it. Not on purpose. It just came out of her, the way a habit does.

When I asked why she needed to have lunch with a client in Lynchburg on a Thursday, she said, "It's a referral, Wendell. If you trusted me, you'd understand."

When I asked why her phone was always face down on the counter now, she said, "Because the light's annoying. If you loved me, you'd understand."

Once, in bed, with the lamp off, she rolled over and put her hand on my chest and said, "You've been quiet. You're not still upset about Lyle, are you? It's one guest, babe. One. If you loved me, you'd understand."

"I understand," I said.

"Good," she said. And she was asleep in five minutes. I wasn't asleep at all.

My best friend Ray Tolbert came over that Saturday to help me fix the gutter on the back of my house. Ray is 63, a retired football coach from Patrick Henry High, and he has a voice like a gravel truck and the subtlety of one too. He stood at the bottom of the ladder holding it, which is to say he stood at the bottom of the ladder drinking my beer.

"So you're really going through with this," he said.

"Going through with what?"

"Don't play dumb with me, Wendell. Paige told my wife, my wife told me. You're bringing the man's wife to your rehearsal dinner."

"Ray, hand me that bracket."

"Here's your bracket. Answer the question."

"I'm bringing an old friend."

"You met her three weeks ago behind a dumpster."

"We had a lot in common."

Ray laughed so hard the ladder shook. "Lord. I've known you since 1987. You're the most boring man in Roanoke. You eat the same lunch every day. You iron your jeans. And now you're running some kind of soap opera."

"I'm not running anything. I'm just not stopping anything."

"What's the difference?"

I came down the ladder and wiped my hands on a rag.

"The difference, Ray, is I'm not going to yell. I'm not going to call him. I'm not going to post anything or tell her mother or throw her ring in the river. She invited a guest. I invited a guest. That's all. Whatever happens when they're in the same room is up to them."

Ray looked at me for a long minute, the way he used to look at a quarterback who'd just told him a play would work.

"And if she breaks down and tells you it's all a mistake and she loves you?"

"Then she'll have to tell it to Roz first."

That evening Deborah came over with the final seating chart. She spread it out on my dining room table like a house listing, with little round tables drawn in pencil and names written in each one. She'd put Lyle at table three, with her college roommates. Close to the head table. Facing it.

Then her finger stopped at the head table, on my side.

"Wendell Cross," she read. "Plus one."

"Plus one."

"Nicole says you still haven't given her a name. She needs a name for the place card."

"Put down 'Roz.'"

"Roz who?"

"Just Roz. She's a baker."

Deborah tilted her head. "I've never heard you talk about a Roz."

"We go back."

"How far back?"

"Not as far as you and Lyle."

She looked at me sharply. For a second I thought she was going to push. I thought she was going to say, "Who is this woman?" and I'd have to decide right there whether to lie, and I don't lie, so it would have all come out on my dining room table next to a pencil drawing of table three.

But Deborah only laughed, a bright, short laugh.

"Okay, Mister Mysterious. You're allowed one old friend. If I loved you, I'd understand, right?"

"Right," I said.

She wrote ROZ on the place card in her loopy handwriting, the same handwriting as the card in the shoebox, and slid it into the pile.

Then her phone buzzed on the table. She glanced at it and smiled before she could stop herself, and she flipped it face down.

"Who's that?"

"Nobody. Work." She gathered up the chart. Then she stopped. "Oh, and Lyle says he might bring somebody after all. His sister. Is that okay? Nicole will need to add a chair."

His sister. His sister, who would need a chair, at a wedding where his wife had never been invited. I thought about that king suite on the fourth floor, two doors down.

"Tell Nicole to add a chair," I said. "The more the merrier."

The rehearsal dinner was on a Friday in the Regency Room at the Hotel Roanoke, an old Tudor-style hotel that sits on the hill above the railroad yards, with chandeliers and dark wood and pictures of steam engines on the walls. Forty guests. Both our families, the wedding party, her college roommates, two of my teacher buddies, Ray and his wife, Paige. A string trio from the Roanoke Symphony playing Hall and Oates songs slow, because Deborah thought that was clever.

I got there early. Roz met me in the lobby.

She'd bought the dress. It was dark green, plain, nice, the kind of dress a woman buys when she wants to look like she's not trying, and she had her hair down instead of pinned up with a pencil. She had a little purse on her arm. A purse just big enough for a stack of cards with a rubber band around them.

"You look nice," I said.

"I look like a woman who's about to blow up her life," Roz said. "But thank you."

"You can still go home."

"Wendell, I drove an hour in pantyhose. I'm not going home."

"Did he leave yet?"

"Left at three. Told me he'd call from the Homestead. Kissed me on the forehead." She checked her watch. "His sister is in Tampa, by the way. She doesn't know anything about this. I called her this morning."

"So who's the extra chair for?"

"I have no idea. I guess we'll find out."

We walked into the Regency Room together. Roz's hand was on my arm. Deborah was at the far end of the room in a white sheath dress, laughing with two of her roommates, and she looked over and saw me and smiled, and then she saw the woman on my arm and the smile stayed on her face, a little puzzled, a little polite. She'd never seen Roz in her life. She didn't know who she was looking at.

But Lyle did.

He was by the bar with a glass of champagne, tall and tan with a full head of silver hair, telling some story to my cousin from Bedford and laughing at it before he got to the end. He turned around to grab a napkin. He saw Roz.

The champagne glass just slid out of his fingers. It hit the parquet floor and burst like a light bulb, and the string trio stopped in the middle of "Rich Girl," and forty people turned around to see what Lyle Garner was staring at.

"Hi, honey," Roz said. "How's the golf?"

Nobody moved. A waiter came running with a towel and then saw everybody's faces and stopped running.

Deborah came across the room fast, in her heels, with her hostess smile on.

"Wendell, who's your friend?"

"Deb, this is Roz," I said. "My plus one."

"Roz Garner," Roz said, and she held out her hand. "Lyle's wife. Thirty-one years in June. I don't believe I got an invitation, so Wendell was kind enough to bring me."

Deborah didn't take the hand. She looked at it like it was something the cat dragged in. Then she looked at me.

"You... you did this?"

"You invited your guest. I invited mine. If you loved me, you'd understand."

I said it quiet and plain, exactly the way she'd said it to me at the bakery. Paige, at the table behind Deborah, put her hand over her mouth.

Lyle came over now, stepping around the broken glass, with his hands out in front of him like he was trying to calm a horse.

"Roz. Honey. Let's go outside and talk about this."

"We can talk right here, Lyle."

"This is not the place."

"Funny. It was the place for you."

"Roz, it's a wedding. Deb's an old friend. I just came to wish her well."

"With a king suite on the fourth floor? Two doors down from the bride?"

That one landed. I watched it land on half the people in the room. Deborah's mother, a tiny woman in a lavender jacket named Alice, sat down in the nearest chair like somebody had cut her strings.

"Mom," Deborah said. "Mom, she's lying. She's some crazy woman."

"I'm a crazy woman with a shoebox," Roz said.

She opened her little purse and took out the stack of cards with the rubber band. She took the rubber band off and laid them on the white tablecloth of the head table, one after another, like a hand of solitaire. A birthday card with a sunset. A Valentine with a red heart. A Christmas card with a cartoon reindeer.

"I found these in his golf bag in April," Roz said, to the room, not loud, just clear. "I didn't know who D was. I do now." She picked up the sunset card and opened it, and she read it in a flat voice, the way you'd read a recipe. "'Happy birthday to the one that got away. Every lunch is the best day of my month. Always yours, D.'"

Every head in the room turned to Deborah.

"That's... those are old," Deborah said. "Those are from years ago. Lyle, tell her."

"That one's postmarked last March," Roz said. "It's got the stamp with the flowers on it."

Lyle didn't say anything. He was looking at his shoes, his tan going a little gray.

"Lyle," Deborah said again, and her voice cracked. "Tell her."

"Deb," Lyle said. "Maybe you should tell him."

"Tell him what?"

"Tell him that I asked you to call off the wedding. Last week. At the Roanoker. And you said you'd think about it."

It was so quiet I could hear the elevators ding out in the lobby.

Deborah turned to me, and her face had come completely apart. The hostess smile was gone. There was nothing left but a woman of 56 in a white dress in a room full of people, trying to work out which lie was still standing.

"Wendell, I said no. I told him no. I was going to marry you. I was always going to marry you."

"With his room two doors down."

"That was his idea! I didn't book that room!"

"But you knew about it," Paige said quietly from behind her.

Deborah spun around. "Paige!"

"You told me, Deb. In the car. You said it was just to talk. You said if anybody loved you, they'd understand."

Deborah looked at her sister like she'd never seen her before.

I looked around the room. At Alice in her lavender jacket. At Ray, who had his big arms crossed and was nodding at me slowly. At the string trio holding their bows in the air. At the waiter with his towel.

"I want to apologize to all of you," I said. "Most of you drove a long way. The food's paid for and it's good food, so please eat it. The bar's open till ten. That's on me too." I took a breath. "There won't be a wedding tomorrow."

"Wendell," Deborah said. "Wendell, you can't do this. Not like this. Not in front of everybody."

"You invited him in front of everybody."

"I'll fix it. I'll make him leave. Lyle, leave. Get out."

"He can stay," I said. "It's his wife's plus one who's leaving."

Roz was already picking up her cards and putting the rubber band back on them, one by one, steady as a woman packing cookies in a box. She put them in her purse. Then she reached over and picked up Lyle's car keys off the bar, where he'd set them down next to his napkin. She jingled them once.

"We came in my car this morning, Lyle," she said. "You told me yours needed brakes. So I'll be taking these."

"Roz, how am I supposed to get home?"

"You booked a room," she said. "Two nights."

I walked her out. Behind us, Deborah's voice was going high and thin, calling my name, and then calling her mother, and then just calling.

In the lobby, under a big oil painting of a steam engine, Roz stopped and handed me back my arm.

"Thank you," she said. "That was the best rehearsal dinner I ever attended."

"I'm sorry about your marriage."

"Don't be. It ended in April. Tonight was just the paperwork." She looked toward the Regency Room. "You'd better go home and turn off your phone, Wendell. Because that woman in there is not the kind who lets something go."

As if she'd heard, my phone buzzed in my jacket. I pulled it out.

One missed call. Deborah. Then it buzzed again before I could put it away.

I drove home with the phone face down on the passenger seat and Hall and Oates still stuck in my head. By the time I pulled into my driveway in Raleigh Court, the little red number on the phone said 11. By the time I'd hung up my suit, it said 19. I made a grilled cheese at midnight and ate it standing over the sink, and while I ate it, it went to 26.

There were texts too. I read them in order, the way you read a weather report when a storm's coming.

"Please call me."

"It's not what she said."

"You humiliated me in front of my entire family."

"I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. Please."

"You planned this. You planned this for WEEKS. Who does that?"

"I love you. Only you. Lyle means nothing."

I typed one answer and sent it.

"I'm going to bed. Good night, Deb."

Then I turned the phone off for the first time since Carol died, and I slept nine hours straight, which I hadn't done in a month.

Saturday, the day we should have been married, her mother came to my porch.

Alice Wills is 79 and about the size of a fourth grader, and she rang my doorbell at ten in the morning and waited there in her lavender jacket with her purse held in front of her with both hands. I let her in and made her coffee. She sat on the edge of my couch like she was afraid it might bite.

"I came to ask you to forgive her," she said.

"I know you did."

"She's my daughter. She's been crying since last night. She didn't sleep. Paige stayed with her."

"I'm sorry she's crying, Alice."

"She says it was all a misunderstanding. She says she was going to tell Lyle no. That she was going to tell him for good, tonight, after the wedding."

"After the wedding."

Alice looked down at her coffee.

"I know how it sounds," she said.

"How does it sound to you?"

She was quiet a long time. Then she put the coffee down on my coffee table and folded her hands on her purse.

"It sounds like her father," she said. "He used to say he'd stop. After Christmas. After the kids were older. After his mother passed." She looked up at me. "He never stopped, Wendell. I waited 22 years for after."

"I'm not going to wait for after."

"No," Alice said. "I don't think you should." She stood up and smoothed her lavender jacket. At the door, she put her small hand on my arm. "Thank you for not yelling. Last night. I've been in a lot of rooms where a man yelled. You didn't."

Sunday, Deborah came herself.

She rang the bell for forty minutes. She had a bag from Fork in the Alley, the barbecue place I like on Sunday nights, with my usual order in it, brisket and the mac and cheese, and she held it up to the little window in my front door so I could see it. I sat in my kitchen and graded sophomore shop projects, little wooden birdhouses, and I gave a kid named Tavon a B-plus because his roof was crooked but his corners were perfect.

At one point she called through the mail slot.

"Wendell, I know you're in there. I can see your truck. I brought your brisket."

I didn't answer.

"If you loved me, you'd open this door!"

I wrote "Nice joints, Tavon" on the bottom of the birdhouse and set it on the stack.

After forty minutes the doorbell stopped. When I finally looked, the barbecue bag was sitting on my top step. I brought it in, because it's a sin to waste brisket, and I put it in the refrigerator.

My phone said 63.

Monday, Lyle Garner called me.

I almost didn't pick up. It was a Lynchburg number I didn't know. But I was curious, and I'm human.

"Wendell? It's Lyle. Lyle Garner."

"I know who you are."

"Look, man, I just want to say, this got out of hand. I never wanted to hurt anybody."

"You booked a king suite two doors down from my fiancée the night before my wedding, Lyle."

"I know how it looks."

"Everybody keeps telling me they know how it looks."

He laughed, that big laugh he did at his own jokes, and then he heard himself doing it and stopped.

"Roz changed the locks," he said. "On the house. And the bakery. She took the car. She took the dog. I'm staying at a Days Inn in Lynchburg. She won't take my calls."

"That sounds familiar."

"I'm calling because Deb told me to call you. She thinks if I tell you it was all my fault, you'll take her back."

"Was it all your fault?"

There was a pause on the line, and I could hear a TV in the background, some game show.

"No," Lyle said finally. "She's the one who said I should come. I asked if I should bring Roz, as cover. She said no, she didn't want Roz there. She wanted me to see her in that dress. She said I'd been looking at her in my head for thirty years and I ought to see it for real." He blew out a breath. "And that extra chair, the sister thing? That was her idea too. So nobody would wonder why I booked two nights. There's no sister. My sister lives in Tampa and doesn't even know I'm alive."

"Why are you telling me this, Lyle?"

"Because I'm in a Days Inn, man," Lyle said. "And she's still telling everybody I'm the bad guy."

I hung up. I'm not proud of that. I just didn't have anything else to say to him.

Tuesday, she texted that she was having chest pains.

"I'm at home and my chest is so tight I can't breathe. Please come. I'm scared. I don't want to call 911."

My hand was on my truck keys before I thought about it. Then I stopped, and I called Paige instead. Paige is a nurse. Paige answered on the first ring.

"She's sitting next to me on her couch eating Cheez-Its," Paige said, very quietly. "Wendell, she's fine. She's watching Dateline."

"Okay."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry, Paige. You're the only one in that family who keeps telling me the truth."

"Somebody has to," Paige said. "It's exhausting."

Wednesday the honeymoon came due.

We'd booked two weeks in Hawaii, Maui and the Big Island, through a travel agent in Salem, and I'd put down the deposit, $4,200, on my credit card in the spring. Deborah called the agent on Wednesday and tried to change the booking to her name only, with a guest to be named later. The agent, a sharp older woman named Dolores, called me right after.

"Mr. Cross, your fiancée wants me to switch the Maui trip into her name. It's on your card. I can't do that without your say-so. What do you want me to do?"

"Cancel it, please, Dolores. Whatever's refundable, refund it."

"And the nonrefundable part?"

I thought about it. I thought about Carol, who always wanted to see the ocean from a sailboat and never got to, because I always said next year.

"Is there anything in there I can use myself?"

Dolores laughed. "There's a sunset sailing trip off Lahaina that's prepaid. Two seats. You'd have to go by October."

"I'll take one seat," I said. "Give the other one to anybody who wants it."

That night Deborah sent one more text.

"You canceled our honeymoon?!?! You canceled HAWAII?!?!"

And I answered it.

"Our honeymoon was for a wedding. There's no wedding."

Thursday it was Nicole Varner, the wedding planner, who called me from her car. I could hear her blinker going.

"Mr. Cross, I'm in an awkward spot. Deborah came by my office this morning. She wants me to send the vendor release forms to her, so the refunds from the hotel, the florist and the band come back to her account. She says the wedding was her event."

"Who wrote the checks, Nicole?"

"You did. Every one. I have the copies right here."

"Then the refunds come back to the checking account they came out of. Mine."

"That's what I told her." Nicole sighed. "She said, and I'm sorry, I'm only repeating it, that if you'd ever really loved her, you'd let her have it. She said it would show good faith."

"Tell her I said good faith goes both ways."

"I'll tell her." The blinker stopped. "For what it's worth, Mr. Cross, I've done 300 weddings. I've seen a lot of rehearsal dinners. That one's going to be the one I tell my grandkids about."

"Which part?"

"The part where nobody yelled," Nicole said. "I didn't know that was possible."

The refunds came back the next week, $17,000 of the $28,000, to my account. The cake was nonrefundable. The bakery on Mill Mountain sent me a note saying they'd donated the lemon cake to the Rescue Mission downtown, and that the men there said it was the best thing they'd eaten all year. I kept the note. Maybe Deborah wasn't the only one who saves paper.

Then nothing for three weeks. Not a call, not a text. I heard from Paige that Deborah had gone to stay with Alice, that she'd told her book club I'd been abusive and cold, and that one of the women in that book club had been at the rehearsal dinner and had simply said, "Deb, I was there," and the book club had changed the subject to the new Grisham. I heard from Ray that Lyle had moved into an apartment over a tire shop in Lynchburg. I heard from nobody about Roz, and I didn't ask, because that was hers.

I went back to school. I taught kids to square a board. I went sailing in October, alone, off Lahaina, and an old Hawaiian captain asked me where my wife was, and I told him she was gone, and he said, "Ah. Mine too," and we didn't talk any more after that, and it was the best afternoon I'd had in a year.

The week after I got back was the fall open house at William Fleming High School.

Every year, the shop room fills up with parents and grandparents walking around looking at the projects on the benches, the birdhouses and the cutting boards and the jewelry boxes, and the kids stand next to them looking proud and embarrassed. It smells like sawdust and coffee from the cafeteria. I love it more than Christmas.

I was standing at Tavon's bench, telling his grandmother about his corners, when I looked up and saw Deborah in the doorway.

She was in a blue blazer, with her hair done, her realtor's smile on, holding a folder against her chest. She looked thinner. She walked right into the shop room, past the drill press, past twenty parents and thirty kids, and she stopped three feet from me.

"Wendell."

"Deb."

Tavon's grandmother looked from her to me and quietly took Tavon by the elbow and moved off toward the lathe.

"I'm sorry to do this here," Deborah said. "You won't answer my calls. I didn't know where else to find you."

"You found me."

"I want to try again." She said it fast, like she'd practiced it in the car. "I've been in counseling. Three weeks. I've cut Lyle off completely. I blocked him. I even threw away the cards, Wendell. All three of them. You know I never throw away a card."

"I know."

"I'm not the same person I was at that dinner."

"You were that person for three years. The dinner was just the night everybody saw it."

A couple of the parents had stopped pretending to look at birdhouses. A dad by the band saw was holding his phone, not filming, just holding it like he wasn't sure what to do with his hands.

Deborah lowered her voice. "Please. Not in front of all these people."

"You picked the room, Deb."

"I picked it because it's the only place you can't walk away from me."

"It's my classroom," I said. "I can walk wherever I want in it."

Her eyes filled up. She opened the folder she was holding. Inside was the place card. The little white card from the head table at the Regency Room, with ROZ written on it in her loopy hand, and under it the card that said DEBORAH. She'd kept them both. Of course she had.

"I kept these," she said. "I keep everything. Because I knew someday we'd laugh about it. Someday it'd be a story we tell. Please, Wendell. One more chance. If you ever loved me... you'd understand."

There it was. The sixth time. Maybe the seventh. I had stopped counting somewhere around the barbecue.

I took the folder from her hands, very gently. I closed it. I handed it back.

"I loved you, Deb," I said. "That's exactly why I understood."

Then I turned around, went back to Tavon's bench, and held out my hand to his grandmother.

"Ma'am, I'm sorry for the interruption. Now, let me show you how your grandson cut these corners, because I've been teaching this class 34 years, and I have never once seen a joint fit that clean."
