Fifty-eight years that car had been in my family. My grandfather bought it new off the lot on Central Avenue the summer he came home from the Army. And the woman I'd been living with for two years had sold it while I was at work to pay for a trip to Mexico she called "content."

My name is Martin Sandoval. I'm 47, and I'm an elevator mechanic in Albuquerque, New Mexico. Union, twenty-four years. Hospitals, hotels, office buildings, the old courthouse downtown. The hearing was six weeks after the day she sold the car, on a Tuesday morning, in a courtroom on the fourth floor of the Second Judicial District Court on Lomas Boulevard.

Her mother, Irma, sat in the first row right behind her, with her arms folded and her mouth set.

The lawyers had already worked it out. Arviso had given his statement. Benito's photographs were in the file. The napkin with my signature practiced on it six times in Colt's handwriting was in a plastic evidence sleeve on the clerk's desk.

Colt pleaded first. Forgery, a fourth-degree felony, and receiving stolen property. Eighteen months, with credit for time served and the Arizona hold to sort out after. When the judge asked if he had anything to say, he stood up in his orange jumpsuit and said, "Your Honor, I just want to say I was only trying to help her build her brand."

Judge Atencio pulled her glasses down and looked at him over them.

"Mr. Amador," she said, "you helped yourself to nine thousand dollars of a dead man's car. Sit down."

He sat down.

Then it was Veronica's turn.

She pleaded guilty to forgery. Because it was her first offense, because she'd come in on her own and told the truth about Colt, and because I'd told the DA I didn't need her to go to prison, the judge gave her a conditional discharge. Probation for two years. If she stayed clean and paid every cent, the felony would come off her record.

"Now," Judge Atencio said, "restitution."

She went down the list. The tow and impound fees on my car, paid by Ms. Lujan. The two airline tickets to Cancun, already disputed and refunded by my bank, so nothing owed there. Fourteen thousand dollars to Mr. Arviso, jointly and severally, from Ms. Lujan and Mr. Amador, since he'd paid it for a car they had no right to sell.

Denny Arviso looked like Christmas had come early.

"And one more item," the judge said. She looked down at a sheet of paper. "A charge of three thousand eight hundred and ninety dollars at Nordstrom, Coronado Center, on the victim's credit card, for one designer handbag. Not disputed by the victim." She looked up. "Ms. Lujan, is that the bag?"

Veronica's hands tightened on the strap.

"Yes, Your Honor."

"Has it been used?"

"Once," Veronica whispered. "In Tulum. For pictures."

"Is the receipt still in it?"

Veronica opened the bag with shaking fingers and looked inside. She pulled out a folded white slip of paper with a tissue still stuck to it.

"Yes, Your Honor."

"Mr. Sandoval's statement says he declined to dispute that charge so there'd be a record of what was purchased with his money." The judge glanced at me in the gallery. "That was smart, sir." She looked back at Veronica. "Ms. Lujan, you will surrender that handbag and the receipt to the bailiff today. The victim's counsel will return it for a refund to the victim's card. If the store won't take it back, it will be sold and the proceeds applied to what you owe."

"Today?" Veronica said.

"Right now," said Judge Atencio.

The courtroom was very quiet. Veronica stood up. She looked at the bag for a long moment, the cream-colored leather with the gold clasp that she'd held up to a glass counter six weeks ago and photographed and sent to me with two words.

Then she walked to the clerk's desk and set it down next to the plastic sleeve with the napkin in it.

The bailiff picked it up and put it in a big brown evidence bag, and folded the top over, and wrote a number on it with a black marker.

"Anything further?" the judge asked.

"No, Your Honor," said the DA.

"Then we're adjourned."

People stood up. Chairs scraped. Colt was led out the side door without looking back. Denny Arviso came over and tried to shake my hand again, and again I didn't take it, and again he didn't seem to mind.

Veronica stopped at the little wooden gate between the tables and the gallery. Her public defender was tugging at her elbow, but she stood there looking at me.

"Is it back?" she asked. "The car?"

"They released it Friday."

"Is it okay?"

"Benito checked it over. Not a scratch."

She nodded. Her eyes were wet. "I'm sorry, Martin. I'm sorry I said he didn't care. Your grandpa."

"He'd have prayed for you," I said. "Then he'd have changed the code on the safe."

Benito laughed out loud in the courtroom, and the bailiff gave him a look, and he covered his mouth with his old brown hand like a schoolboy.

We rode the elevator down without anybody saying much. It stopped on every floor, slow and steady, and I listened to the brake set at each one the way I always do.

The Mustang was parked at a meter out on Lomas, candy apple red in the hard New Mexico sun, with a parking ticket already flapping under the wiper. Tomas took one look and started laughing.

"Boss," he said. "Do you want to dispute that one?"

I pulled the ticket off the windshield, folded it, and put it in my shirt pocket.

"No," I said. "I want a record of it."
