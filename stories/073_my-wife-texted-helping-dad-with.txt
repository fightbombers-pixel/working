"Helping Dad with his tax papers, will be late."

I read my wife's text out loud at my own kitchen table, at a little after six on a Thursday, to the only other person sitting there. Her father. Edgar Tillery, eighty-four years old, a napkin tucked in his collar and a piece of my fried catfish halfway to his mouth.

He'd driven himself over from his horse farm at five o'clock, because it would've been his wife Ruth's eighty-second birthday, and catfish was what Ruth always made on her birthday. Maxine hadn't called him in nineteen days. He'd counted.

Edgar set his fork down.

"Read that again," he said.

I read it again. Then I turned the phone around on the table so he could see it with his own eyes, and I slid it across the oilcloth to him, next to the hush puppies.

He put his reading glasses on and looked at it for a long time.

"Chester," he said. "I'm right here."

"I know you are, Edgar."

"I haven't seen her since the Fourth of July picnic."

"I know."

Forty minutes later, my wife would walk through my back door, find her father sitting at my kitchen table, and drop her car keys on the linoleum.

What she didn't know was what Edgar had brought over in a Publix grocery sack, sitting on the chair next to him. A blue folder he'd found on his own kitchen counter three weeks ago, after her last visit. A folder with a Gainesville law firm's name across the top.

My name is Chester Poole. I'm sixty-two, and I climbed poles and fixed power lines for Florida Power for thirty-eight years, mostly right here in Marion County, mostly in the middle of storms. Thirty-eight years on the line teaches you one thing. Before you touch a wire, you test it. You never trust anybody who tells you a line is dead. You find out for yourself. Hold on to that.

Edgar pushed the phone back across the table to me.

"Tax papers," he said. "You do my taxes."

"Every year since 2002."

"She knows that."

"She knows that."

He picked his fork back up and ate the bite of catfish, and chewed it slowly, and swallowed. Ruth used to say Edgar was the only man in Florida who could eat through a hurricane. He was eating through this one too. Then he reached over to the Publix sack and took out the blue folder and laid it on the table between us.

"I was going to ask you about this after supper," he said. "Now I think I'll ask you before dessert."

I opened it.

The top sheet was on letterhead from a law firm in Gainesville. Hume, Baird and Lattner, Elder Law and Estate Planning. It was a draft. Durable Power of Attorney, naming Maxine Tillery Poole as agent for Edgar R. Tillery, with full authority over all real property, bank accounts and investments. There was a yellow sign-here flag next to Edgar's name.

Under that was a second document. A form called a Petition to Determine Incapacity, with Edgar's name already typed in, and Maxine's.

And under that was a listing agreement from a company called Prentice Equine Realty, for the sale of forty acres known as Tillery Farm, off County Road 225. The broker's signature was already on it. Ashton Prentice.

I knew that name. Everybody in Ocala who's ever looked at a horse farm knows that name. His face is on a billboard on State Road 40, in pressed jeans and a cowboy hat, grinning over the words SELLING THE HORSE CAPITAL OF THE WORLD.

"She left it on my counter," Edgar said. "Under the fruit bowl. She must have forgot it. I don't think I was supposed to see it."

"Did she ask you to sign anything?"

"She said she'd bring 'some papers' next time. To help me 'get organized.'" He took his glasses off. "Chester, what's a petition to determine incapacity?"

I didn't want to tell him. But Edgar Tillery had been a farrier and a horseman for sixty years, and he could tell when a horse was lying about a sore foot. He could tell when I was going to lie, too.

"It means somebody's asking a judge to say you can't take care of yourself anymore," I said. "So they can take care of your things for you."

He sat very still.

"I'm eighty-four," he said. "I'm not dead."

"No, sir."

"I shod three horses on Tuesday."

"I know you did."

The back door opened. Maxine came in.

She had her purse on her shoulder and her car keys in her hand, and she was halfway through saying "Sorry, Chester, it ran long," when she saw who was sitting at the table.

The keys hit the linoleum.

I didn't plan what I said next. I want to be honest about that. It just came out, the way things do after thirty-two years.

I smiled at her and said, "That was fast. Done with him already?"

Maxine stared at me.

And Edgar blinked up at his daughter over his reading glasses, with my catfish on his plate and her lawyer's papers under his hand, and he said, "What tax papers? Done with who?"

Nobody said anything for about five seconds. The refrigerator hummed. Out on the porch, the bug light snapped.

"Daddy," Maxine said. "What are you doing here?"

"Eating supper. It's your mother's birthday."

"I, I know that. I know it is."

"Where were you, Maxine?"

She bent down and picked up the keys. She took her time doing it. When she stood up, she had a different face on, the one she uses at the feed store when a customer's check bounces.

"I meant the accountant," she said. "I was at the accountant's office, about Daddy's taxes. I must have typed it wrong. I was in a hurry."

"Which accountant?" I asked.

"The one in town."

"Chester's my accountant," Edgar said.

"I know that, Daddy. I meant, I was getting a second opinion. About the estate. For the future."

"At nine o'clock at night?"

"They stay open late during tax season."

"It's October," I said.

She looked at me. She looked at the blue folder open on the table. I watched her see the Hume, Baird and Lattner letterhead, and the yellow flag, and Ashton Prentice's signature, and I watched her face go from the feed-store face to nothing at all.

"Where did you get that?" she said.

"You left it on my counter," Edgar said. "Under the fruit bowl. I'm old, Maxine. I'm not blind."

"Daddy, that's just, that's just planning. Everybody does it. It's in case something happens."

"Something like what?"

"Like, like if you get sick. Or confused."

"Am I confused?"

"No. No, Daddy. But you could get—"

"Then why does it have my name typed in it already? 'Edgar R. Tillery, alleged incapacitated person.'" He tapped the paper with one thick finger, the one he broke on a mule in 1967. "Alleged. I know what alleged means."

Maxine's chin started to shake. She turned to me.

"You put him up to this. You've always been jealous of how close I am to Daddy."

"You haven't called him in nineteen days," I said. "He counted."

She grabbed her purse. "I'm not going to stand here and be ganged up on. I need some air."

"Maxine," Edgar said.

"I said I need air!"

She went back out the door she'd come in. A few seconds later, her Buick started in the driveway, and the headlights swung across the kitchen window, and she was gone.

Edgar sat there with his hands flat on the blue folder.

"Chester," he said quietly. "Who's this Ashton Prentice?"

"A man on a billboard."

"Why is a man on a billboard trying to sell my farm?"

I looked out the window at the dark where her taillights had been.

"I don't know yet, Edgar," I said. "But I'm going to test the line before I touch it."

Herschel Gaddy and I climbed poles together for twenty-six years. He's sixty-six now, retired, with a bad hip and a worse temper and a bass boat he loves more than both his ex-wives. He showed up at my house Friday at seven in the morning with a box of donuts from the Krispy Kreme on Pine Avenue, because I'd called him late the night before and said, "I need a second man on a hot line."

He read the blue folder standing up at my kitchen counter, with a glazed donut in his other hand.

"Son of a witch," he said. "She's trying to put her own daddy in the home."

"She's trying to sell his farm. The home's just the paperwork."

"What's Edgar's place worth?"

"Forty acres off 225, with a barn and frontage on the county road? Before the market went crazy, maybe a million and a half. Now? Developers are paying seventy, eighty thousand an acre out there for subdivisions."

Herschel did the math in his head. He was always faster than me at math. "Three million and change."

"About that."

"And she's the only kid."

"She's the only kid."

He put the donut down. "So Edgar dies someday, she gets it anyway. Why rush?"

"That's the first thing I want to know," I said. "The second thing is whether this law firm is real."

We tested the line.

I called the number on the letterhead. A pleasant woman answered, "Hume, Baird and Lattner." I said I had a question about a document prepared by their firm for a client named Edgar Tillery, and could she tell me which attorney handled it.

She put me on hold for four minutes. When she came back, her voice was different.

"Sir, we don't have any client by that name. And I need to ask you, where did you get that letterhead? Our firm changed its name and its logo in 2021. Mr. Hume retired in 2019. The letterhead you're describing hasn't been used in five years."

"Somebody made a copy."

"Then somebody made a forgery, sir. Our managing partner would very much like a copy of that document. And I'd suggest you contact law enforcement."

I thanked her and hung up. Herschel was looking at me with both eyebrows up.

"Fake," I said.

"Fake lawyer, fake papers." He shook his head. "What about the billboard man?"

That one took the county website and about twenty minutes. The listing agreement for Tillery Farm had a buyer already penciled in on the second page, in the box marked "Prospective Purchaser, if known." A company called Silver Springs Run LLC. Offer, one million four hundred thousand dollars.

"A million four?" Herschel leaned over my shoulder. "For forty acres off 225? That's a fire sale."

The state's corporate records site had Silver Springs Run LLC. It had been registered in Florida in August. Registered agent, a woman named Delia Prentice Haws, with an address in The Villages.

"Prentice," Herschel said.

"His sister, I'd bet."

"So the billboard man is listing the farm for Edgar's daughter, and selling it to his own sister's company for half what it's worth."

"And then he sells it to a developer for full price and keeps the difference."

"And your wife?"

"Either she's in on it," I said, "or he's playing her too. I don't know which yet."

I had one more call to make. Dr. Imogene Fairchild has been Edgar's family doctor for twenty-two years. Her office is on Southeast 17th Street. She called me back from her car at lunch.

"Chester, I can't discuss Edgar's medical records with you. You know that."

"I'm not asking about his records. I'm asking about his mind. Is there anything wrong with it?"

There was a pause on the line. Then she laughed, a short one.

"Edgar Tillery corrected my math on his own blood pressure chart in August. There's nothing wrong with that man's mind that wasn't wrong with it in 1990." Another pause. "But I'll tell you something, since you're asking. I had a call two weeks ago. A woman who said she was Edgar's daughter. She wanted copies of his records 'for a guardianship evaluation.' She said a doctor from Gainesville would be evaluating him soon. She said he'd been 'slipping.' I told her I couldn't release anything without Edgar's own signature, and that in my medical opinion he was as sharp as a tack. She hung up on me."

"Did she give you the Gainesville doctor's name?"

"No. But she said the evaluation was going to be done at the farm. On a Monday."

Maxine didn't come home Friday until after nine again. This time she didn't text me anything.

I was sitting at the kitchen table with the weather radar on my phone. It's a habit from thirty-eight years on the line. I check it about twelve times a day, even when I don't have to. There was a band of storms coming up out of the Gulf, due Saturday night.

She came in and put her purse down on the counter and stood there.

"Daddy won't answer my calls," she said.

"He's upset."

"He's upset because you got him upset. He was fine until you put ideas in his head." She crossed her arms. "Chester, I don't know what you think you saw in that folder. But I'm trying to protect my father. He's eighty-four. He drives himself all over the county. He's still shoeing horses, for heaven's sake. What happens when he falls off a ladder in that barn and lies there for two days? Who's going to find him? You?"

"I check on him every Wednesday."

"Wednesdays. Wonderful. And the other six days?"

"Where were you last night, Maxine?"

"I told you. I was getting advice."

"From Ashton Prentice?"

She didn't answer right away.

"Ashton is a real estate professional. He's helping me understand what Daddy's farm is worth. In case we have to sell it to pay for his care."

"Edgar has a hundred and ninety thousand dollars in CDs at the credit union and a pension from the state," I said. "He doesn't need to sell anything to pay for anything. I do his taxes."

"You don't know everything."

"I know that law firm in Gainesville doesn't use that letterhead anymore. I called them this morning. They said it's a forgery."

That one hit. I saw it. She put her hand on the back of a kitchen chair.

"That's, that's not possible. Ashton said his lawyer drew those up."

"Ashton's lawyer used a letterhead that's five years old, from a firm whose senior partner retired in 2019. They'd like a copy. They said I should call the sheriff."

"You wouldn't."

"I haven't. Yet."

"Chester." Her voice dropped. "Please. Don't do anything. Ashton can explain. There's an evaluation on Monday. A real doctor from Gainesville. If Daddy's fine, then he's fine, and that's the end of it. That's all it is. It's just to be sure."

"Is Ashton the one who told you your father was slipping?"

"Daddy is slipping. He forgot my birthday in March."

"He sent you a card in March. You left it on the dashboard of your car for a week."

She stared at me. Then she picked up her purse.

"I'm sleeping at Carol Ann's tonight," she said. Carol Ann is her friend from the feed store. "Don't call me."

"Are you sleeping at Carol Ann's," I said, "or are you sleeping with Ashton Prentice?"

She didn't answer. She walked out. That was all the answer I needed.

Saturday night the storm came in, just like the radar said.

I'd been on the line through Andrew and Charley and Irma and about a hundred no-name squall lines that took out half of Marion County at a time, and I could tell by the way the air felt at four in the afternoon that this one was going to be loud.

Herschel and I drove out to Tillery Farm at six with a cooler of sandwiches and two sleeping bags. Edgar thought we were being ridiculous.

"Nobody's coming out here in this," he said.

"That's why they'd come in this," Herschel said.

Edgar's farmhouse is an old two-story Florida cracker house with a tin roof and a wraparound porch, at the end of a quarter-mile shell rock drive behind a pipe gate. His office is off the back of the kitchen, a little room with a window onto the barn, where he keeps a rolltop desk that belonged to his father. In the bottom left drawer of that desk, in a fireproof box, Edgar keeps his deed, his will and his and Ruth's marriage license.

Maxine knew that. Everybody in the family knew that. Ruth used to joke that if the house burned down, Edgar would run back in for the rolltop before he ran back in for her.

By ten o'clock the wind was howling, and the rain was coming sideways across the pasture. The power went out a little after ten. I'd have bet money on it going ten minutes sooner, so the storm beat my radar by a hair. Edgar went up to bed with a flashlight, grumbling. Herschel took the couch in the front room. I sat in Edgar's office in the dark with my back against the wall, with a big Maglite in my lap and the rolltop desk four feet away.

Around midnight I heard a truck.

Not on the drive. Out on County Road 225, slowing down, and then nothing, like it had pulled off on the shoulder by the pipe gate and cut its lights.

I texted Herschel one word. Truck.

He texted back. Heard it.

Ten minutes went by. Fifteen. The rain was so loud on the tin roof you couldn't hear yourself think. Then, under the rain, I heard something else. A scrape at the back door. A long, slow one, like somebody working the latch with something thin.

The back door opened. Wind came in, and the smell of wet grass, and a man's shape against the gray.

He was big. He had a flashlight with tape over half the lens, so it threw a thin, dim beam, and he moved through the kitchen like he knew exactly where he was going. He went straight to the office door. Straight to the rolltop. He knelt down and pulled at the bottom left drawer.

I clicked on the Maglite and put it right in his eyes.

"That's Ruth's marriage license in there, son," I said. "Leave it alone."

He came up off his knees at me like a linebacker.

I'm sixty-two years old. I've got a bad shoulder from a pole in 1998 and two screws in my left ankle. But I spent thirty-eight years on the line wrestling things that didn't want to be wrestled, and I'd been waiting for him for two hours in the dark. He hit me in the chest and we both went back into the wall, and Edgar's framed photograph of his best quarter horse came down and broke on the floor. He swung at my head. I ducked, and his fist went into the wall plaster. I got my arm around his and turned, the way you turn a stubborn guy wire, and he yelled and went down on one knee on the broken glass from the picture frame.

He came up swinging again. I hit him once, hard, with my right, in the side of the face. He staggered back into the kitchen.

Herschel was in the doorway by then with a cast-iron skillet in one hand and his phone in the other, already dialing 911.

The man saw Herschel and the skillet. He turned and ran. Out the back door into the rain, holding his hand against his chest, and I saw blood dripping between his fingers in the beam of my flashlight. He'd cut his hand open on the glass.

I went after him as far as the porch. He was running down the shell rock drive toward the gate, slipping in the mud. Halfway down, he went sideways off the edge of the drive into the drainage ditch, came up covered in mud and kept running.

A minute later we heard an engine on 225. Then a terrible grinding noise. Then nothing but the rain.

"What was that?" Herschel said behind me.

"I think he tried to pull out too fast," I said. "Onto a wet shoulder. In the dark."

Upstairs, Edgar's voice came down, loud and annoyed. "Chester! What in the Sam Hill are you two doing down there?"

"Somebody tried to rob your desk, Edgar."

There was a pause.

"Did they get Ruth's license?"

"No, sir."

"Then I'm going back to sleep," he said. "Wake me when the sheriff gets here."

Deputy Raylene Hooks got there forty minutes later, in a Marion County SUV with the light bar flashing red and blue across the wet pasture. She was in her late thirties, tall, with a rain jacket over her uniform and the calm voice of somebody who's been to a lot of bad scenes in the middle of the night.

She took our statements in Edgar's kitchen by flashlight. She photographed the scrape on the back door latch, the blood on the floor of the office, the hole in the plaster, the broken picture. She bagged a piece of glass with blood on it.

Then she drove down to the gate.

When she came back, she was holding a clipboard and a clear plastic evidence bag, and she looked different. Interested.

"There's a pickup truck in the ditch on the other side of your gate, Mr. Tillery," she said. "Nose-down. He tried to back out onto the road, and his back wheels went off the shoulder. He abandoned it. The plate comes back to a Travis Kilgore, thirty-four, of Reddick. Address is a farm owned by Prentice Equine." She held up the evidence bag. "And this was on the passenger seat."

Inside the bag was a manila folder. And inside the manila folder, I could see through the plastic, was a form. A typed medical evaluation, three pages, on the letterhead of a Gainesville clinic. Patient name, Edgar R. Tillery. Diagnosis, moderate to severe cognitive decline, unable to manage his own affairs. It was signed by a doctor I'd never heard of.

It was dated Monday.

Two days from now.

Travis Kilgore walked into the emergency room at AdventHealth Ocala at about three in the morning with eleven stitches' worth of cut across his right palm and a story about a fishing knife. The triage nurse had seen Raylene Hooks's be-on-the-lookout an hour earlier. Raylene was there before they finished sewing.

She called me early Sunday morning, before I'd finished my coffee.

"Mr. Poole. Can you and Mr. Tillery come down to the substation on 441? Bring that blue folder. Mr. Kilgore has decided he'd like to talk, and I'd like you to hear what he's talking about."

The Marion County sheriff's substation on 441 is a low tan building next to a feed store, which I thought was funny, since my wife works at a feed store. Raylene put Edgar and me in a little room with a two-way window, and on the other side of the glass sat a big young man in a muddy Prentice Equine T-shirt with his right hand wrapped up like a boxing glove.

He looked scared. He looked about nineteen, even though the paperwork said thirty-four.

Raylene sat down across from him with a tape recorder and a Styrofoam cup of coffee.

"Travis, you've been read your rights, and you've told me you want to talk without a lawyer. Is that still true?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"Tell me about last night."

He told her. It came out all at once, the way it does with men who aren't built for lying.

Ashton Prentice had hired him two years ago to muck stalls and drive the horse trailer at Prentice's farm in Reddick. Three weeks ago, Ashton had called him into the office and asked him if he wanted to make twenty-five hundred dollars cash for one night's work. Go to an old man's farmhouse off 225 when there's a storm and the power's out. Go in the back. Bottom left drawer of a rolltop desk. Take the fireproof box. Don't hurt anybody. The old man's deaf as a post and sleeps upstairs.

"Why did he want the box?" Raylene asked.

"He said there was an old will in it. He said the old man's daughter needed it gone. If there wasn't any will, the daughter got everything automatic, he said. Without the old will, she'd be the only heir, and it'd be clean."

On my side of the glass, Edgar made a sound low in his chest.

"And the folder on your passenger seat?" Raylene said. "The medical evaluation?"

"Mr. Prentice gave me that Friday. He said drop it at his sister's house in The Villages on my way home. Her husband's the doctor who's going to sign it Monday. He already signed it. Mr. Prentice said it saves time."

"What kind of doctor is her husband?"

Travis looked confused. "He's a back doctor. Like a chiropractor. He used to be. He's retired."

Raylene wrote that down slowly.

"Travis. Did Ashton Prentice ever say how much he was going to make on this?"

Travis rubbed his face with his good hand. "He was on the phone in the barn one time. With somebody, I don't know who. He was laughing. He said, 'She thinks she's selling for three million. She's selling to me for one-four. Then I sell to the Gator Lakes boys for three-four, and she never knows the difference.'" He looked up at Raylene. "I figured 'she' was the daughter. He calls her 'Maxie' on the phone."

I sat there next to Edgar and didn't move for a long time.

"So he's robbing her, too," Edgar said finally, very quietly. "That fool girl. He's robbing her too."

"Looks that way."

Edgar turned his head and looked at me. His eyes were wet, and he was angry, and I'd never seen him look so old.

"She was going to put me in a home, Chester. For a man who's robbing her blind."

That afternoon, Maxine came to the farm.

I don't know who told her. Probably Ashton, after his truck didn't come back and Travis didn't answer his phone. She came up the shell rock drive in her Buick way too fast and slid to a stop by the porch, and she got out and came up the steps with her hair wild from the wind.

Edgar and I were sitting on the porch in the rocking chairs. Herschel was inside fixing the plaster in the office.

"What did you do?" she said to me. "What did you do to Ashton's man?"

"He broke into your father's house last night, Maxine."

"That's a lie. Travis said he got lost in the storm. He said you attacked him."

"He told you that?"

"He told Ashton. Ashton told me. Travis pulled over to wait out the rain, and you came at him with a flashlight—"

"Inside your daddy's house," I said. "At his desk. With his hand in the drawer where your mama's marriage license is."

"You're lying!" Her voice went shrill. "You've always hated me. You've always wanted Daddy to yourself. You and your stupid taxes and your Wednesdays. You want this farm. That's what this is. You want to turn him against me so he'll leave it to you!"

"Maxine," Edgar said.

"No, Daddy. You don't understand what he's doing to you—"

"Maxine Ruth," Edgar said, louder, in the voice I'd heard him use on a stallion that wouldn't stand still. "Sit down."

She stopped. She didn't sit, but she stopped.

"I was at the sheriff's station this morning," he said. "I heard that boy talk. I heard every word. He said Ashton Prentice paid him twenty-five hundred dollars to come in my back door in the middle of a hurricane and steal my will so you could get the farm 'clean.'"

"That's not, Ashton would never—"

"He said Ashton's brother-in-law is a retired back doctor who already signed a paper saying I've lost my mind. Dated tomorrow." Edgar leaned forward in his rocker. "And he said Ashton's going to buy my farm from you for a million four, and sell it the same week for three million four hundred thousand. He laughed about it on the phone, Maxine. He called you Maxie."

Her knees gave a little. She put a hand out for the porch rail and held on to it.

"No," she said. "No. He said a million four was just the first number. For the paperwork. He said the real money would come later, through his company. He said we'd have a house on the water in Cedar Key."

"We," I said.

She looked at me. Then she looked at her father. Then she looked out at the pasture, where two of Edgar's mares were standing under the live oaks with their tails to the wind.

"He's coming tomorrow," she said. "With the doctor. At nine. He said it was just a formality. He said once the doctor signs, I file, and in two weeks it's done." She was crying now. "Daddy, I didn't know about the break-in. I swear to God. I didn't know anything about that."

"You knew about the doctor," Edgar said.

She didn't answer.

"You knew you were going to stand on my porch and watch a stranger tell a judge I'm out of my head."

"I thought you were slipping," she said. "Ashton said—"

"Ashton said," Edgar repeated. He stood up out of the rocking chair, slowly, one hand on his bad knee. "Ashton said. Maxine, your mother would have taken a buggy whip to you."

He went inside. The screen door banged.

Maxine stood on the porch holding the rail, crying, and looked at me like I could fix it.

"Chester, what do I do?"

I thought about it.

"Tomorrow at nine," I said, "you show up. Same as you planned. And you let Ashton do exactly what he came to do."

"Why?"

"Because before you touch a line," I said, "you test it."

She called me at eleven that night. I was in bed at home, not asleep, with the radar open on my phone out of habit, watching the last of the storm drift off over the Atlantic.

"He called me," she said. No hello. "Ashton. Just now."

"What did he want?"

"He wanted to know if Travis talked to the sheriff. He said Travis isn't answering his phone and his truck's been towed." She was whispering, like somebody might hear her in Carol Ann's guest room. "Chester, I lied to him. I said I didn't know anything. I said Daddy was asleep when I got there and you weren't around. I said, I said I'd see him at nine."

"What did he say?"

"He said good. He said, 'Wear something nice, Maxie, it's the last day you'll ever have to drive that Buick.'" She made a sound that was half a laugh and half something worse. "He called me Maxie. I used to think that was sweet."

I didn't say anything.

"Chester," she said. "Are you going to be there?"

"I'll be there."

"Is Daddy going to forgive me?"

"That's not my call, Maxine. That's his."

"Are you?"

I looked at the radar. The storm was gone. The screen was clear green all the way to the coast.

"Get some sleep," I said. "Nine o'clock."

She hung up. I lay there a long time looking at the ceiling fan going around. Thirty-two years, and the first honest phone call we'd had in months was the one where she asked me if I was going to forgive her, and I didn't answer.

Monday morning was clear and washed clean, the way it always is in Florida after a big storm. Every live oak on the farm was dripping, and there were branches down all over the pasture, and the air smelled like cut hay and wet earth.

At five minutes to nine, a white Escalade with a Prentice Equine magnet on the door came up the shell rock drive and parked by the barn. Ashton Prentice got out. He was in the same pressed jeans and hat as his billboard, but he looked tired, and the grin he put on for the porch didn't reach his eyes. Behind him, out of the passenger side, came a thin, stooped man in his seventies with a leather doctor's bag.

Maxine's Buick was already parked by the porch. She was standing at the top of the steps, pale, with her arms wrapped around herself.

Ashton came up the walk with his hand out.

"Morning, partner," he said to me. "Ashton Prentice. You must be the son-in-law. Maxie's told me so much about you."

"I bet she has."

He turned to the stooped man. "This is Dr. Merrill Haws, from Gainesville. He's here to do a simple evaluation of Mr. Tillery. Totally routine. Takes about twenty minutes." He looked past me at the front door. "Is the old man up?"

"I'm up," Edgar said from the doorway. "I've been up since five. I fed eleven horses and fixed a fence. Come on in, Doctor. I'd like to see your license."

Dr. Haws stopped on the bottom step. "My, my license?"

"Your medical license. To practice medicine in the State of Florida. Since you're going to tell a judge I'm crazy, I'd like to see it."

Dr. Haws looked at Ashton.

"He's confused," Ashton said smoothly. "This is exactly what we were talking about, Doctor. Agitation. Paranoia. Very common."

"I'm not confused, son," Edgar said. "I'm eighty-four. There's a difference."

A second car came up the drive. A gray Toyota. It parked behind the Escalade, and a woman in her fifties in a cardigan got out with a medical bag of her own.

"Who's that?" Ashton said.

"That's Dr. Imogene Fairchild," I said. "Edgar's doctor for twenty-two years. Board-certified in internal medicine. She's going to sit in, if Dr. Haws doesn't mind."

Ashton's grin slipped about an inch.

"That's not necessary."

"Oh, I think it is."

And then a third car came up the drive. A Marion County SUV, no lights this time. Deputy Raylene Hooks got out, put her hat on and walked up the path to the porch steps.

Ashton Prentice took a step back.

"Morning, Mr. Prentice," Raylene said. "Dr. Haws. I'm glad I caught you both together. Saves me a drive to The Villages."

"Deputy." Ashton's voice was steady, but his hands weren't. "Is there a problem? We're here on a private family matter. Mrs. Poole invited us."

"I'm sure she did." Raylene took a folded paper out of her jacket. "Mr. Prentice, I have a warrant for your arrest for burglary of a dwelling, conspiracy, and exploitation of an elderly person, which in Florida is a felony. Mr. Kilgore gave us a statement yesterday. We also found a medical evaluation in his truck with Mr. Tillery's name on it, dated today, already signed by Dr. Haws here." She turned to the stooped man. "Dr. Haws, your chiropractic license expired in 2016. I checked. You're not under arrest yet, but you're going to want to sit down in my car and wait for me, and you're going to want to call a lawyer."

Dr. Haws sat down on the porch steps right where he was, with his bag in his lap.

Ashton turned around, fast. Not to Raylene. To Maxine.

"You did this," he said. "You set me up. You and your father."

"You were going to buy the farm from me for a million four," Maxine said. Her voice was shaking. "You told me it was worth three. You laughed about me on the phone."

"Who told you that?"

"Your own farmhand. You called me Maxie."

"You were going to put your own daddy in a home for a house in Cedar Key," Ashton said, loud, so everybody could hear it. "Don't you dare look at me like I'm the bad guy here, Maxie. You came to me. You said the old man was slipping and you couldn't wait twenty years. You said that. Those were your words."

Maxine's mouth fell open. Edgar, standing in his doorway, closed his eyes.

"Mr. Prentice," Raylene said. "Turn around, please. Hands behind your back."

Ashton turned. The cuffs went on with that sound they make. He looked at the billboard grin of himself on the Escalade's door magnet like it belonged to somebody else.

"I want my lawyer," he said.

"You can call him from the jail," Raylene said, and walked him down the path.

When the SUV was gone with Ashton in the back and Dr. Haws in the front passenger seat, staring at his knees, it was quiet on the porch except for the horses blowing in the pasture.

Maxine turned to her father.

"Daddy," she said. "Daddy, please. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I'll do anything."

"I went to a real lawyer Friday," Edgar said. "In Ocala. After Chester called that Gainesville outfit. I made a new will. When I'm gone, this farm goes into a trust for the 4-H horse program at the county fairgrounds, and it can't ever be sold to a developer. Your mama would've liked that." He looked at her. "You'll get your mama's jewelry and her quilts. That's what's left for you, Maxine."

She sat down on the top step and put her face in her hands.

Then she looked up at me.

"Chester," she said. "Please. I don't have anywhere else to go. Take me home. We can fix this. Thirty-two years. Please."

I stood at the top of the steps, next to her father, and I thought about Thursday night. About the text on my phone. Helping Dad with his tax papers. About her keys hitting my kitchen floor.

I put my hand on Edgar's shoulder.

"Edgar," I said. "It's Monday. Let's go do your taxes."
