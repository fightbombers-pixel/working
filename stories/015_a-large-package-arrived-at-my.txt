"Happy birthday, baby. Fifty-eight and still the best-looking CPA in Polk County."

My wife said it from my phone, propped against the sugar bowl, to a table of eleven people who all raised their beers at the screen like she was sitting right there with us. She was in a hotel room in Omaha, or that's what the little window behind her said, beige wallpaper and a lamp. She'd been gone four days on a training trip. She was going to be gone three more.

The doorbell rang before anybody could cut the cake.

It was a courier, a kid in a yellow vest, holding a box about the size of a case of printer paper. "Theodore Lynch? Needs a signature."

I signed, carried it back to the dining room and set it next to the cake, because my brother Wade said, "Open it, it's probably from a client who owes you money," and everybody laughed.

Inside were photographs. Real ones, printed, glossy, stacked in rubber bands. Fifty of them, I'd count later. On top was my wife.

Not my wife on any day I knew. My wife at a backyard party under paper lanterns, with people I'd never seen in my life. My wife on a patio with mountains behind her, the kind of mountains we don't have in Iowa. Every photo had a date written on the back in blue ink. 2015. 2017. 2019. 2021.

"Teddy?" Paula said from the phone. "What is it? You've gone quiet."

"A gift," I said. "From a client."

I turned the phone face down on the tablecloth. My daughter-in-law looked at me. Wade looked at me. My son Owen put his fork down.

The last photo in the stack was a wedding. A little white chapel, a man with gray hair in a bolo tie, and my wife in an ivory dress holding a bouquet of yellow roses, laughing. On the back it said October 13, 2018.

We'd been married since 2011. In October of 2018 she'd told me she was in Minneapolis for a week, teaching nurses how to use a new hospital bed.

Under the photo was a note card. Five words and a name, in the same blue ink.

"Her real name is Laura Mendez."

Nine days later, my wife would be standing in a courthouse hallway holding that same photo, and she wouldn't be able to make her hands stop shaking.

What she didn't know, that Saturday, was that the box had a return address taped to the bottom, and that the woman at that address had her eyes.

My name is Ted Lynch. I'm fifty-eight as of that day, and I've run my own tax and accounting office in Des Moines for twenty-six years. Most of what I do is boring, and I like it that way. The one thing my job taught me that matters here is simple: people lie in their words, but they almost never lie in their receipts. Hold on to that.

I slid the photo back into the stack and put the lid on the box.

"Okay," I said to the table. "Who wants cake?"

Nobody wanted cake.

Wade followed me into the kitchen. My brother is four years younger than me, owns two tow trucks and a bad attitude, and he's never once in his life pretended not to see something.

"That was Paula in a wedding dress," he said.

"Yep."

"With a guy who looked like he sells turquoise at a truck stop."

"Yep."

"Ted. What the hell is going on?"

I turned the box over on the counter. There was a shipping label from a store in San Antonio, Texas. The sender line said Rosario's Beauty Salon, with a street address and a phone number.

"I don't know yet," I said. "But I'm going to find out before she gets home."

The phone buzzed in my pocket. A text from Paula.

"Why did you hang up on me on your birthday? Call me tonight. Love you."

That night, after everybody went home, Wade and I spread the fifty photos across the workbench in my garage under the shop lights. I'm the kind of guy who scans everything. Receipts, warranties, my kids' report cards from 2004. Paula used to tease me about it. So before we touched anything else, I fed every photo, front and back, through the scanner on my office printer and saved them to a thumb drive, and put the drive in the fire safe bolted to the garage floor.

Then I opened my laptop and pulled up our tax returns.

"You're doing taxes," Wade said. "Right now. Your wife has a second husband and you're doing taxes."

"Paula deducts her unreimbursed travel," I said. "Every trip. She gives me the dates and I put them on a spreadsheet. I've done it for her every year since 2012."

"Romantic."

"Accurate."

I read the dates off my spreadsheet. Wade read the dates off the backs of the photos. It took us a little under an hour, and by the end of it Wade had stopped making jokes.

Every single photo lined up with a trip. Fort Worth, March 2015, the backyard party with paper lanterns. St. Louis, June 2017, a funeral, people in black around a grave, my wife in sunglasses next to an older woman with a mole on her cheek exactly where Paula has one. Minneapolis, October 2018, the chapel. Kansas City, April 2019, the man in the bolo tie with his arm around her at a ballpark.

Wade held that one up to the light.

"Look at his hat," he said.

It was an Iowa Cubs cap. Our Iowa Cubs. Triple-A, Principal Park, two miles from my office.

"That's not a souvenir from Tucson," Wade said. "Somebody brought that guy here."

"Or he came here himself."

That one stuck in me like a splinter, and I didn't know yet why.

I kept going. That's what accountants do when the world tilts: we look for the money.

Paula's mother had died years ago, or so she told me when we met. Her whole family had died in a house fire in Laredo when she was nineteen, she said, and she didn't like to talk about it, and I never pushed. But she had an aunt. An aunt in a nursing home in Arizona, the only family she had left. And every month since 2018, eighteen hundred and fifty dollars went out of our joint checking to a place called Saguaro Senior Living.

I'd never once questioned it. Who questions a woman paying for her old aunt?

"How many months?" Wade asked.

I ran it. "Eighty-six."

"How much is that?"

"A hundred and fifty-nine thousand, one hundred dollars."

Wade whistled low. "For an aunt."

I looked up Saguaro Senior Living on the internet. There was nothing. No website, no listing with the state of Arizona, no reviews from angry daughters. Just a post office box in Tucson on our bank statements and a phone number she'd given me once in case of an emergency.

I put the phone on speaker and dialed it.

It rang four times. Then a man picked up. Older voice, gravel in it, a TV going in the background.

"Stroud residence."

I hung up.

Wade and I sat there in the garage looking at each other, the shop lights buzzing over our heads.

"So the nursing home is a house," Wade said.

"The nursing home is a husband."

My phone lit up in my hand. Paula. A video call this time. I almost let it ring out, and then I answered, because a man who doesn't answer is a man with something to hide, and I needed her thinking I had nothing.

She was in bed in the hotel, hair down, in my old Drake University T-shirt.

"There you are," she said. "You hung up on me in front of everybody. On your birthday."

"Owen's kid knocked over a glass. It was a mess."

"Owen doesn't have a kid."

"Owen's neighbor's kid."

She looked at me a beat too long. Then she smiled.

"What was in the box, Teddy?"

"Client stuff. Old records. Somebody cleaning out a basement."

"On a Saturday? By courier?"

"People are weird about their records."

"Mm." She tilted her head, the way she does when she's reading a menu she doesn't trust. "Where are you right now? Is that the garage?"

Behind me on the workbench were fifty photographs of her with another man. I shifted the phone an inch to the left so all she'd see was my pegboard and a coffee can full of screws.

"Changing the oil in the mower," I said.

"At ten at night."

"I couldn't sleep. I'm fifty-eight now, I get up to pee anyway."

She laughed, and it was the same laugh from the chapel photo, and that's when it finally hit me, standing in my own garage. I'd been married fourteen years to a woman who could laugh like that at two different men.

"Get some sleep, old man," she said. "Three more days. I'll bring you something from Omaha."

"Bring me a steak."

"Love you."

"Yep."

The screen went black. Wade was staring at me.

"Yep?" he said. "Fourteen years and you said yep?"

"It's what I always say."

"Then you're fine. She didn't notice." He picked up the chapel photo again. "So what now? You call a lawyer?"

"Tomorrow I call a lawyer," I said. "Monday I'm going to San Antonio."

"To the beauty salon."

"To whoever sent this."

Wade put the photo down very carefully, like it might go off.

"And if it's a trap?"

"Then I'd like to know who set it."

I booked the first flight out of Des Moines on Monday morning, a connection through Dallas, and I put it on my business card so it wouldn't show up on the joint account Paula checks from her phone. Then I called the number on the shipping label, even though it was after ten on a Saturday night and a salon would be closed.

A woman answered on the first ring, like she'd been sitting by the phone for a week.

"Rosario's."

"My name is Ted Lynch," I said. "I live in Des Moines, Iowa. I got your package."

The line went so quiet I could hear her breathing.

"Is she there with you?" the woman whispered.

"No. She's in Omaha. Or that's what she says."

"She's not in Omaha, Mr. Lynch." A long breath. "Don't call this number again from your house. Laura checks your phone bill. Come to San Antonio. Come in person, and don't tell her you're coming."

"Ma'am, who are you?"

"I'm her sister," she said. "The one she told you burned to death in Laredo."

I called Joan Pickard on Sunday morning at her house, which you're not supposed to do with a lawyer, but Joan did my taxes' legal side for nineteen years and once told me that if I ever needed her on a Sunday, I'd better be bleeding.

"Are you bleeding?" she said.

"Sort of."

She listened to the whole thing without interrupting once, which for Joan is a medical event. She's sixty, five-foot-nothing, and has a voice like a cheese grater.

"Okay," she said when I was done. "Three things. One. Don't move a dime. Not a single dime, Ted, or her lawyer will say you started it. Two. Don't confront her. You're an accountant, act like one. Smile and count. Three. Iowa is no-fault. Cheating alone doesn't change the split. But money spent on another household is marital money she wasted, and a judge will want it back. And if that wedding is real, that's not a divorce problem. That's a crime."

"Bigamy."

"In Iowa it's an aggravated misdemeanor. In Arizona it's a felony. And if her name isn't her name, that's a whole other floor of the building." She paused. "Go to Texas. Bring me paper. Paper, Ted. Not stories."

"I always bring paper."

"That's why I like you."

Rosario's Beauty Salon was in a strip mall on the south side of San Antonio, between a taqueria and a place that cashed checks. It was a little after ten on Monday when I walked in, and there were two women under the dryers and a radio playing old Selena.

The woman behind the counter looked up, and I stopped in the doorway like I'd walked into a glass door.

She was older than Paula by a few years and heavier, with gray in her hair she didn't bother to hide. But the eyes were the same. The mole on the cheek was the same. She was the woman standing next to my wife at the funeral in the 2017 photo.

"You're Ted," she said.

"You're dead," I said. "In a house fire. In Laredo. When you were twenty-two."

She laughed, one short bark that wasn't happy at all, and turned the sign on the door to Closed.

"Lupe, finish Mrs. Garza for me," she said to the girl at the back. "I'll be in the office."

The office was a closet with a desk, a mini fridge and a calendar from a tire shop. She gave me a bottle of water and sat down across from me with her hands folded, like a woman about to tell a priest something.

"There was no fire," she said. "There was never a fire. There's me, there was Mama until 2017, and there's my sister Laura, who left home in 1992 with forty dollars out of Mama's purse and never came back as herself."

"As herself."

"Paula Reyes was our cousin. A baby. She died of pneumonia in 1974, three months old. Laura found her birth certificate in Mama's papers when she was eighteen. A dead baby doesn't complain when you borrow her name." Rosario shook her head. "She got a Social Security card with it the next year. Back then nobody checked anything."

I took out my notebook. I always have one. She watched me write.

"You're the accountant," she said.

"I am."

"She said you were boring. She said it like it was a good thing. Like boring was the whole point."

"How many husbands, Rosario?"

She held up three fingers.

"A man in El Paso in 1999. Ray Becerra. He drove a beer truck. She married him as Laura Mendez, her real name, and she left him in 2003 with his credit cards maxed and his truck gone. They never divorced. She just walked out of the house one day and became Paula." She put one finger down. "Then you, in 2011. Then Gene in Tucson. 2018. You saw the picture."

"Gene Stroud."

"Gene Stroud. Retired fireman. Big house, big pension." She pressed her lips together. "She calls you the bank and she calls him the vacation."

I sat with that for a second. A man can only take so many sentences like that in a morning.

"Why now?" I asked. "You had those photos for years. Why send them now?"

Rosario got up, opened a drawer in the desk and handed me a manila envelope. Inside was a copy of a birth certificate from Bexar County. Laura Inez Mendez, born March 2, 1973. Under it was a printout of a text conversation.

"Because of what she did to Mama," she said. "In 2017 Mama was dying, and Laura came home for the first time in twenty-five years. She cried at the bed. She sang to her. And after the funeral, the money from Mama's house was gone. Sixty-one thousand dollars. Mama signed a paper in the hospital that Laura put in front of her, and Mama couldn't read English that well, and she trusted her baby girl." Her voice cracked and she pushed through it. "I let it go. I thought, okay, she's gone, God will handle her. Then she called me at Christmas, drunk on wine, bragging."

She tapped the printout. It was a text from a number with a Tucson area code, dated December 26.

"The CPA's done. I'm cashing out in March. Half his house, half his retirement, and Gene and I are buying the place in Sedona. Don't be jealous."

It was March. It was the first week of March.

"Half his retirement," I said slowly. "In Iowa, a divorce splits it down the middle."

"She knows that. She studied it. She studies everything." Rosario leaned in. "She had the photos printed for me, Ted. Every trip, she sent me pictures to make me jealous. Look at me, look at my life, look at my mountains. I kept them all. And I thought, the boring man in Iowa should see what his wife sends her sister."

My phone buzzed on the desk. Then Rosario's buzzed on the desk next to it, a little different ring. She picked hers up, read it and went pale under her makeup.

She turned it around so I could see.

It was from the Tucson number.

"Somebody called Rosario's Saturday night from an Iowa number. Was that you? Did you send him something? Answer me."

"She checks the phone bill," Rosario whispered. "I told you."

On my phone, the other text was from my wife.

"Landing Wednesday instead. Missed you. Where are you today?"

I flew to Tucson that night instead of home. Joan had told me to bring paper, not stories, and the biggest piece of paper in this whole thing was hanging on a wall somewhere in Arizona in a frame.

Gene Stroud lived in the foothills north of town in a big tan stucco house with a three-car garage and a red pickup truck in the driveway with a Tucson Fire Department sticker in the back window. I got there at nine on Tuesday morning and rang the bell.

He was bigger than in the photos. Sixty-one, thick through the chest, the kind of man who still did push-ups because a doctor told him not to. He looked at me through the screen door.

"Help you?"

"My name's Ted Lynch. I'm from Des Moines, Iowa."

Something moved behind his eyes. Just for half a second. Then it was gone, and he frowned like a man who'd never heard of Iowa.

"Okay."

I held up my phone with the chapel photo on the screen. "I think we're married to the same woman."

He stared at the phone a long time. Then he pushed the screen door open, took the phone out of my hand and walked back into his house with it like it was his.

I followed him in. The living room was nice. Leather furniture, a flat screen, a framed copy of the exact same wedding photo on the wall over the fireplace. Yellow roses, white chapel, my wife laughing.

"Married to her," Gene said to the photo, not to me. "Since 2018. Paula Stroud. She works for a hospital bed company. She travels."

"She travels to me," I said. "Since 2011."

He sat down hard on the arm of the couch. He put his face in his hands. His shoulders went up and down. I stood there feeling like the worst man in Arizona.

"Two weeks every month," he said through his fingers. "Two weeks. I thought she was in Omaha."

"So did I."

He looked up, red-eyed. "What do you want from me?"

"The truth on paper. When you met her, how much you gave her, what she told you about money."

"I don't know you. You could be anybody."

"I could. I'm a CPA with a brother who tows cars and a lawyer who scares people. That's about as scary as I get."

He almost smiled at that. Then he stood up and stuck his hand out, and I shook it. His grip was like a vise.

"She's coming here this weekend," he said. "Or she was supposed to. Let me think about this. Let me talk to my own lawyer. Where are you staying?"

"The Desert Palms on Oracle Road. Room 114."

"Give me tonight," he said. "I'll call you in the morning. We'll figure out how to burn her down together."

I went back to the motel feeling, for the first time in four days, like I had somebody on my side. I had a burrito at a place across the street, called Wade to tell him I had an ally, and Wade said, "Ted, a guy you met this morning is not an ally, he's a guy you met this morning."

When I got back to Room 114, the door was shut and locked like I'd left it.

Inside, my suitcase was open on the bed. My clothes were on the floor. The manila envelope from Rosario was gone. So were the printed copies of the photos I'd brought to show Joan.

The only things left on the bed were my socks and a single yellow rose petal, which I'm pretty sure was an accident.

The night clerk was a heavy kid named Trent with a Diamondbacks cap and a bored face. He didn't want to help until I told him I'd write a two-star review with the word bedbugs in it.

He turned his monitor around.

At ten past eight, while I was eating a burrito across the street, a red pickup truck pulled into the space in front of Room 114. A big man in a ball cap got out, went to my door, and opened it with what looked a lot like a key card. Four minutes later he came out carrying a manila envelope and drove away.

Trent froze the frame on the back window of the truck. You could just make out the sticker. Tucson Fire Department.

"That guy," Trent said. "He was in here around six. Said he was your brother-in-law and you'd locked yourself out. I made him a key."

"What did he say his name was?"

Trent checked the log. "He didn't. He just knew the room number."

Of course he did. I'd given it to him myself.

I didn't call Gene in the morning. Gene didn't call me either. I figured we'd both said everything we had to say through a motel door.

I flew home Wednesday. Paula's flight from "Omaha" was supposed to land at four. Mine landed at two. I drove straight from the airport to my house on the west side, and my key didn't work.

I stood on my own porch jiggling a key in a lock that had been changed, with a suitcase in my other hand, while Mrs. Halvorsen across the street watered a plant that didn't need water and watched me over it.

Then my phone rang. It was the bank.

"Mr. Lynch? This is Carla in the Ingersoll branch. We're required to tell you when a joint account is closed. Your savings account ending in 4471 was closed this morning by the other account holder. The balance was transferred out."

"How much?"

"Two hundred twelve thousand, four hundred dollars."

"Transferred where?"

"I'm sorry, sir, I can't tell you that. It went to an account in her name only."

I said thank you, because my mother raised me right, and sat down on my own porch steps.

Joan was there in thirty minutes with a manila folder in her hand and her reading glasses on her head.

"She's fast," Joan said. "I'll give her that. She filed this morning. Divorce. Plus an emergency motion for temporary support and exclusive use of the house. She says you're volatile, controlling, and that you've been, quote, stalking her family members in two states."

"I've been visiting her family members. They were supposed to be dead."

"I'm aware." Joan flipped a page. "Hearing on the temporary motion is a week from Monday. If she wins it, you're out of your own house until trial, you're paying her support, and she gets to sit in your living room while that two hundred grand sits in hers."

"So she's ahead."

"She's way ahead, Ted." Joan took her glasses off her head and put them on. "But she made one mistake, and it's a big one. She signed this."

She turned the petition around and pointed at the bottom of the second page. A sworn verification, notarized. I declare under penalty of perjury that the foregoing is true and correct. And above it, typed out nice and clean, the name of the petitioner.

Paula Reyes Lynch.

"She swore to a judge," Joan said, "under a dead baby's name."

The next morning Joan brought a woman to my office. Fifty or so, gray suit, hair pulled back so tight it looked like it hurt. She shook my hand once, hard.

"Special Agent Monica Vail, Iowa Division of Criminal Investigation," she said. "Your lawyer says you have a story. I don't like stories. Show me paper."

I like a woman who talks like that.

I gave her paper. The Bexar County birth certificate of Laura Inez Mendez, born 1973, which Rosario had emailed me again after the motel. The death certificate of Paula Reyes, three months old, February 1974, which I'd ordered from the state of Texas online for twenty-two dollars. Our marriage license from 2011. Eighty-six months of bank statements to Saguaro Senior Living. Scans of fifty photographs off a thumb drive from my garage safe.

Vail read for twenty minutes without saying a word. Then she took her glasses off.

"The Social Security number she uses," she said, "was issued in 1991 to a woman who died in 1974. That's identity theft, and it's federal. The marriage license in your county is a false statement. The sworn petition is perjury. And if Arizona has a 2018 license with the same number on it, I've got bigamy on top." She tapped the scans. "Where are the originals?"

"In a fire safe in my garage. Most of them. Some were stolen from my motel room in Tucson on Tuesday night."

"By whom?"

"By her other husband. On camera."

Vail looked at Joan. Joan looked at Vail. They both looked like cats who'd heard a can opener.

"Keep the originals where they are," Vail said. "I'll get a subpoena for the Arizona license. Don't say a word to her. Let her walk into that hearing feeling good."

That night Wade called me while I was eating supper alone at his kitchen table, because he was at the shop and I was too tired to go anywhere else.

"Ted," he said. "Mrs. Halvorsen just called me. She says there's a red pickup truck with Arizona plates parked down the block from your house. Been there since four."

"Is Paula in it?"

"She didn't say. She said, and I quote, a big man and a blonde lady who isn't blonde."

Paula had dyed her hair. Of course she had.

"Don't go over there," Wade said. "You hear me? Don't go."

I was already putting on my coat.

It happened Saturday night, not that night. Wade talked me down, and I listened, which I'll admit I don't do often. The truck left at nine. Mrs. Halvorsen reported it gone with what sounded like disappointment.

But I'd put a camera on the side door of the garage that Thursday, the same little doorbell camera Owen gave me two Christmases ago that I never got around to installing. Twenty-nine bucks on sale. It sent an alert to my phone at eleven-forty Saturday night while I was watching the late news on Wade's couch.

Motion detected. Side door.

On the little screen a big man in a ball cap was working the side door of my garage with a crowbar.

"Wade," I said.

He was already up. "I'm driving. You call 911."

I called. We were there in eleven minutes. The red pickup was in my driveway with its lights off. A second car I knew, a white SUV with a parking sticker from my wife's company, was idling at the curb with somebody in the driver's seat.

When Wade's tow truck came around the corner with its yellow lights going, the white SUV pulled away from the curb so fast it bounced off the mailbox. I saw a flash of dark blonde hair in the driver's window. Then it was gone.

The side door of my garage was hanging off one hinge. Inside, the shop lights were on, and Gene Stroud was on his knees in front of my fire safe with a four-foot pry bar wedged into the door.

He looked up when I came in. He didn't look surprised. He looked annoyed, like I'd shown up early for a dentist appointment.

"Go home, Ted," he said.

"I am home."

He stood up, and he kept the pry bar.

"Give me what's in the box," he said, "and I'll leave. Nobody gets hurt."

"The police are four minutes out, Gene."

"Then I've got four minutes."

He swung. Big, slow, a fireman's swing, like he was taking down a door. I'm fifty-eight and I sit at a desk, but I grew up throwing hay bales on my uncle's farm in Story County, and I know how to get out of the way of something heavy. The bar caught me on the left shoulder instead of the head, and it hurt like a truck hit me. Then I was inside his reach, too close for the bar to do anything, and I drove my good shoulder into his chest and we both went over the lawnmower.

He was bigger. I was madder. He got one good punch into my ribs. I got my knee onto his wrist, and the pry bar clattered across the concrete, and Wade stepped on it with a boot the size of a cinder block.

"You all done?" Wade asked him.

Gene lay on his back on my garage floor, breathing hard, looking up at the shop lights.

"She said you'd be at your brother's all night," he said to the ceiling.

"I was," I said. "Then my doorbell told on you."

The Des Moines police got there a minute later, two officers with flashlights. One of them was a young woman with a ponytail who took one look at the door, the crowbar and the fire safe and said, "Okay. Who lives here?"

"I do," I said.

"Who's this?"

"My wife's husband."

She blinked at me. "Say that again?"

"It's a long story, officer. There's a state agent who'll want to hear it."

They cuffed Gene in the driveway and put him in the back of the cruiser for burglary. He'd stopped fighting. He sat with his hands behind him and his big shoulders up around his ears, and for the first time he looked his age.

I walked over to the open window of the cruiser. My shoulder was on fire.

"Before they take you," I said, "I'm going to tell you one true thing, Gene. Just one. Then you can decide what to do with it."

"I don't want anything from you."

"She's still married to a man in El Paso. Ray Becerra. Beer truck driver. She married him in 1999 under her real name and never divorced him. That means your marriage in that little white chapel was never real. It means mine might not be either. And it means that when she cashes out, whatever she told you about Sedona, there's no law in Arizona that makes a dime of it yours." I leaned on the cruiser door. "You're not her husband, Gene. You're her number three."

He stared at me. I watched it land on him, piece by piece, the way you watch a building fall in a demolition video.

"She told me you knew," he said quietly.

"Knew what?"

"About me. She said you were in on it. That you two had an arrangement and you liked your life the way it was." He laughed, a bad sound. "I came up to see you in 2019. Sat behind you at a ballgame. Bought a hat. You were eating a hot dog with your son and you looked so… boring. I felt sorry for you."

That was the Iowa Cubs cap. Two miles from my office, in the April 2019 photo, a man was sitting behind me at a ballgame, wearing a souvenir and studying me like a used car.

"She was going to clean you out and buy a house with me," Gene said. "Sedona. Five bedrooms. She showed me the listing." He swallowed. "She was going to do the same to me, wasn't she."

"You're asking the wrong boring man."

The officer with the ponytail tapped on the roof of the car.

Gene looked up at me through the window.

"The hearing's Monday," he said. "She's going in there as the victim. She's got a whole speech." He took a breath. "I've got every text she ever sent me. Two hundred of them about you. Tell your agent I want to talk."

Monday morning the Polk County Courthouse smelled like floor wax and burnt coffee. My left arm was in a sling. Joan said that was fine, it made me look honest.

Paula was already in the courtroom when we walked in. Dark blonde hair, navy dress, little gold cross on a chain I'd never seen before. She sat next to her lawyer, a slick young guy named Brandon Tell with a tan from somewhere that wasn't Iowa in March. She turned and looked at me with big wet eyes, the eyes of a woman who'd been through something terrible.

Then she saw the sling and the eyes went flat for half a second, and I knew she knew about Saturday night.

"Teddy," she said across the aisle, soft, for the benefit of the room. "Are you hurt?"

"I fell over a lawnmower."

"You should be more careful."

"I'm learning."

Judge Ellen Strand came in, we all stood up, we all sat down. Brandon Tell went first. He was good. He talked for fifteen minutes about a wife who'd given fourteen years to a cold, controlling man, who'd had to flee her own home, who'd come home from a work trip to find her husband had been flying around the country harassing elderly relatives. He said the word stalking four times. He asked for exclusive possession of the house and four thousand dollars a month in temporary support.

Then he called Paula to the stand.

She was beautiful up there. I'll say that. She cried in the right places. She told the judge I'd become obsessed. She said she'd moved the savings because she was frightened I'd hide it.

Then it was Joan's turn. Joan stood up slowly, five-foot-nothing, reading glasses on her nose.

"Mrs. Lynch. Would you state your full legal name for the record?"

Paula blinked. "Paula Reyes Lynch."

"And your date of birth?"

"November 9, 1973."

"Where were you born?"

"San Antonio, Texas."

"Do you have a sister?"

Brandon Tell was on his feet. "Objection, relevance."

"It's very relevant, Your Honor," Joan said. "She says my client stalked her family. I'd like to know who her family is."

"I'll allow it," the judge said. "Briefly."

Paula's hands were in her lap. I watched her thumb go around and around her wedding ring.

"My family passed away," she said. "In a fire. A long time ago."

"Your Honor," Joan said, "I'd like to hand up two documents."

She gave copies to the clerk, to the judge and to Brandon Tell. The Bexar County birth certificate of Laura Inez Mendez, born March 2, 1973. The State of Texas death certificate of Paula Reyes, born November 9, 1973, died February 1974, aged three months, pneumonia.

Same birthday as the one my wife had just sworn to. A dead baby's birthday.

The courtroom got very quiet. Brandon Tell read the second document and his tan seemed to fade a shade or two.

"Mrs. Lynch," Joan said. "Who is Laura Mendez?"

Paula didn't answer. She was looking past Joan, past me, at the back of the courtroom.

I turned around. Rosario was sitting in the last row in a church dress, with her purse on her knees and her chin up. She'd flown in Sunday night on a ticket I paid for. Next to her sat Special Agent Monica Vail, and next to Vail, in an orange jail shirt with a deputy at his elbow, sat Gene Stroud.

"I'd like a recess," Brandon Tell said quickly.

"I bet you would," the judge said. "Ten minutes. And Counsel, I'd advise your client that she's still under oath."

Paula came out of that courtroom fast. Brandon tried to steer her toward an attorney conference room, but she shook him off and went straight down the hallway toward the back pew crowd, and she didn't go to Gene.

She went to her sister.

"You," she said. Her voice wasn't soft anymore. It was Texas, all of it, the accent she'd buried for thirty years coming up like water through a floor. "You fat, jealous, pathetic little hairdresser. You sent him those pictures."

"You sent them to me first," Rosario said. "Every one. Look at me, look at my life. Remember?"

"I gave Mama the best last month of her life."

"You gave Mama a paper to sign, and you took the house."

"That money was mine! I was her daughter too!"

"You were nobody's daughter!" Rosario was crying now, and she didn't care. "You were Paula Reyes. You were a dead baby's name. You buried your own sister alive in Laredo so you could marry an accountant."

Paula slapped her. Hard, across the face, the sound of it bouncing off the marble. Rosario's head turned with it. Two people on the bench by the elevator gasped. Then Paula went for her hair with both hands, and Rosario grabbed her wrists, and they were tangled up together against the wall like two teenagers in a school hallway, which is maybe the last time they'd ever touched each other.

A deputy got between them. Then two deputies. One of them held Paula's arm behind her back and she kicked at him in her nice navy heels.

Gene sat on the bench with his cuffed hands in his lap and watched his wife fight her sister. He didn't move. He didn't say anything. He looked like a man watching the house he'd been planning to buy burn down on the news.

Paula saw him, finally.

"You told them," she said. "You stupid old man. You told them."

"You were going to Sedona without me," Gene said quietly. "I read the listing again last night. It says sole owner."

That's when Special Agent Monica Vail stepped into the middle of the hallway, held up a badge and said, in that cheese-grater voice she shared with my lawyer, "Laura Inez Mendez."

Paula went still.

It was the first time in fourteen years I'd seen my wife hear her own name. She turned toward it like you turn toward a phone ringing in a dark house.

"You're under arrest," Vail said, "for aggravated identity theft, perjury and making a false statement on a marriage license. Arizona would like to discuss bigamy. So would Texas. Put your hands behind your back."

"Teddy," Paula said.

She said it to me. Not to Gene, not to her lawyer, not to her sister with a red handprint on her cheek. To me. The boring one. The bank.

"Teddy, tell them. Tell them who I am. You know me. Fourteen years. You know me."

The deputy turned her around and the cuffs went on with that little ratchet sound. She kept looking at me over her shoulder.

I reached into the inside pocket of my sport coat with my good hand. I'd brought one thing with me that morning from the garage safe. One original photo out of fifty.

The little white chapel. The yellow roses. A woman in an ivory dress, laughing.

I held it up so she could see it.

"I don't know you," I said. "I never did. But I've got your receipts."

I handed the photo to Agent Vail. And then I walked back into the courtroom, because the judge was waiting, and I had two hundred and twelve thousand dollars to talk to her about.
