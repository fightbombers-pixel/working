"Is sitting in an account her boyfriend opened for her, four months before she went to the resort. Mr. Pope, I've been doing this thirty-eight years. The ones who holler loudest about hidden money are usually the ones holding a shovel."

I didn't laugh that time. I just looked at May 14 on the statement. It was a Tuesday. I'd taken her to Olive Garden that night for no reason at all.

Mediation was in Peggy's conference room, which had a long oak table, a window over the courthouse square, and a framed photo of the Tyler Rose Parade from 1987 on the wall. The mediator was a retired district judge named Hattie Rowan, seventy-one, with silver hair cut short and a voice like a school principal.

Sandra came in at two sharp with Kirby Hollings, and right behind them, Ricky Hobbs.

He'd taken the sunglasses off for the occasion. They were hooked in the collar of his golf shirt. Without them his eyes were smaller than I'd figured, and they didn't stay still.

Charlene came too. She sat in a chair by the wall with her purse in her lap, and she didn't look at me.

"Before we start," Judge Rowan said, "I'll remind everyone that this is a confidential process, and that anything we agree to today, we agree to on paper. Mr. Hollings, I understand your client has a third party present?"

"Mr. Hobbs is Mrs. Pope's financial consultant, Your Honor. He's here to assist with valuations."

"He's here," Peggy said pleasantly, "because he's the man she took to Lake Conroe. But by all means, let him assist."

Hollings went red. Sandra stared at the table.

"Let's hear the petitioner's position," Judge Rowan said.

Hollings opened a binder.

"Mrs. Pope was married to the respondent for thirty-four years. She supported his career. When she needed a few days away to care for her own mental health, he responded by secretly diverting sixty-one thousand five hundred dollars of community money into a hidden account, cutting off her access to funds, and humiliating her with service of process at a private resort."

"A couples resort," Peggy said.

"A wellness resort," Hollings said.

"The website says couples," Peggy said. "I printed it." She slid a page down the table.

Judge Rowan didn't pick it up. She just looked at Hollings over her glasses. "Go on."

"Given the respondent's conduct, we're asking for sixty percent of the community estate and three years of maintenance."

Sandra lifted her chin. "He's been planning this for months. He's a banker. He knows how to hide money. That's what they do."

"That's not what we do," I said.

It was the first thing I'd said. Everybody turned.

I opened the brown folder Peggy had put together and took out the first two pages and slid them down the table to Sandra. She didn't want to touch them, so Hollings did.

"Those are the transfers," I said. "Twenty thousand five hundred out of checking, forty-one thousand out of savings. Exactly half of each. The time's written on both in my handwriting. Peggy filed them with the court the same afternoon. They're not hidden, Sandra. They've been in the courthouse across the street for three weeks."

Hollings flipped through his binder, looking for something he didn't have.

"Then there's this," I said, and slid the third page.

"The resort charges. Monday through Thursday, four thousand and thirty-eight dollars and twenty cents on our joint card, plus whatever went on your Dillard's card at checkout. That's community money spent on another man. Peggy tells me Texas calls that waste of the community estate. I call it the most expensive soup your daddy never ate."

Sandra's hand went flat on the table.

"And then there's this one."

I slid the last page. The savings statement. I'd drawn a box around May 14 in red pen, the way I box the line on a loan file that's going to sink it.

"Eighteen thousand dollars," I said. "Out of our joint savings, into an account ending 0912, in your name alone. Opened the day before by Ricky Hobbs. You didn't tell me. You didn't put it on your inventory. And your lawyer just stood up and accused me of hiding money."

Hollings looked at the page. Then he looked at his client. Then he looked at Ricky.

"Is this accurate?" he said quietly.

"It was for her future," Ricky said. "It's a perfectly legitimate—"

"I'm asking her," Hollings said.

Sandra didn't answer. She was looking at Ricky the way you look at a stranger who's just told you he knows your phone number.

"You said it was a rainy day fund," she said. "You said Wallace never looks at the savings."

"Sandy, let's not—"

"You said that, Ricky. At lunch. You said he reads everybody's statements but his own."

There was a knock at the door. Peggy's secretary leaned in.

"Mr. Haskins is here," she said. "And a Mrs. Kincannon, from Houston."

Ricky's face went the color of a dinner napkin. He stood up halfway.

Delbert came in first, in his pressed shirt and his bolo tie, with a cane I'd never seen him use. Behind him was a heavyset woman in a gray pantsuit carrying a rolling briefcase, the kind of woman who's been walking into rooms with bad news for a living since before Ricky Hobbs could tie his shoes.

Charlene stood up from her chair by the wall. "Daddy? What are you doing here?"

"Sit down, Charlene," Delbert said. And then, more gently, "Or come sit by me. I don't care which."

She looked at Sandra. She looked at her father. Then she picked up her purse and moved two chairs down, and sat next to the old man, and he patted her knee.

"Judge," Mrs. Kincannon said, "I apologize for the interruption. I'm Phyllis Kincannon, chief compliance officer for Piney Woods Financial Network. I was meeting Mr. Haskins down the hall to take his statement regarding a complaint he filed. When he told me who was in this room, I asked to come say one thing in front of witnesses. It'll take one minute."

Judge Rowan looked at Peggy, who shrugged, and at Hollings, who looked like a man watching a tornado through his kitchen window.

"One minute," the judge said.

Phyllis Kincannon turned to Ricky.

"Mr. Hobbs, I've been trying to reach you since Saturday. You haven't returned my calls, so I'll tell you here. Effective this morning, your registration with Piney Woods is terminated. We've reviewed the Haskins annuity replacement and four others like it. We consider them unsuitable. We are filing a termination notice with FINRA today, and the firm will be reimbursing Mr. Haskins for the surrender penalty and every dollar of fees, out of your deferred compensation."

"You can't—" Ricky started.

"I can. I did. And Mr. Hobbs? The account ending 0912. That's a Piney Woods account. Any court in Texas that asks me for those records, I'll hand them over the same day."

She turned to the judge. "That's my minute. Thank you."

Ricky sat down slowly. Then he looked at Sandra, and his face changed. All the smoothness went out of it like air out of a tire.

"You said the old man never read his mail," he said. "You said your daddy couldn't see past his own glasses. That's what you told me."

The room went quiet.

Delbert looked at his daughter across the oak table.

"I can see fine, Sandy," he said. "I just trusted you to."

Sandra put both hands over her face.

For a long time nobody said anything. Down on the square a church bell rang the half hour. Kirby Hollings closed his binder very quietly, the way you close a casket.

"Judge Rowan," he said, "I'd like to request a short recess to confer with my client."

"I imagine you would."

They went out into the hall. Ricky didn't follow. He sat at the end of the table turning his sunglasses over and over in his hands until Peggy said, "Mr. Hobbs, I don't believe you're anybody's consultant anymore," and he got up and left without a word. I heard the elevator ding.

The recess took forty minutes. When Sandra came back in, her eyes were red, and she didn't sit down across from me. She sat at the far end of the table, by the window.

Hollings did the talking.

"My client is prepared to withdraw the request for maintenance," he said. "And the allegation regarding the respondent's accounts. She'll accept a division of fifty-five percent to the respondent, forty-five to her, with the eighteen thousand in the Piney Woods account and the resort charges credited to her side. The house to be sold and the proceeds divided on the same terms."

Peggy wrote it on her legal pad and turned it toward me.

I read it twice, the way I read everything.

Then I looked down the table at my wife.

"Is that what you want, Sandra?"

She looked at me for the first time all afternoon. The resort tan was already fading.

"I want to go back to Luby's," she said, "and not say it."

"I know you do," I said. "But you said it."

I picked up Peggy's pen and signed at the bottom of the page. Judge Rowan signed under me. Hollings slid it to Sandra, and she signed it without reading it, the way she'd signed everything Ricky put in front of her, and I thought that was the saddest thing I saw all year.

Delbert got up first, leaning on the cane.

"Wallace," he said. "Walk an old man to his truck?"

"Yes, sir."

Judge Rowan was packing up her papers. Peggy capped her pen and dropped it in her purse, and looked at me over her reading glasses on the beaded chain.

"Fifty-five, forty-five, and every dollar of that resort put back on your side," she said. "Just and right, Mr. Pope."

I held the door for Delbert, and we went out into the hall together, and neither one of us looked back.
