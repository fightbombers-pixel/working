The board names Colin Simms interim chief executive officer. Motion three. The company demands the return of six hundred forty thousand dollars from Lakeshore Build LLC within ten days. If it isn't returned, the board refers the matter to the Kalamazoo County prosecutor." He looked up. "I'll vote yes on all three."

"So will I," I said.

Bart's hand was flat on his leather folder.

"Two to one," Frances said. "Passed."

Bart looked at Margaret. His face had changed. The salesman was gone out of it, and what was left looked older and meaner and a lot more like a man with a second mortgage.

"You said half of it was yours," he said to her.

"I thought it was."

"You said you'd be voting by noon. You said he'd never read a paper in his life."

"He doesn't! He never reads anything, he just…"

"He reads everything, Maggie! The man reads the labels on the breakers!" Bart stood up so fast his chair rolled back into the wall. "You told me you had him. You told me he'd fold like he always folds."

Margaret's mouth opened and closed.

"You told me Lakeshore was for us," she said. "For the lake house. You showed me pictures of the dock."

"There's no lake house," Bart said. "There's a bank in Grand Rapids that wants three hundred thousand dollars from me by Christmas. I needed a vote, Maggie. I didn't need a wife. I've had two."

Margaret made a sound I'd never heard her make in thirty-one years. It wasn't crying. It was smaller than that.

"Dad," Colin said quietly. He stood up at the end of the table and held out his hand, palm up. "Keys. Leave them on the desk."

Bart looked at his son's hand for a long time. Then he took his key ring out of his pocket and threw it on the laminate table, where it skidded past the cookie tray and stopped in front of Abby. He walked out of the conference room. Through the window I watched him cross the gravel yard to his truck, past the excavators that were half mine now, and drive away up Sprinkle Road without looking back.

Margaret's lawyer was already packing his briefcase.

"I'll be in touch about the divorce terms, Ms. Gleason," he said.

"I'm sure you will," Frances said. "Bring your reading glasses."

He left too. That left Margaret sitting alone on her side of the long table, with Bart's empty chair next to her and her purse in her lap.

She looked at me. Then she looked past me, at our daughter.

"Abby. Sweetheart. Come here. Please."

Abby didn't get up.

"You lied to me, Mom," she said. "For two months. You told me Dad was hiding things."

"I didn't know about that paper. I swear I didn't know."

"You signed it."

"I was watching TV!"

"You were laughing at the wedding," Abby said. "When Mr. Simms said that thing about Dad's hands. I was sitting at table nine. I heard you. Everybody heard you."

"It was a joke, honey."

"Dad was at the dialysis clinic at six that morning so twenty-two people could get their treatments. You know who told me that? Ray Delgado. Because I was on his crew. I was there, Mom. I held the flashlight."

Margaret turned to me.

"Lewis. We can fix this. Thirty-one years. We can still fix it."

"No," I said.

"Just like that?"

"You didn't laugh just like that, Margaret. You'd been practicing for two months."

She sat there for a while. Then she stood up, put her purse over her shoulder, and walked out without saying anything else. The door clicked shut behind her. Through the window, the Acura with the seats she liked backed out of the gravel lot and turned the other way from where Bart had gone.

Gus closed his laptop.

"Well," he said. "I'm eating a cookie."

Colin came down the table and shook my hand, a long hard handshake, the kind his father used to practice and never meant.

"I don't know how to run a company, Lewis."

"You know how to sweep a yard and read a backup log. That's more than he knew at the end. Monday morning, seven o'clock, my office. Bring coffee."

Abby picked up Bart's key ring from the table where it had landed in front of her. She turned it over in her hand. Then she looked at my hands, at the black line still under two of my nails, and she put her own hand down next to mine on the table. She had the same line under hers.

"Mom laughed because she thought blue collar meant small," she said. "It doesn't, does it, Dad? It just means you're the one who can see the work."

"That's all it ever meant, sweetheart," I said. "Now let's go. Ray's crew is short a flashlight."
