For the last twelve years it's had another man's name on the sign. I let that happen because I love my daughter, and my daughter loved her husband." He turned his head. "Darrell."

"Lucien, can we please do this in the office?"

"We did it in the office four times, son. This time we're going to do it out here."

The room had gone completely silent. Somebody's coffee cup crinkled.

"As of seven o'clock this morning," Lucien said, "Darrell Lacombe is no longer the general manager of this dealership. Or an employee of it. Or anything else to it. The corporate apartment on Crockett Street was closed yesterday. The company credit cards were cancelled at midnight. And his name comes off that sign this week." He nodded toward the window, at the big red letters out on the pole. "We're going back to Broussard."

Darrell put his coffee down on the hood of the Tahoe.

"You can't do this. I built this place. I've sold more cars than anybody in the history of this..."

"You sold my cars," Lucien said. "On my lot. With my money. And you spent my money on a loft for your finance manager." He looked past Darrell. "Ms. Langley."

Keisha jumped like somebody had pinched her.

"You signed off on the Crockett Street lease as a company expense. Nineteen months. Forty-five thousand six hundred dollars. My bookkeeper has your initials on every month." He set the microphone down on the arm of the folding chair. "Your employment is also terminated. Leave your keys and your badge with my daughter."

"Darrell," Keisha said. She turned to him. "Darrell, tell him. Tell him you approved it. You told me to sign it. You said it was fine."

Darrell stepped away from her. Not far. Just enough.

"I never told you to sign anything," he said.

"You did! You stood in my office and said 'Sign it, baby, it's for the factory reps!'"

"You're the finance manager, Keisha. Your name, your signature." He spread his hands to the room like he was closing a deal. "I'm not going down for your paperwork."

"My paperwork? You said you were leaving her! You said we'd live on Calder! You said we'd be fine!"

"Calder's gone," I said.

I didn't say it loud. But the showroom was so quiet everybody heard it.

Keisha turned around and looked at me, standing by the door in my orange vest, with my purple arm and my hard hat under it.

"The judge gave it back yesterday," I said. "Your stuff's on the front porch. All of it. Jimmie and I packed it this morning before the sun came up. We were careful with the green dress."

Darrell laughed, short and ugly. "So that's it. You got nothing, Keisha. You got no job, no house, no man. That's on you."

"No man?" Charlene said, very quietly, from beside me. "Darrell, honey, you're the one standing in a showroom you don't own, in a suit I paid for."

Two of the salesmen made a sound like a basketball crowd watching somebody get dunked on.

Darrell looked at his wife. He looked at his father-in-law. He looked at the forty-two people who had worked for him for twelve years, and not one of them would meet his eyes. Then he picked his keys up off the Tahoe's hood and started for the door. That meant he had to walk right past me.

He stopped. Up close he smelled like a lot of cologne and not enough sleep.

"You," he said. "This is all because of you. Four words. You couldn't just mind your own business."

"You were having dinner with my girlfriend, Darrell," I said. "That was my business."

He shoved past me and out the glass doors into the parking lot. Through the window, we watched him walk toward a silver Escalade with dealer plates and stop, because a porter was already standing at the driver's door with his hand out, waiting for the key.

Charlene let out a long breath beside me.

Keisha was still standing next to the stage with her folder pressed to her chest. She looked at me across the showroom, and for a second she looked like the woman I'd let move into my mother's house four years before, scared and young and sorry.

"Tyrone," she said. "Please. I don't have anywhere to go."

"You've got a sister in Port Arthur," I said. "You told me about her every night you weren't there."

I put my hard hat back on and walked out to my truck. Jimmie was leaning against the bucket with his arms folded.

"Lights on?" he said.

"Lights on."
