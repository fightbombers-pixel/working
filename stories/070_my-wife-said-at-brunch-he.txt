"He drives a trash truck. I'm embarrassed to be seen with him."

My wife said it at Sunday brunch at the Coronado Country Club, at a round table by the window with five of her friends, loud enough that the couple at the next table stopped buttering their toast. She didn't whisper it. She said it to the table, the way you announce a weather report.

Her friend Marnie Quintanilla laughed and put her hand on Lourdes's arm. "Oh, honey, we know. We've all seen him in that orange vest."

"He wore it to my Christmas party once," said Sondra Hampton, and the whole table laughed. "Straight from work. I had to open a window."

I was sitting right there. Third chair from the window, with a plate of huevos rancheros I'd barely touched. Twenty-six years married to Lourdes, and I'd driven a garbage truck for thirty-five of them.

I didn't say anything back. I smiled. When the waitress came by with the check folder, a young woman named Ana with a tired face and a ponytail, I held out my hand for it before anybody else could reach.

Three hundred and forty dollars for six people's brunch. Mimosas, mostly.

I counted out the cash from my wallet, three hundreds and two twenties, and then I took out one more hundred-dollar bill and folded it once and handed it straight to Ana.

"That one's yours," I said. "You work harder than anybody at this table."

Ana's eyes went wide. The table went quiet. Lourdes's face turned dark red from the neck up.

Twenty-two hours later, Lourdes's boss would call her at our kitchen table, and his voice would be shaking so hard she'd have to put him on speaker to understand him.

What she didn't know was the name on the bottom of a twelve-year contract in a blue binder at El Paso City Hall. Or what I'd seen at six in the morning on the last three Fridays, from the cab of my truck, on Route 9.

My name is Alberto Arredondo. I'm fifty-four years old, and I've been picking up El Paso's garbage since I was nineteen. I started on the back of a truck, hanging off the side step in a hundred and ten degrees, and I still drive one route myself every Friday. Thirty-five years on a garbage route teaches you one thing. You learn more about a family from what they put out on the curb than from anything they say in church. Hold on to that.

In the parking lot, Lourdes didn't say a word until we were in the truck. My personal truck, an old silver Silverado with a hundred and ninety thousand miles on it, which she also hated.

"You made me look like an idiot in there," she said.

"I paid the bill."

I started the truck. I didn't say what I was thinking, which was that I knew exactly what time she'd be leaving on Friday, and exactly where she'd be parked.

Monday morning, I was sitting at our kitchen table in Kern Place with a cup of coffee and the sports page when her phone rang on the counter.

"To work. Same as always."

Paso del Norte Disposal runs out of a twelve-acre yard on Gateway East, with a maintenance shop the size of an airplane hangar, a fuel island, a wash rack and a cinderblock office building painted the color of a Creamsicle, because Mr. Tafolla's wife picked it in 1998 and nobody has ever had the nerve to repaint it.

The woman who picked that color still runs my dispatch.

Concha Tafolla is sixty years old. She's five foot nothing, has the voice of a drill sergeant and has routed every truck in that yard every morning since we had four of them. She was on the radio when I came in, telling a driver on the West Side that if he missed the alley behind the Mesa Street Walgreens one more time she'd make him walk it with a trash bag.

She clicked off the radio and pointed a pencil at me.

"Route 9," I said.

Route 9 is the Rim Road area, the big old houses up on the side of the mountain with the view of the whole city and half of Mexico. Fridays are pickup day. I've driven it myself every Friday for eleven years, because the turns are tight and the driveways are steep and I don't like to give it to a new man.

Victor Hampton's house is on Rim Road. A big white Spanish-style place with a red tile roof and a four-car garage.

"Three Fridays ago," I said, "I pulled up to the Hamptons' cans a little after six in the morning. Lourdes's white Acadia was parked in their driveway. Behind his Range Rover. She'd told me she was at spin class."

"Then I give him exactly what the law says," I said. "Not one thing more."

Victor Hampton showed up at the yard on Tuesday ten minutes early, in a navy blazer and loafers with no socks. He parked his Range Rover next to a roll-off container full of broken concrete and stood there for a second looking at the Creamsicle office like it might bite him.

I watched him from the window. Then I went out to meet him, because I wanted him to walk across the yard.

You have to understand what a garbage yard is like at ten in the morning. The trucks are out, but the shop is roaring. There's the smell, which you stop noticing after about a year and which visitors never stop noticing. Victor noticed it. He breathed through his mouth all the way across the gravel.

"Mr. Arredondo." He put his hand out. "Victor Hampton. Thanks for seeing me, chief."

No waiver. Pay your fines to the city, hire licensed roll-offs, and I'll sign it."

Sondra walked up the driveway, past her husband like he was a mailbox, and stopped at the bottom of the front step and looked up at my wife.

"Get your shoes," she said. "And get out of my house."

Lourdes picked up her shoes. She came down the walk, barefoot, past Victor, past Raymond with his envelope, and she stopped at the curb next to my truck. She looked up at me through the window. Her mascara was running.

"Alberto," she said. "Please. Take me home."

"You've got your car," I said. "It's right there."

She looked back at the white Acadia in the Hamptons' driveway.

"I'm embarrassed to be seen in it," she said, very small. She meant it to sound like a joke, I think. It didn't.

"I know the feeling," I said.

I released the brake and pulled forward to the next house on Route 9, and in my side mirror I watched Victor Hampton standing in his driveway in a white bathrobe, holding an envelope he hadn't wanted to take, while his wife walked back into the house that her father's money had built and locked the door behind her.

Every October, Paso del Norte Disposal throws a barbecue for the drivers and their families. Rudy Tafolla started it in 1999, and we've never missed a year. Brisket, beans, a band from Socorro that plays Selena covers and Los Lonely Boys and a couple of polkas for the old guys, and a bounce house for the kids in the corner of the yard where the empty roll-offs usually sit.

It was the next day, Saturday. A little over two hundred people. Drivers, mechanics, office staff, their wives and husbands and kids and grandmothers. Concha was running the brisket line like she runs dispatch.

"You," she said, pointing her tongs at me when I came by for a plate. "You're on burgers. Big Rudy burned the first batch."

"I own this company, Concha."

"And I own the tongs." She handed me a spatula. "Mrs. Gallardo from Rim Road called the office yesterday afternoon, by the way. She wanted to know if the nice man who drives her route was all right after 'that business with the man in the bathrobe.' She's sending you a pie."

"How does she know I'm the owner?"

"She doesn't. She thinks you're just a driver." Concha smiled for the first time all week. "She said, 'Tell him the Hamptons always put their recycling out wrong, and he never once complained.'"

"They did put it out wrong."

"Everybody on that street knows you, mijo. They just don't know your name."

I took the spatula to the grill. Joaquin was there already, laying out buns on a sheet pan and burning his fingers on the grate because he refuses to use a towel, the same as I did at his age.

Lourdes had never once come. In twenty-six years, she'd come to exactly zero of them. "It's a garbage party," she'd said once. "It's a party at a garbage yard."

At about two in the afternoon, Joaquin came over to the grill where I was flipping burgers and stood next to me.

"Mom's here," he said.

I looked up. She was standing just inside the gate, by the fuel island, in jeans and sunglasses. She looked like she hadn't slept since Thursday. She probably hadn't. Victor's office had been padlocked by noon Friday. Sondra had filed for divorce by three. Marnie Quintanilla hadn't returned one of Lourdes's calls.

She walked across the yard toward us. People stopped talking as she went past. Drivers who'd known me for twenty years, who'd heard about the brunch by Monday afternoon, because you can't keep anything quiet in a garbage yard, they watched her go by and didn't say anything.

She stopped in front of the grill.

"Can we talk?" she said.

"I'm cooking."

"Please, Alberto. Just five minutes."

"Go ahead."

She looked around at the drivers, at the families, at Concha with her arms folded by the brisket. She took her sunglasses off.

"I made a mistake," she said. "A terrible mistake. I know that. I lost my job. I lost my friends. Victor, Victor was a lie, all of it. I see that now. I want to come home. I want to fix it. Twenty-six years, Alberto. Doesn't that mean anything?"

"It meant something on Sunday," I said. "At brunch. You told five of your friends you were embarrassed to be seen with me. Twenty-six years, and that's what you had to say about them."

"I didn't mean it."

"You meant it enough to say it loud."

She started crying. Not big crying. Just tears under the sunglasses she'd pushed up on her head.

"Joaquin," she said, turning to our son. "Tell your father. Please. Tell him to give me another chance."

Joaquin set down the tray of buns he was holding. He looked at his mother for a long moment. Then he looked around the yard. At the drivers and their families. At Concha. At Big Rudy holding his granddaughter up so she could see the band. At the two hundred people who'd come to eat brisket at a garbage yard because the man who owned it still drove a route every Friday.

"Mom," he said quietly. "You said you were embarrassed to be seen with him." He waved his hand at the crowd. "Look how many people came here today just to be seen with him."

Lourdes stared at our son.

Then she looked at me, waiting.

I flipped the last burger onto the platter, wiped my hands on the towel in my back pocket, and picked up the platter.

"Go home, Lourdes," I said. "My people are hungry."
