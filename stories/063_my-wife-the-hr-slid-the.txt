"Accept the sixty percent salary reduction, Clarence, or I'll find someone who will."

My wife said that at seven in the morning on a Monday, standing at the front of the fabrication shop at Pearl River Fire Protection in Jackson, Mississippi, in front of thirty-four pipe fitters holding coffee cups at the weekly safety meeting. She's the director of human resources. She said it into the little handheld microphone we use so the guys in the back can hear over the compressors.

Then she slid a sheet of paper across the steel layout table toward me, the same table I'd been drawing sprinkler systems on for twenty-six years.

I looked down at it. My salary was $112,000. The number on the new offer was $44,800. Under it there was a line for my signature and a date, already typed in.

Nobody in that shop breathed. Somebody's radio was playing gospel in the back by the pipe threader, and somebody reached over and turned it off.

"I need twenty-four hours," I said.

Then I folded the paper twice, put it in the chest pocket of my work shirt, and went on with the safety meeting. Ladder inspections. Hot work permits. The new rule about hard hats in the yard. I read every item off my clipboard while my wife stood by the door with her arms crossed and thirty-four men tried very hard to look anywhere but at either one of us.

Twenty-six hours later, she was standing in that same shop with a letter from the Mississippi State Fire Marshal in her hand, and a lawyer from Atlanta on speakerphone was telling her to stop talking.

What she didn't know, sliding that paper across my table, was whose name was printed on the certificate framed over the front desk.

My name is Clarence Mays. I'm 58, and I design fire sprinkler systems. Hospitals, schools, warehouses, churches, the big casino down in Biloxi. I hold a NICET Level Four, which is the highest certification there is in my trade, and there are eleven men in the whole state of Mississippi who have one. The thing my trade teaches you is that a sprinkler system sits up in the ceiling doing nothing for thirty years, and nobody thinks about it, and nobody thanks it, right up until the day there's a fire. Then it's the only thing in the building that matters. Remember that.

When the meeting broke up, the men filed out into the yard slowly, in twos and threes, not talking. Yvonne went back to her office without looking at me. Her heels clicked across the concrete and the office door shut.

Dub Pickering, my shop foreman, waited until the last fitter was out the bay door. Then he came over and leaned on the layout table next to me.

Dub is 49, big and bald, with forearms like hams and a Bible verse tattooed on one of them. He's been with the company nineteen years. I trained him myself.

"Sixty percent," he said.

"Sixty percent."

"She cut me ten. Last Friday. Called me into her office and said times are tight and the new owners want efficiency." He shook his head. "Ten I can swallow. Sixty? Clarence, that ain't a pay cut. That's an eviction notice."

"I know."

"In front of the whole shop. With a microphone." He rubbed his head. "Why would she do that to you? You're her husband."

"I don't know yet."

Dub looked at the office door. Then he looked at the floor. Then he looked at me. He had the face of a man who's been carrying something heavy for a while and just found out where to set it down.

"Clarence," he said. "I need to tell you something. And I've been praying on whether to tell you for about three weeks."

"Then tell me."

"Not here." He glanced at the office door again. "Lunch. My truck."

"Dub..."

"Lunch," he said. "Please. I don't want her seeing my face when I say it."

We ate in his Silverado in the far corner of the yard, under a pecan tree, with the windows down and the heat already coming up off the gravel. He had a ham sandwich he didn't touch. I had nothing.

"Three weeks ago Saturday," Dub said, "I came in to pick up my level. I left it in the gang box. It was about nine at night. Jasper's Tahoe was in the lot. And Yvonne's Camry."

I didn't say anything.

"The front office lights were off," Dub said. "But the light was on in Jasper's office. The blinds were down. I heard her laughing." He stared through the windshield. "I got my level and I left. I didn't see anything. I want to be clear on that, Clarence. I didn't see anything. But a man doesn't need to see everything."

"No," I said. "He doesn't."

Jasper Moody was 38. He'd been our CEO for eleven months, ever since his father, Lou Moody, died of a heart attack on the fourteenth tee at Annandale. Lou founded Pearl River Fire Protection in 1983 with two trucks and a borrowed pipe threader. He hired me in 1998 when I was a fitter with a bad attitude and a good eye, and he paid for every certification I ever got. He was my mentor, and the closest thing to a father I had after my own died.

Jasper went to Ole Miss and then to an MBA program in Atlanta and then came home to run his daddy's company. He wore quarter-zip sweaters with the company logo on them and said things in meetings like "synergy" and "lean out." He'd never threaded a pipe in his life.

"Dub," I said. "Is there anything else?"

He took a breath. "The guys are saying the company's getting sold. Some outfit out of Atlanta. Private equity. Jasper's been showing men in suits around the shop for a month. That's why Yvonne's cutting everybody. To make the numbers look good for the buyers."

"Who told you that?"

"Rosie at the front desk. She's been making the coffee for their meetings. She says they're closing Friday."

"This Friday?"

"This Friday."

I sat with that a while. A mockingbird was going crazy in the pecan tree.

"Why tell me now?" I asked. "Why not three weeks ago?"

"Because three weeks ago I thought maybe it was nothing. Maybe they were working late on the sale." Dub finally picked up his sandwich, looked at it, and put it down again. "But this morning she stood up in front of all of us and tried to cut you down to nothing, Clarence. In front of the men you trained. That wasn't business. That was personal. And I watched her do it with a smile on her face."

He looked at me.

"You've got twenty-four hours," he said. "What are you going to do with them?"

I reached into my chest pocket and touched the folded paper.

"I'm going to go home and talk to my wife," I said.

Yvonne got home at six. I was sitting at the kitchen table with the folded paper laid out flat in front of me and a glass of sweet tea going warm.

We've been married twenty-nine years. We have a son, Andre, who's twenty-seven and in the Air Force at Keesler, and a daughter, Danita, who's twenty-four and teaching third grade in Hattiesburg. We've lived in the same brick house in Clinton since 1999. Yvonne's 55, with silver threading through her hair that she started letting show last year, and she was the prettiest girl at Jackson State when I met her.

She put her purse on the counter and looked at the paper.

"I don't want to fight about this at home," she said.

"You didn't want to fight about it at work either. You wanted to do it on a microphone."

She sighed the way she sighs at the end of a long day. "Clarence. It's business. It's not personal. The new owners want lean operations. Everybody's taking a cut."

"Dub's taking ten. I'm taking sixty."

"Dub's not making a hundred and twelve thousand dollars."

"Neither is anybody else in that shop with a Level Four."

"There are other designers."

"Name one in Mississippi who's not already employed."

She opened the refrigerator and stared into it like it might have an answer in there. "This was decided above my head."

"By Jasper."

"By the board."

"Jasper is the board, Yvonne. Lou left him fifty-one percent."

She closed the refrigerator. "You know what your problem is, Clarence? You think that company can't run without you. You think because Lou loved you, you're untouchable. But Lou's dead. And Jasper's not his daddy. He's got a plan for that company, and frankly, you're a line item. An expensive one."

"A line item."

"I didn't mean it like that."

"You said it on a microphone this morning. You meant it like that."

"I said it in front of the staff because Jasper wanted it done in front of the staff." She stopped. She'd said more than she meant to. "Transparency. That's all. So the men can see everybody's sacrificing."

"Everybody except Jasper."

"Jasper's not taking a salary until the sale closes."

"Until the sale closes Friday."

Her eyes went to me sharp. "Who told you that?"

"Is it true?"

"That's confidential."

"So it's true."

She didn't answer. She picked up her purse again, like she'd forgotten something in the car, and then set it back down.

"Sign the paper, Clarence," she said, quieter. "Please. Sign it and keep your head down for a year. After the sale things will settle and you can ask for a raise. Fifty-eight years old, nobody's going to hire you at what you make. You need us a lot more than we need you."

"Who's 'us,' Yvonne?"

She looked at me.

"You keep saying 'us' and 'we.' You and the company. You and the board. You and Jasper." I kept my voice steady. "Twenty-nine years, you've said 'us' and meant you and me. When did that change?"

She stood very still in the middle of our kitchen with her hand on her purse strap.

"I'm going to take a bath," she said. "We'll talk tomorrow. After you sign."

She went upstairs. I heard the water run.

I sat at the table for a while. Then I went out to the garage, to the old steel filing cabinet where I keep twenty-six years of drawings and every certificate I've ever earned, and I opened the bottom drawer.

In the very back, behind my tax returns, was a long white envelope with PEARL RIVER FIRE PROTECTION printed in the corner, and my name written across the front in shaky blue ink.

Lou Moody had handed it to me in the hospital the week before he died, from his bed, with the oxygen hissing in his nose. He'd said, "Don't open this unless you need it." I'd put it in this drawer and never touched it.

I opened it now.

Inside was a single business card. DELTA FIRE & SPRINKLER, JACKSON, MS. ODELL FAIRLEY, OWNER. And on the back, in Lou's handwriting, five words.

"If Jasper runs you off."

I called the number on the card from the garage, standing between my truck and the lawn mower, at a quarter to eight at night.

It rang four times. Then a deep, slow voice with sixty years of Mississippi in it said, "Fairley."

"Mr. Fairley, this is Clarence Mays. From Pearl River."

There was a pause on the line. Then I heard a chair creak, like a big man leaning back.

"Well," Odell Fairley said. "Well, well. Clarence Mays."

"I'm sorry to call so late."

"Son, I've been waiting on this call for eleven months. You could call me at three in the morning on Christmas and I'd answer." He chuckled. "Let me guess. Jasper finally did something stupid."

"How did you know?"

"Because Lou Moody sat in my office the spring before he died, drinking my coffee, and told me he was worried about two things. His heart, and his boy." Odell's voice softened. "Lou and I competed for thirty-five years. Bid against each other on every job from Memphis to the Gulf. But we went to the same church and we fished the same lake, and we respected each other. He told me, 'Odell, my boy's smart, but he's never had to build anything. If he ever runs Clarence off, you hire him the same day. Don't let him go to Birmingham. Keep him in Mississippi.' I gave him my word."

I leaned against my truck. It felt like Lou was standing in the garage.

"He gave me your card in the hospital," I said. "With a note on the back."

"I know he did. He told me he would." Odell paused. "So. What happened?"

I told him. The microphone. The paper. Sixty percent. Yvonne. The sale on Friday. I didn't tell him about Dub's story. That wasn't anybody's business but mine.

When I was done, Odell was quiet for a long time.

"In front of the whole shop," he said finally.

"Thirty-four men."

"And your own wife read it out." He made a low sound. "Clarence, I'm going to tell you something, and I need you to listen close. Do you know who's buying Pearl River?"

"Some outfit from Atlanta. That's all I know."

"Bluestem Partners. Private equity. They buy up family contracting businesses across the South and roll them together." He paused. "Their lawyer called me two weeks ago."

"Called you? Why?"

"Due diligence. They were asking around about Pearl River's reputation. And they asked about you by name. They wanted to know if Clarence Mays was the real deal, because they'd seen your name on the state license and on every hospital job in the backlog. I told them you were the best sprinkler designer in the state, and if they were buying Pearl River, they were really buying you." He chuckled again. "That lawyer got real quiet when I said that."

I stood very still in the garage.

"Clarence," Odell said. "You understand what I'm telling you? Go look at that certificate in your file cabinet. Not the NICET one. The other one."

I didn't have to look. I knew exactly what he meant.

In Mississippi, a fire sprinkler contractor can't design or install a single sprinkler head without a license from the State Fire Marshal's office. And every licensed company has to name one person, a certified individual, who is legally responsible for every system the company puts in. The state calls him the responsible managing employee. Without that person, the company's license is suspended until it names a new one, and the new one has to be approved by the state. That takes weeks. Sometimes months.

For twenty-two years, at Pearl River Fire Protection, that person had been me. My name was on the certificate in a gold frame over Rosie's front desk. Lou had hung it there himself. He used to tap the glass when he walked by.

"They forgot," I said. "Jasper and Yvonne. They forgot whose name is on the license."

"Or they never knew," Odell said. "Jasper's never pulled a permit in his life. And your wife works in human resources. She sees your salary. She doesn't see your stamp."

"What happens if I leave?"

"You send a letter to the Fire Marshal's office withdrawing as the responsible managing employee. It's your right. You're not their property. The day they get it, Pearl River's license goes inactive until they name somebody else and the state approves him." Odell's voice was steady, like a man reading a blueprint. "And Bluestem's lawyers are going to read about it in their due diligence about two minutes after it happens. They are not going to close on a fire sprinkler company that can't legally install a fire sprinkler."

"The hospital job," I said slowly. "Baptist. Three point two million. Permit's in review right now. Under my name."

"Then I expect that permit goes on hold too."

I closed my eyes.

"Mr. Fairley," I said. "I'm not trying to burn Lou's company down."

"I know you're not, son. That's why Lou trusted you. But you're not the one holding the match." He let that sit. "Here's my offer. Same title you have now. Operations manager and chief designer. A hundred and twenty-five thousand to start, a truck, and five percent of the company if you'll stay ten years. I'm seventy. I don't have a son who wants it. I'd rather it go to a man who knows the work."

I couldn't say anything for a while.

"Do you want to think about it?" Odell asked.

"She gave me twenty-four hours," I said. "I've used twelve."

"Then use the next twelve sleeping. Come by my office at seven in the morning. I'll have the offer typed up. And Clarence?"

"Yes, sir?"

"Bring a pen of your own. Lou always said you were particular about pens."

I went inside at nine. The bath water had drained. Yvonne was in bed with the lamp off, facing the wall, either asleep or pretending.

Her phone was on the nightstand, charging, face down. I didn't touch it. I didn't need to. I lay down on my side of the bed in the dark and listened to her breathe, and I thought about twenty-nine years, and about a laugh in an office with the blinds down on a Saturday night.

At five-thirty I got up, showered, put on a clean work shirt, and drove to Delta Fire & Sprinkler on Highway 80.

Delta Fire & Sprinkler is a long metal building with a gravel lot full of white trucks, and at six in the morning the lights were already on. Odell Fairley met me at the door himself. He was bigger than I'd pictured from his voice, broad as a door, in a short-sleeved dress shirt and suspenders, with a straw fedora on a hook behind his desk.

His office smelled like coffee and pipe dope. On the wall behind him, framed, was a newspaper clipping from 1991: a photograph of two young men in hard hats standing on the roof of the old Jackson Mall, shaking hands. One of them was Odell. The other was Lou Moody.

"Fire at the mall," Odell said, seeing me look. "Both our crews showed up. We put the sprinkler loop back together in fourteen hours, side by side, his pipe and my heads. The paper called us the odd couple." He sat down heavily. "He beat me on every bid for the next five years. I never forgave him."

"He talked about you," I said. "He said you were the only competitor he ever lost sleep over."

"Good." Odell smiled. "Coffee?"

"Please."

He poured it into a mug that said DELTA in faded red letters, and then slid two things across the desk. One was a typed offer letter in a leather folder. The other was a blank sheet of paper with the State Fire Marshal's licensing fax number written across the top in pencil.

"Withdrawal first," Odell said. "Then the offer. Do it in the right order. If anybody ever asks, you resigned your license responsibility before you ever took a job with me. Lou's boy might hire a lawyer. Let his lawyer find nothing."

So I sat at Odell Fairley's desk and wrote the letter in my own hand, two paragraphs. I, Clarence Mays, certificate number such-and-such, hereby withdraw as responsible managing employee for Pearl River Fire Protection, effective immediately. I signed it with Lou's Parker. Odell's office manager, a quiet woman named Bernadine who'd come in early just for this, typed it up clean and fed it into the fax machine. We listened to it screech and hum and go through.

Then Odell's desk phone rang. He picked it up, listened, and handed it to me.

"Mr. Mays? This is Pam Whitfield at the State Fire Marshal's licensing office. I'm in early. I just got your withdrawal." She sounded like she'd known me for years, and she had, in a way. I'd been sending her paperwork since 1998. "I have to ask, for the record. Is this voluntary? Nobody's forcing you?"

"Nobody's forcing me, Ms. Whitfield. It's voluntary."

"Then I'll process it first thing. Pearl River's license will show inactive by the end of the hour. They'll get an automatic notice by email." She hesitated. "Mr. Mays, can I say something off the record?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"In twenty years, I've never seen your name on a single violation. Not one. Whoever lost you just lost the cleanest license in the state."

When I hung up, Odell was watching me over his coffee cup.

"Now the offer?" he said.

"Not here."

He raised an eyebrow.

"I want to sign it in Lou's parking lot," I said. "Where he can see it."

Odell looked at the clipping of the two young men on the roof for a long moment. Then he nodded.

"Nine-thirty," he said. "I'll bring it."

At seven-fifteen, I was back in the Pearl River lot, an hour before Yvonne usually got in.

But her Camry was already there. Parked right next to Jasper's black Tahoe.

I sat in my truck for a minute. Then I picked up a manila folder off the passenger seat and walked into the building.

The front office was quiet. Rosie wasn't in yet. The coffee maker was gurgling. And the light was on in Jasper's office, the door half open, and I could hear them talking.

"He'll sign," Yvonne was saying. "He always folds. He's a pushover, Jasper. He's been a pushover for twenty-nine years. That's what I'm telling you."

"He better," Jasper said. "Bluestem wants the payroll number by Wednesday. If his salary's not cut, the whole valuation shifts. And I'm not losing two million over a pipe fitter."

"Designer."

"Whatever he is." A pause. A softer voice. "And then Friday, after the wire hits, we tell him the rest."

"Friday," Yvonne said. "After the wire."

I stepped into the doorway.

"Good morning," I said.

They both turned. Yvonne was sitting on the corner of Jasper's desk with a cup of coffee in her hand. Jasper was in his chair in a gray Pearl River quarter-zip, leaning back with his feet on an open drawer. For about three seconds nobody moved.

"Clarence," Yvonne said. She stood up off the desk too fast and spilled coffee on her wrist. "You're early."

"I'm always early. You told me that once. You said it was my best quality."

Jasper put his feet down. He gave me the smile he must have learned in Atlanta, wide and white and empty.

"Clarence! Great. Glad you're here. Saves us a trip. You've had a chance to think about the offer?"

"I have."

"And?"

I set the manila folder on his desk. On top of it, I laid the folded paper from yesterday, the one with $44,800 on it. I'd never unfolded it again.

"I'm declining," I said.

Jasper laughed. "Declining. Okay. Well, I respect that. Of course. Then we'll process your separation today, and we'll start a search. Yvonne, can you..."

"I'm not done," I said.

He stopped.

I opened the folder. On top was a typed letter on plain white paper, two paragraphs, signed by me in blue ink with my own pen.

"This is my resignation," I said. "Effective at the end of today. And under it," I lifted the first sheet so he could see the second, "is a copy of the letter I faxed to the Mississippi State Fire Marshal's office this morning from Odell Fairley's office, withdrawing as Pearl River's responsible managing employee on the fire sprinkler contractor license."

Jasper looked at the letter. Then at me. Then at Yvonne.

"What's a responsible managing employee?" he said.

Yvonne didn't answer him. She was staring at the second letter, and I watched her read it twice, the way she reads benefits paperwork, line by line, with her lips moving a little.

"Jasper," I said, "every fire sprinkler company in Mississippi has to name one certified person who's legally responsible for every system it designs and installs. The State Fire Marshal holds that person accountable. Not the CEO. Not the owner. Him." I nodded toward the front office. "For twenty-two years at this company, that's been me. My name's on the certificate in the gold frame over Rosie's desk. Your father hung it there."

"So we put somebody else's name on it."

"Who?"

"Whoever. Another designer."

"With a Level Four? There are eleven of us in this state. Ten of them have jobs. And now the eleventh does too." I tapped the letter. "When the state gets this, and they already have it, your license goes inactive until you name a replacement and the state approves him. That's weeks. Until then you can't legally design a system, pull a permit or put a single head in a ceiling."

Jasper's face did something I'd never seen it do. The salesman smile stayed up, but the color behind it went out.

"The Baptist job," Yvonne said, very quietly.

"The Baptist job permit's in review under my license," I said. "It'll be on hold this afternoon."

"That's three point two million," Jasper said. "That's the backlog. That's the whole backlog. Bluestem's valuation is built on that backlog."

"I know."

"You can't do this."

"I didn't do anything, Jasper. Your HR director offered me forty-four thousand eight hundred dollars a year in front of thirty-four men. I declined. People decline offers every day."

He stood up so fast his chair hit the wall.

"You did this on purpose. You and Fairley. This is sabotage. I'll sue you. I'll sue you both."

"For what? Quitting?"

"For... for interference! For breach of..." He grabbed for words like a man grabbing for a railing in the dark. "Yvonne. Yvonne, he's got a non-compete, right? Tell him he's got a non-compete."

Yvonne didn't look up from the letter.

"He doesn't," she said. "Your father never made him sign one. I checked his file last month. When we were planning the cut."

"When you were planning the cut," I said.

She looked up at me then, and her eyes were wet, and for the first time in twenty-nine years I didn't know what was behind them.

"Clarence," she said. "Why didn't you just sign it?"

"You gave me twenty-four hours," I said. "I used them."

I picked up my folder and left them there, in Jasper's office, with the blinds half up and the coffee going cold.

By nine o'clock the whole building knew.

Rosie had come in at eight and found my resignation letter taped to the gold frame over her desk, where I'd put it on my way out of Jasper's office. Rosie told Dub. Dub told the shop. And when I came back from loading the last of my drafting tools into my truck, the bay doors were open and thirty-four men in Pearl River work shirts were standing in the fabrication shop with their arms crossed, not working.

Yvonne came out of the front office into the shop with a sheet of paper in her hand and a cordless office phone held against her chest. Jasper was right behind her, red in the face.

"Okay, everybody, can I have your attention," Yvonne said. Nobody handed her the microphone. She didn't ask for it. "There's been a misunderstanding. Clarence is..."

"Clarence quit," somebody said from the back.

"Clarence is transitioning," Jasper said. "And we're going to have a new designer within the week."

"Who?" Dub said.

"That's not your concern."

"It's my concern if I'm hanging pipe on a job with no license," Dub said. "Because if the state walks onto a job site and finds us installing without a responsible managing employee, it's my fitters who get run off, and my name that goes on the violation."

Jasper opened his mouth.

The phone against Yvonne's chest started talking. Somebody on the other end was saying "Hello? Hello? Ms. Mays, are you still there?"

Yvonne fumbled and hit a button. A woman's voice came out of the speaker, crisp, with an Atlanta accent.

"This is Karen Ashworth, counsel for Bluestem Partners. Who am I speaking with?"

"Yvonne Mays. Director of human resources. Mr. Moody is right here."

"Mr. Moody. I've just received notice from the Mississippi State Fire Marshal's office that your fire sprinkler contractor license was placed in inactive status at eight-forty this morning for failure to maintain a certified responsible managing employee. Can you explain that?"

The shop was dead silent. Thirty-four men listened to a lawyer in Atlanta through a cordless phone the size of a candy bar.

"It's a paperwork issue," Jasper said. "Our designer resigned. We're replacing him."

"Your designer," Karen Ashworth said. "Clarence Mays."

"Yes."

"Mr. Moody, our entire valuation of Pearl River is built on a backlog of permitted work that is under Mr. Mays's license. Our due diligence report names him as a key person. I flagged him personally, after I spoke to your competitor, Mr. Fairley. And you told me, in writing, two weeks ago, that key personnel were stable and under no risk of departure." Paper rustled on the line. "Did something happen to make Mr. Mays leave?"

Jasper looked at Yvonne. Yvonne looked at the floor. And in the back of the shop, a fitter named Leon who'd worked for us for thirty years said, loud and clear, "She cut him sixty percent on a microphone yesterday morning. We all heard it."

There was a long pause from Atlanta.

"I see," Karen Ashworth said. "Then I'm going to advise my client to suspend closing pending a full review. Friday's wire will not go out. And Mr. Moody, I'll remind you that under the letter of intent, you represented that all salary reductions were voluntary and applied equally."

"They were voluntary," Yvonne said quickly. "He could have said no."

"He did say no," Karen Ashworth said. "That appears to be the problem." Another rustle of paper. "Ms. Mays, I'd also suggest you not participate further in this call. I don't think it's appropriate."

"Why?" Yvonne said. "I'm the HR director. I handle personnel."

There was a short pause. When the lawyer spoke again her voice was flatter, careful.

"Ms. Mays, under the post-closing organizational plan Mr. Moody signed three weeks ago, the human resources function at Pearl River is being outsourced to Bluestem's shared services group in Atlanta. Your position isn't in the new structure. I assumed you'd been told."

Yvonne turned slowly to look at Jasper.

"Three weeks ago," she said.

"Yvonne..."

"You signed that three weeks ago? When you told me I'd be vice president after the sale? When you told me Friday we'd tell Clarence everything and then we could stop sneaking around?"

The whole shop heard it. Thirty-four men. Dub closed his eyes like a man at a funeral.

Jasper reached for the phone. "Karen, I'll call you back."

"Please do," said Karen Ashworth, and the line went dead.

Yvonne stood in the middle of the fabrication shop with the dead phone in one hand and the Fire Marshal's notice in the other. She looked at Jasper. Then she looked at me.

"Three weeks ago Saturday," Dub said quietly, to nobody in particular. "Lights off in the front office. Light on in his."

Yvonne's knees buckled a little and she caught herself on the layout table, my old table, the one she'd slid the paper across the day before.

Jasper looked around the shop for one friendly face. There wasn't one. Thirty-four men who'd been told yesterday to tighten their belts for the good of the company stood there with their arms crossed, and the CEO in his quarter-zip sweater turned around and walked back into the front office without a word.

While the shop was still standing there in the quiet after the phone call, Rosie came out of the front office.

Rosie Calhoun is 63, and she's answered the phone at Pearl River since the day Lou opened the doors. She's small and round, with reading glasses on a chain and a cardigan she wears even in July because she says the air conditioning is set for men who sweat. In her arms she was carrying the gold frame from over her desk, with my resignation letter still taped to the glass.

She walked straight past Jasper and past Yvonne and stopped in front of me.

"Lou told me something," she said. "About two years ago. He said, 'Rosie, if Clarence ever walks out that door for good, you take that certificate off the wall and you give it to him. It's his. It was always his. I just got to hang it.'" She held it out. "I'm doing what Lou told me."

"That's company property," Jasper said.

"It's a piece of paper with Clarence's name on it in a frame Lou bought at Walmart for eleven dollars," Rosie said, without turning around. "I'll pay you the eleven dollars."

I took it. The glass was warm from her arms. Through it, under my own resignation letter, I could see my name in raised gold letters, and the State Fire Marshal's seal, and the little smudge in the corner where Lou used to tap it every morning on his way to the coffee pot.

"Thank you, Rosie."

"Don't thank me," she said. "Just call me when Mr. Fairley needs somebody to answer his phone."

At nine-thirty, a white Ford F-250 with DELTA FIRE & SPRINKLER on the door pulled into the Pearl River lot and parked right next to my truck.

Odell Fairley climbed down out of it slowly in his straw fedora, with the leather folder under his arm. Half the shop drifted out into the yard behind me to watch. Jasper came out too, and Yvonne behind him, keeping her distance.

"Mr. Fairley," I said.

"Clarence." He shook my hand. Then he looked past me at the building, at the sign out front that Lou had hand-painted in 1983. "I told you I'd bring it. You said you wanted to sign it here."

"Lou would have wanted to see it."

"He's seeing it," Odell said, and opened the folder on the hood of my truck, right next to the gold frame Rosie had given me.

It was two pages. Operations manager and chief designer, Delta Fire & Sprinkler. A hundred and twenty-five thousand dollars. A company truck. Five percent ownership after ten years.

Jasper came across the gravel. "Odell. Mr. Fairley. You can't poach my people in my own parking lot."

"Your daddy asked me to, son," Odell said, without looking up. "Eleven months ago, in my office. I gave him my word. Ask anybody at First Baptist if Odell Fairley keeps his word."

Jasper stopped like he'd walked into a glass door.

I took out my pen. Lou always said I was particular about pens. It's a black Parker he gave me the day I got my Level Four. I signed both pages on the hood of my truck, in the Pearl River parking lot, in front of half the shop.

When I straightened up, Dub was standing right there.

"Mr. Fairley," Dub said. "You got room for a shop foreman? I don't want a raise. I just want to work for a man who's going to have a license next week."

Odell looked at him. Then at me.

"He's the best foreman in Mississippi," I said.

"Then he's hired," Odell said. "And anybody else who wants to follow him, my office opens at seven."

Behind Dub, Leon raised his hand. Then a young fitter named Shawn. Then two more. Then I stopped counting, because Yvonne was walking toward me across the gravel, slow, with her arms wrapped around herself like it was cold.

"Clarence," she said. Her voice broke on it. "Please. I made a mistake. I made a terrible mistake. He lied to me. He lied to me about everything."

"He did."

"I'm going to lose my job. You heard her. I'm going to lose my job and I don't have anything. Please. Can we go home and talk? Just talk. Twenty-nine years. Please."

Thirty men in the yard were watching us. Odell had taken off his fedora and was looking at the sky, politely.

I thought about the microphone. About the paper sliding across my table. About "he's a pushover" through a half-open door at seven in the morning.

"Yesterday," I said, "you stood up in front of these men and told me if I wouldn't take what you offered, you'd find somebody who would."

"I know. I know I did."

"Well, I found somebody who would," I said, and I handed Odell back his folder, signed.
