"You're overacting, Glen. It's just a wedding."

My wife said it across a tasting table at the Riverbend Barn on a Saturday afternoon, in front of our daughter, her fiancé, his parents, the wedding planner and the man who'd just been handed my job. Eight people heard it. A couple of them laughed, the polite kind of laugh you do with your mouth full of a sample crab cake.

The job was walking my daughter down the aisle.

"Dad, don't look at me like that," Hayley said. She's twenty-seven, a dental hygienist, and she has my mother's chin, which she sticks out when she's already decided something. "Stan's walking me. We talked about it. And honestly, Mom and I think it's better if you're just not there. It would be awkward. You'd be sitting in the back all weird."

"Not there," I said. "At your wedding."

"It's just a wedding," Joanna said again.

I'd been putting money into Hayley's wedding fund since the week she was born. Twenty-seven years, a little out of every paycheck. Fifty-eight thousand dollars, sitting in a savings account with both our names on it. I'd brought the folder with the statements to the tasting, because I'm a banker, and bankers bring folders.

Stan Dodson smiled at me from the head of the table. Sixty-one, silver hair, a tan from somewhere that wasn't Indiana, a blazer with brass buttons. He'd joined my wife's church choir eight months ago, and since then he'd been to more of our family dinners than my brother had in ten years.

On the little mock-up of the head table, the planner had set out paper name cards. Mine said "Father of the Bride" in gold script.

I reached over, picked up my card, and slid it across the tablecloth until it stopped against Stan's wine glass.

"Then you'll want this," I said. "You've got the seat."

I stood up, tucked my folder under my arm and buttoned my jacket.

Three days later, my phone had forty-seven missed calls on it, and the first voicemail was my daughter calling me "Daddy" for the first time since she was twelve.

What she didn't know was what I'd seen on the bottom of the check Stan Dodson handed the planner twenty minutes before.

My name is Glen Whitaker. I'm fifty-nine, and I manage the Jackson Street branch of the Delaware County Credit Union in Muncie, Indiana. Thirty-four years in banking. You learn a lot of things in thirty-four years, but the first one they teach you is the one that matters most: read the bottom of a check first. The name on top can say anything. The numbers on the bottom tell you who's really paying. Remember that.

Twenty minutes earlier, Stan had made a big show of it. He'd pulled a cashier's check out of his blazer and handed it to Mona Pickett, the planner, and said, "For the upgrade. The string quartet and the champagne tower. My gift to the bride." Everybody had clapped. Hayley had hugged him. And I'd been sitting close enough to read the line of numbers along the bottom edge, upside down, the way I've read ten thousand of them across a teller counter.

I knew that routing number. It belonged to a little savings bank in Kokomo that was bought out and shut down in 2019. Our branch still had a memo about it pinned in the back office, because people kept trying to deposit old checks from it.

I was halfway across the gravel lot when I heard heels behind me.

"Mr. Whitaker? Mr. Whitaker, wait."

It was Mona, the planner. Forties, headset still around her neck, clipboard pressed to her chest.

"I'm so sorry about in there," she said. "I've done three hundred weddings. That was a first."

"It's not your fault, Mona."

"I just..." She looked back at the barn. "Mr. Dodson told me to deposit his check Monday and book the quartet. Your wife said it was fine. But you're the father of the bride, and you're a bank guy, and you looked at it really funny when he handed it over."

So I had looked at it funny. I've never had a poker face.

"Can I give you some free advice, Mona?"

"Please."

"Before you book anything, call the bank printed on that check. Not the number on the check. Look the bank up yourself."

She looked down at the check, then back up at me.

"Is something wrong with it?"

"I'm just a guy who's not invited to the wedding," I said. "But I'd call first."

She nodded slowly and walked back toward the barn, and halfway there she stopped and turned the check over in her hand and looked at the bottom of it.

The front door of the barn opened, and Stan Dodson came out onto the porch with a glass of the sample wine, and he watched Mona looking at his check, and then he looked across the lot at me. He raised his glass.

I got into my car and drove home.

Joanna got home around seven. Hayley was with her. I heard them laughing in the driveway before they came in, and they stopped laughing when they saw me at the kitchen table with my laptop open.

"Oh, don't start," Joanna said, putting her purse down.

"I haven't said anything."

"You walked out of the tasting. Connor's mother asked me if you were sick."

"What did you tell her?"

"That you get like this."

Hayley went to the fridge and got a seltzer and leaned against the counter, arms folded, chin out.

"Dad, seriously. It's not personal."

"It's my daughter's wedding and I'm not invited. It's a little personal, honey."

"It's because of the way you are about Stan. You're always so suspicious. You're always asking him questions. It's weird. He's been so good to us."

"What kind of questions?"

"Like what bank he uses. Like what his company is called. Like, who asks that?"

"Bankers," I said.

"Exactly," Joanna said. "Not everything's a loan application, Glen."

I turned the laptop around so they could both see it.

"Then explain this one to me."

It was our joint savings account. The wedding fund. The balance on top said two hundred and eleven dollars and fourteen cents. And the line below it, from yesterday afternoon, said: Transfer out, fifty-eight thousand dollars, to SD Holdings LLC.

Hayley stepped closer and read it. Joanna didn't even look.

"I know about that," she said. "I did that."

"You moved the whole wedding fund. Twenty-seven years. To a company called SD Holdings."

"It's Stan's company. He's handling everything now, the venue, the honeymoon, all of it. He gets thirty percent off through his resort partners. He's going to put the kids up in a villa in Cancun for ten days. Ten days, Glen. We were going to do a weekend in Indianapolis."

"Did you sign anything?"

"I didn't need to sign anything, it's Stan."

"Joanna. Who is SD Holdings? Where's it registered? Who's on the account?"

"Oh my God." She threw her hands up. "You're doing it right now. You're interrogating."

"That was our daughter's wedding money."

"And it's going to our daughter's wedding! What's your problem?"

"My problem is that the check he gave Mona today is drawn on a bank that closed six years ago."

That stopped the room for about a second and a half. Hayley looked at her mother. Joanna looked at me like I'd tracked mud on the carpet.

"That's ridiculous."

"I read the routing number."

"Upside down. Across a table. With a crab cake in your mouth." She laughed. "Glen, you're overacting. It's just a wedding."

"It's fifty-eight thousand dollars."

"It's a wedding!" Hayley said, and her voice went up and cracked. "Why does everything have to be a number with you? Stan actually cares about what I want! He asked me what flowers I wanted, Dad. You asked me what the venue's cancellation policy was."

I didn't have an answer for that, because she was right. I had asked about the cancellation policy. It's what I know how to do.

She grabbed her keys off the counter.

"I'm going to Connor's. Mom, call me."

The door slammed. Joanna stood there for a minute, then picked up her purse and went upstairs, and I heard the bedroom door close, and then I heard her on the phone, low and warm, the voice she used to use on me.

I sat at that table for a long time.

You have to understand, it wasn't just one tasting. It was eight months of it. It was Stan at Thanksgiving carving the turkey I'd bought, because Joanna said he had "such good hands." It was Joanna coming home from choir practice at eleven at night when choir ended at eight-thirty. It was Hayley's birthday dinner at the steakhouse downtown, where Stan made a toast about "the man every girl deserves in her corner," and everyone looked at him and not at me, and I paid the bill. It was a hundred little things, and I'd been the quiet guy in the corner reading the bottom of all of them.

Around ten, I opened a different browser tab. I typed in a website I'd had saved for nine years, a charter outfit in Key West run by a captain named Rollie Sims. My father always said he wanted to go out with Rollie for sailfish before he died. He never did. He died in 2016 with the website still bookmarked on his old desktop, and I'd copied it onto mine.

There was a cancellation. A three-day charter, starting Monday.

I booked it. Two thousand one hundred dollars, out of my own checking account, the one only my paycheck goes into. I booked a flight out of Indianapolis for Sunday morning. And then I sent my wife a text from the kitchen, even though she was right upstairs.

"Taking my dad's trip. Back Thursday. Have a nice week."

She texted back one word.

"Fine."

Captain Rollie Sims was seventy-one years old, about five foot six, and burned the color of a saddle. He met me at the dock on Monday morning at six with two cups of Cuban coffee and looked me up and down.

"You're Ed Whitaker's boy."

"I am. He talked about you for twenty years."

"He called me every January for twenty years to book a trip, and every January he canceled." Rollie handed me a coffee. "Said his wife wouldn't let him spend the money. Said next year."

"That sounds about right."

"Well, next year's here." He jumped down into the boat, light as a teenager. "Get in. Leave your phone in the cooler. Nothing good ever came from a phone on a boat."

I did leave it in the cooler, all day Monday. We didn't catch a sailfish, but we caught two mahi and a barracuda with teeth like a bear trap, and Rollie told me stories about my father I'd never heard, about a girl in Pensacola in 1968 and a bar fight in Mobile my father swore he didn't start. I laughed so hard I had to sit down on the cooler. It was the first time I'd laughed like that since Christmas.

That night at the motel, I took the phone out. There were four missed calls from Joanna and a text that said, "Where's the checkbook for the blue account?" I didn't answer it. The blue account was my account.

Tuesday we went out farther. Around eleven in the morning, Rollie hooked into something big and handed me the rod, and for forty minutes I fought a sailfish across that green water while Rollie yelled instructions in my ear. When it jumped, all that blue fin standing up against the sky, I yelled out loud. I didn't even know I could make that sound.

We let it go. Rollie says you always let the first one go.

I was sitting in the fighting chair, shaking and grinning, when I heard the phone buzzing inside the cooler. It wasn't one buzz. It was buzzing like a hornet trapped in a jar.

"Leave it," Rollie said.

"It's been buzzing for a while."

"That's what I'm saying."

I opened the cooler anyway. The screen said forty-seven missed calls. I read the number out loud, and Rollie whistled through his teeth.

"Forty-seven. Somebody died or somebody's broke."

Joanna, eleven. Hayley, nineteen. Connor, six. A number I didn't know, five. My brother Arlen, three. Mona Pickett, three. And the newest voicemail was from Hayley, from ten minutes before.

I played it on speaker, because the wind was too loud otherwise.

"Daddy." Her voice was all broken up. "Daddy, please call me. Please. Something's wrong. Stan's not answering. Nobody can find him. Please call me back."

Daddy. She hadn't called me that since she was twelve and got stung by a wasp at Prairie Creek.

I called her back. She picked up before it even rang on my end.

"Dad!"

"I'm here, honey."

"Dad, the barn called. Mona called. The check Stan gave her bounced. It didn't just bounce, the bank said the bank doesn't exist anymore. And then Mona called the resort in Cancun, and they said they've never heard of Stan Dodson, there's no villa, there's no booking, there's nothing." She was crying so hard she was hiccuping. "And Stan's phone goes to a recording. And Mom's account..."

"What about Mom's account?"

There was a scuffle, and then Joanna was on the phone.

"Glen." Her voice was flat in a way I'd never heard before. "Glen, he took my mother's money."

"What money?"

"The inheritance. The ninety thousand. From Mom's house. It was in my name only, you never..." She stopped and started again. "He said he could get eleven percent on it through his resort partners. He said I should move it before the wedding, while the rates were good. I moved it last Wednesday. To SD Holdings."

"And now?"

"Now the bank says SD Holdings closed the account yesterday morning. Cash withdrawal and a wire. They won't tell me where the wire went. Glen, they won't tell me anything."

"Because it's not your account, Joanna. It's his."

"Glen, please. You're a banker. Fix it. Call somebody."

I looked out at the water. Rollie was looking at me with his arms folded.

"How much did you send him altogether?"

"I don't..."

"Joanna. How much."

"The wedding fund, fifty-eight. Mom's money, ninety." She took a breath that rattled. "And Connor's parents. Wes and Pam gave him forty for the honeymoon villa. Half of it. The other half's due Thursday."

"Due Thursday."

"He told them to bring a check to the barn on Thursday, when he meets with Mona."

"Is he still meeting with Mona on Thursday?"

"I don't know. He texted me this morning. One text. He said there was a bank glitch and not to panic and he'd fix everything Thursday."

A hundred and eighty-eight thousand dollars, and he wasn't done yet. He was coming back for the other twenty.

"Don't text him back," I said. "Don't call him. Don't tell anybody what you just told me. I'm getting on a plane tonight."

"You're coming home?"

"I'm coming home."

"Glen..." She hesitated. "I'm sorry. About the tasting."

"We'll talk about it at the house."

I hung up. Rollie unfolded his arms and started reeling in the lines.

"We're going in," he said.

"Rollie, I paid for the whole day."

"Your dad paid for twenty years of days he never took. You've got credit." He fired up the engine. "Go home and get your money, son."

I landed in Indianapolis a little before one in the morning and drove the hour up to Muncie with the radio off.

The kitchen light was on when I pulled in. Joanna and Hayley were both at the table, in their bathrobes, with cold coffee and a pile of printouts, and both of them stood up when I came in, like I was a doctor walking into a waiting room.

Hayley hugged me first. She held on for a long time.

"I'm sorry, Daddy," she said into my shoulder. "I'm so sorry I said that. About you not being there."

"We'll get to that. Sit down. Let me see what you've got."

What they had was bank statements and screenshots of texts, and none of it was the right stuff. I sat down, opened my laptop, and pulled up the Indiana Secretary of State's business search, which is public and free, and which anybody in this county could have looked up in two minutes for the last eight months.

"SD Holdings LLC," I said, typing. "Here it is. Registered in March. Eight months ago."

"The month he joined the choir," Joanna said quietly.

"Registered agent." I scrolled. "Stanley R. Dodd. With an address in Anderson."

"Dodd," Hayley said. "Not Dodson."

"Not Dodson."

Then I took the name over to the Arizona courts website, which is also public, because Stan had always talked about Scottsdale. It took me four minutes. Stanley R. Dodd, Maricopa County, 2016. Theft and fraudulent schemes. A widow in Mesa and her retirement account. He did three years.

I turned the laptop around so they could see it. The mug shot was eleven years younger and the hair wasn't silver yet, but it was the same smile. The one he'd raised his glass to me with.

Joanna put her hand over her mouth. Hayley started crying again, quietly.

"He sang 'How Great Thou Art' at Mom's memorial," Joanna whispered. "He held my hand at the cemetery."

"That's how they work," I said. "They find somebody who just lost somebody."

"How did you know? How did you know at the tasting?"

"The bottom of the check," I said. "I always read the bottom of the check."

At eight in the morning, I called the Muncie Police Department and asked for someone who handled fraud. At nine-thirty, a detective named Alma Reyes was sitting at my kitchen table with a notebook. She was maybe forty-five, short hair, a voice like she'd heard every story twice.

"Mrs. Whitaker, I'm going to need the date and amount of every transfer. Mr. Whitaker, can I see that court record again?"

I showed her. She photographed the screen.

"Dodd. He's done this before. He'll have done it more than once." She wrote something down. "Here's the honest part. A hundred and eighty-eight thousand moved by wire and cash, it's probably gone, or most of it. Wires move fast. I'll get subpoenas going for the SD Holdings account today, but that takes time, and he knows it takes time."

"He's coming back Thursday," I said.

She looked up.

"For what?"

"Twenty thousand more. My future son-in-law's parents owe him the second half on Thursday. He told my wife he'll be at the barn to meet the planner. He texted her yesterday morning: bank glitch, don't panic, he'll fix everything Thursday."

Detective Reyes put her pen down.

"He's coming back to the barn for a check."

"That's what con men do," I said. "They always come back for one more. In thirty-four years, I've never seen one leave money on the table."

Joanna's phone buzzed on the counter. All four of us looked at it. She picked it up with two fingers like it was hot, and read it, and then she turned the screen around.

It was from Stan.

"Sweetheart, sorted it all out with the bank. Silly mistake on their end. Tell Wes and Pam to bring their check to the barn Thursday at two. Don't tell Glen anything, you know how he overacts. Can't wait to see you. S."

Detective Reyes read it twice.

"Mrs. Whitaker," she said, "I need you to text him back exactly what I tell you."

The text Detective Reyes wrote was short, and Joanna typed it with shaking thumbs.

"Thank God. Wes and Pam will be there at two. Glen's still in Florida, don't worry."

Three dots appeared. Then a heart. Then: "That's my girl."

Joanna put the phone face down on the table and walked out of the kitchen, and I heard the back door open and close.

Reyes stood up and put her notebook away.

"I'll set it up with my lieutenant. Plainclothes in the parking lot, a car on the county road. The Bateses bring an envelope, not a real check, and we take him when he reaches for it. You stay home."

"I'm the father of the bride."

"You're a witness and a victim, Mr. Whitaker, and you're a fifty-nine-year-old bank manager."

"I'm also the only person he'll recognize if he sends somebody else to pick it up," I said. "He knows Wes and Pam from two dinners. He's been looking at me across a table for eight months."

She looked at me for a long moment.

"You stay in the barn. Inside. Away from the windows. You come out when I say so."

"Deal."

When she was gone, I went out the back door. Joanna was sitting on the steps of the deck, in her bathrobe, looking at the bird feeder I'd put up the spring Hayley graduated high school.

I sat down next to her. Not close.

"How long?" I said.

She didn't pretend not to understand.

"Since June."

"Choir ended at eight-thirty."

"I know when choir ended, Glen."

"He told you to keep me away from the wedding."

She turned and looked at me.

"How did you know that?"

"I didn't. I do now."

She looked back at the bird feeder.

"He said you'd ruin it. He said you were always looking for something wrong, and you'd find something wrong, and Hayley would be heartbroken on her wedding day. He said it would be kinder if you weren't there." She laughed, a horrible little laugh. "Kinder. He said he'd walk her because he loved her like his own."

"And you agreed."

"I agreed." She wiped her face with the sleeve of her robe. "And then I told her it was my idea. And she believed me, because why wouldn't she."

"You told her I'd sit in the back all weird."

"I said that."

"You said I was overacting."

"I said that too." She turned to face me. "Glen, I don't know what I was doing. He made me feel like I was twenty-five. You made me feel like a budget."

"I made you a budget because we didn't have money, Joanna. I built that wedding fund out of a budget. Twenty-seven years. Thirty dollars a week, then fifty, then a hundred. Every raise I ever got, a piece of it went into that account."

"I know."

"And you sent it to a man you'd known eight months, without asking me, because he told you I'd overact."

She didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say.

"I'm not going to yell at you," I said. "I'm not going to do anything today. Today I'm going to help get our daughter's money back, and the Bateses' money, and your mother's money if there's any left. After that, we'll talk about the rest of it with a lawyer."

"A lawyer."

"A lawyer."

She nodded slowly, like she'd known it was coming since Saturday and was only now hearing it out loud. I got up and went inside.

I went into my little office off the garage and called the divorce lawyer our credit union sends members to when they ask, a woman with an office on Walnut Street who'd once told me she'd seen every way a marriage can end and most of them twice. I told her about the fifty-eight thousand and the ninety and the choir. She told me to write down every date while I still remembered it and to bring the bank statements Monday. "And Mr. Whitaker," she said, "money spent on a boyfriend comes out of her half, not yours. Indiana judges are old-fashioned about that." I wrote that down too.

Wes and Pam Bates came over at four. Wes is a big man, sixty, a retired line foreman for the power company, and Pam is a little woman who runs the front office at the middle school and doesn't miss anything. Connor came with them, and Hayley came downstairs, and for a while the six of us sat in the living room like people at a funeral home.

Wes looked at me across the coffee table.

"Twenty thousand," he said. "Our twenty. For a villa that doesn't exist."

"I'm sorry, Wes."

"You're sorry? You're the only one who saw it." He shook his head. "Pam told me Saturday night. She said, Glen looked at that check like it had a snake on it. I said Glen's just sore because he got cut. I said that."

"Everybody said that," Hayley said quietly.

Connor reached over and took her hand. He's twenty-nine, an apprentice with an HVAC outfit, a quiet kid who'd never said more than ten words to me at a time. Now he looked straight at me.

"Mr. Whitaker. What do you need us to do Thursday?"

"Detective Reyes will call your dad tonight," I said. "Wes, she's going to give you an envelope. You and Pam drive up to the barn at two, same as he told you. You park out front. You go in and sit at the tasting table with Mona. When he comes in and asks for the check, you hand it to him."

"And then what?"

"And then you let go of it."

Wes cracked his knuckles, one at a time, slowly.

"I'd like to do more than let go of it."

"I know you would," I said. "So would I. But we're going to let Detective Reyes do her job, because if either one of us touches him first, he walks out of court with a lawsuit instead of handcuffs."

Pam put her hand on her husband's knee.

"He's right, Wes."

"I know he's right. I just don't have to like it."

Thursday, I got to the Riverbend Barn at one o'clock, an hour before anybody else, and Mona let me in the back door by the kitchen.

She'd called the bank on the check Saturday night, just like I'd told her, and then she'd called the resort in Cancun, and then she'd sat in her car in the parking lot and cried, she told me, because she'd done three hundred weddings and never had one blow up before the rehearsal.

"I want you to know," she said, setting a coffee in front of me in the back office, "I never deposited it. I put it in my drawer. Mr. Dodson called Monday asking if it cleared. I told him the bank was slow."

"You did good, Mona."

"I did what you said." She looked out the office window at the parking lot. "Is he really going to show up?"

"He'll show up. He thinks there's twenty thousand dollars sitting on that table."

Detective Reyes had two people in the lot, a man in a landscaping truck pretending to work on the flower beds and a woman in a minivan pretending to wait for somebody. Reyes herself was in the kitchen behind me, in jeans and a windbreaker, with a radio clipped to her hip.

At five minutes to two, Wes and Pam's Buick pulled into the lot. Wes got out holding a white envelope like it weighed fifty pounds.

At two, nothing.

At ten after, nothing. I watched Pam check her watch through the window. Mona went out and brought them sweet tea and sat with them at the tasting table, the same table where Joanna had said it was just a wedding.

At twenty after two, a silver Camry I'd never seen came down the gravel drive slowly and parked at the far end of the lot, by the road, nose out. Nobody got out.

"That him?" Reyes said behind me.

"I don't know that car."

"Rental plates," the radio crackled. "Driver's alone. Male. Ball cap."

We waited. The Camry sat there. I could see the man in it looking at the barn, and at the Buick, and at the landscaping truck.

Then the driver's door opened and Stan Dodson got out.

No blazer this time. A gray windbreaker and a ball cap pulled low, and sunglasses. But he walked the same way, rolling a little, like a man walking into a party where everybody was going to be glad to see him. He came up the porch steps, took the cap off, ran a hand through the silver hair, and put the smile back on.

He walked in through the big front doors, and I heard his voice from the tasting room, warm as maple syrup.

"Wes! Pam! So sorry I'm late, traffic on 67 was murder. Mona, darling, you look lovely."

"Hello, Stan," Pam said. Her voice was perfectly steady. I'll never forget that.

"So. Let's get you folks squared away so you can get on with your day." Chairs scraped. "That for me, Wes?"

There was a pause.

"It's for the villa," Wes said.

"For the villa. Absolutely. Ten days in paradise for those two kids."

I was watching through the gap in the kitchen door. I saw Wes hold out the envelope. I saw Stan reach for it. And I saw Stan's eyes slide, just for a second, past Wes's shoulder, to the kitchen door where I was standing.

I don't know what gave me away. Maybe the light from the kitchen window behind me. Maybe a con man just knows when he's being watched by the one guy who reads the bottom of the check.

He didn't take the envelope. He turned and went for the front door, fast.

"Police! Stanley Dodd, stop!" Reyes was past me and through the door before I could move.

Stan went out the front doors and down the porch steps two at a time, and the man in the landscaping truck came around the flower bed, and Stan cut left, away from him, across the gravel toward the Camry.

I was already out the side door by the kitchen. I hadn't planned it. My legs just went. The side door of the Riverbend Barn opens onto the end of the parking lot nearest the road, and the Camry was parked right there, nose out, ready to run, and I got to the driver's door about two steps before he did.

He pulled up short, breathing hard, sunglasses gone, maybe ten feet from me.

"Glen," he said. "Glen, buddy. Florida."

"I came back early."

"Get out of my way."

"No."

He looked over his shoulder. Reyes and the landscaper were coming across the gravel. He looked back at me, and the smile was all the way gone now, and what was under it was just an old man who'd been caught.

"Move, you boring son of a witch."

"Make me, Stan."

He came at me. He swung, a big wild right hand, the kind of punch a man throws who's never had to throw one, and it caught me on the cheekbone and knocked me back against the Camry. I tasted blood. And then something my father taught me in the backyard when I was fourteen came back from wherever it had been for forty-five years, and I grabbed his windbreaker with both hands and turned and drove him down onto the hood of his own rental car, and held him there with my forearm across his back.

He was a lot lighter than he looked. Con men usually are.

"Don't," I said in his ear. "Just don't."

Reyes got there a second later. She had his wrists cuffed behind him in about three seconds flat.

"Stanley Dodd, you're under arrest for theft and fraud. Anything you say..."

She read it to him on the hood of the Camry while the gravel dust settled, and I stepped back and leaned on the next car over and held my face, which was starting to throb. Wes came across the lot at a jog for a big man, and Pam behind him, and Mona out on the porch with her hands over her mouth.

Reyes pulled Stan upright. He looked at me over her shoulder, and he laughed, a tired, ugly laugh.

"You know why I told her to keep you away from the wedding, Glen?"

"Tell me."

"Because I knew you'd be the one." He shook his head. "Every family's got one. One guy who reads everything. One guy who asks what bank. I've done this nine times, Glen. Nine families. The trick is always the same. You find the one guy who reads the bottom of the check, and you get the family to throw him out for you. They always do it. They thank you for it." He grinned with blood on his lip from where his face had hit the hood. "Your wife and your little girl did it for me in one afternoon."

The landscaper opened the back door of the unmarked car and Reyes put a hand on Stan's head and folded him into it.

"Nine," Reyes said to me quietly, shutting the door. "I'm going to need that number on the record."

"He's going to say it again," I said. "Men like that can't help it."

Wes put a hand on my shoulder.

"You're bleeding, Glen."

"I'll live."

The minivan woman found two bags in the trunk of the Camry. One had clothes in it. The other one had banded cash in it, a lot of banded cash, and two plane tickets to Cancun dated for the Sunday after the wedding. One said Stanley Dodd. The other one said Joanna Whitaker.

Reyes held the second ticket up so I could see it, and didn't say anything, and put it in an evidence bag.

"How much cash?" I asked.

"We'll count it at the station. If I had to guess from the bands, sixty, maybe seventy." She looked at the bag. "Some of it's coming back to somebody. Not all of it."

Joanna's car came down the drive about five minutes later. Reyes had told her to stay home, and she hadn't. She parked by the Buick and got out and saw the unmarked car with Stan in the back, and then she saw me on the porch steps with blood on my shirt, and she stopped walking.

"Glen. Oh God, Glen, what did he..."

"Mrs. Whitaker." Reyes walked over to her, holding the evidence bag at her side. "I need to ask you something, and I need you to think before you answer, because it's going in my report."

"Okay."

Reyes held up the bag.

"This was in Mr. Dodd's car. A ticket to Cancun, for the Sunday after your daughter's wedding. In your name. Were you planning to travel with him?"

Joanna looked at the ticket. Then she looked at me. Then she looked at Wes and Pam Bates, who were standing right there by their Buick, and at Mona on the porch, and I watched her understand that every person in that parking lot had paid for that ticket one way or another.

"He said it was a surprise," she said. "He said after the wedding we'd go see the villa. Just to look."

"So you knew about the ticket."

"I knew he was getting one. I didn't..." Her voice went up thin. "I was going to come back, Glen. It was just a week. I was going to come back."

Pam Bates said, very quietly, "With our twenty thousand dollars."

Joanna turned around like she'd been slapped.

"Pam, I didn't know..."

"You knew about the ticket," Pam said. "You knew you were going to Mexico with him a day after our kids got married. And you sat at that table on Saturday and told your husband he was overacting."

Nobody said anything. A truck went by out on the county road.

Joanna looked at me one more time, waiting for me to say it was okay, the way I'd always said it was okay for thirty-one years.

"Ride with the detective, Joanna," I said. "Tell her everything. All of it. Then call a lawyer, because I already called mine."

Reyes opened the passenger door of her own car. Joanna got in without another word.

I was sitting on the porch steps with a bag of ice from Mona's kitchen on my face when Hayley's car came down the gravel drive way too fast and stopped crooked. She got out and ran to me, and stopped short when she saw my face.

"Daddy. Oh my God. Your face."

"It looks worse than it is."

"Mona called me. She said Stan hit you." She sat down on the step next to me. Her hands were shaking. "She said he said... he said he got Mom and me to throw you out. That he does it to every family."

"He said that."

"Nine families."

"Nine."

She looked out at the unmarked car pulling out of the lot, with Stan in the back seat looking at nothing.

"He made us do his dirty work for him," she said. "And we thanked him."

"He's good at what he does, honey. That's why it worked."

She was quiet for a while. Then she took a folded piece of paper out of her purse. It was the gold name card from the tasting table. "Father of the Bride." It had a wine ring on one corner.

"Mona saved it," she said. "She gave it to me Tuesday. I've been carrying it around."

She held it out.

"I know there's no money," she said. "I know there's probably no barn, no quartet, no nothing. I don't care. I'll get married at the courthouse. I'll get married in the backyard. But will you walk me down the aisle, Daddy? Please?"

I took the card and looked at it for a long time, with the ice melting down my wrist. Then I put it in my shirt pocket, behind my reading glasses, where I keep the things that matter.

"Honey," I said, "that's the only seat I ever wanted. Yes."
