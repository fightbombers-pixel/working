"The house is mine now!"

That was the subject line. My wife had typed it in capital letters and sent it to me a little before seven on a Thursday morning, and I opened it at a corner booth in Brownie's Cafe in Yuma, Arizona, across the table from three men I've eaten breakfast with every Thursday for nineteen years.

Fidel Soto was buttering a biscuit. He saw my face and stopped.

The email had two attachments. The first was a thirty-one-page petition for dissolution of marriage, filed in Yuma County Superior Court. The second was a photograph.

My wife, Carmen, in a white bikini top and a pair of my old sunglasses, sitting on the deck of my family's cabin in Pinetop, Arizona, with the pines behind her and a cup of my coffee in her hand. Next to her, with his arm around her shoulders, was a shirtless young man with a tan and a backward ball cap who could not possibly have been more than twenty-five.

He was twenty-three. I found that out later.

Under the photo she'd typed: Kaden and I are staying here now. Don't bother coming up. My lawyer says it's mine. Twenty-seven years, Raul. I earned it.

The cabin was worth about six hundred and forty thousand dollars. My father built it with his own hands in 1978, one log at a time, on weekends, driving four hours each way from Yuma in a pickup with no air conditioning.

I read the email twice. I looked at the picture for a while. Then I set the phone face up on the table so the guys could see it, and I picked up my cup and finished my coffee. All of it. Slowly. It was a good cup of coffee. Brownie's always has good coffee.

Fidel didn't say anything. Neither did the other two. They just watched me.

When the cup was empty, I picked the phone back up and typed four words.

Are you sure about that?

I hit send. Then I went into my settings, and with the three of them watching, I blocked her number and then her email, and I set the phone face down on the table and asked our waitress for a refill.

By sundown, a young man's pickup truck was headed down the mountain without her, and the boat she'd been riding on all week was sitting in a neighbor's barn.

What Carmen didn't know, sitting on that deck in my sunglasses, was whose names were on a piece of paper in a fireproof box in my sister's closet in Flagstaff. And that one of them was hers.

My name is Raul Escobar. I'm 56, and I'm the water master for the irrigation district that feeds about seventy thousand acres of lettuce and alfalfa outside Yuma. My job is to decide which canal gate opens when, and how far, so the Colorado River gets to the farmers who ordered it. I've done it for twenty-two years.

Here's what that job teaches you. Water doesn't go where people think it's going. It goes where the gates send it. Somebody can stand at the end of a ditch and swear up and down that water's coming, and if the gate's closed upstream, they're going to stand there all day getting nothing but dust. Hold on to that.

Fidel finally put his biscuit down.

"Raul," he said. "Who in the world is the kid?"

"I've never seen him before in my life."

"He looks like he's in high school."

"He looks like he sells jet ski rentals at Lake Havasu," said Tino Galvez, who runs the pump station on the Gila side.

Fidel leaned over and looked at the photo again.

"Is that your coffee mug?" he asked.

"That's my dad's coffee mug. He got it at a gas station in Holbrook in 1979."

"And she's drinking out of it."

"She is."

Fidel sat back. He's sixty-two, a ditch rider who's worked under me for fifteen years, and he has known Carmen almost as long as I have.

"You don't seem too upset," he said.

"I'm plenty upset."

"Then why'd you ask her if she's sure? You're not going to fight her for it?"

"Fidel," I said, "I'm not going to fight anybody for anything. I'm going to call my sister."

I paid for breakfast, all four of us, and I went out to my district truck in the parking lot and sat behind the wheel with the air running, and I called Graciela.

My sister is three years older than me. She was a court reporter in Coconino County for thirty years, which means she has spent three decades sitting quietly in the corner of rooms where people lied, typing every word they said. She retired to Flagstaff and does the crossword in pen.

She answered on the second ring.

"You're up early."

"Carmen filed for divorce," I said. "She emailed me the papers this morning. With a picture of herself on Dad's deck with a twenty-something kid. She says the cabin's hers now."

There was a long silence on the line. Then my sister did something I hadn't heard her do since our mother's funeral. She laughed. A short, dry laugh, like a stick breaking.

"She said that?"

"In capital letters."

"Raul, did you tell her?"

"I asked her if she was sure."

"That's all?"

"That's all."

"And then?"

"And then I blocked her."

Graciela laughed again. I heard her get up from her chair, and I heard her walk down her hallway, and I heard her open the closet door where she keeps the fireproof box Mom and Dad gave her the year they got sick.

"I'm holding it," she said. "Escobar Family Ranch LLC. Articles of organization, 2005. Operating agreement, 2005. The deed from Mom and Dad to the company. And the other thing."

"The spousal consent."

"Signed, dated and notarized," Graciela said. "Carmen M. Escobar. In her own hand. At Dad's kitchen table in Yuma, the week after Thanksgiving, 2005. I was there. I typed it."

I sat in the district truck and looked at the canal behind Brownie's, running full and brown in the morning light.

"She doesn't remember it," I said.

"Of course she doesn't. Dad put the papers in front of everybody after the turkey. Me, you, Carmen, my Ernesto, God rest him. Said it was for the taxes. Carmen signed it without reading it, between her second piece of pie and her third. I remember because she got pumpkin on the corner of the page." My sister paused. "It's still there. The pumpkin."

I didn't say anything for a minute.

"Raul?"

"I'm here."

"Who's at the cabin right now? Besides her and the boy?"

"Nobody. Ernie Begay checks on it Tuesdays."

"Then call Ernie," Graciela said. "And find yourself a lawyer who understands companies. I'll drive down tomorrow with the box."

I called Ernie Begay next. Ernie is about seventy, a retired Forest Service fire lookout who lives two miles down the road from the cabin and has looked after it for us since my father died. He does it for two hundred dollars a month and all the firewood he can split.

"Raul," he said. "I was going to call you. There's been a black pickup in your drive since Monday. Lifted. Big tires. Arizona plate. Music all hours. I walked over Tuesday to check the water heater and a young fella came to the door with no shirt on and told me to get off his property."

"His property."

"That's what he said. I asked him who he was. He said he was Carmen's friend and she owned the place now and I didn't work there anymore." Ernie paused. "I didn't figure that was right."

"It's not right, Ernie."

"I didn't figure. Your dad never would've let that happen."

"Ernie, has she been using the pontoon?"

"I saw the cover off it yesterday. They had it out on Rainbow Lake with a cooler."

"Okay," I said. "Thank you, Ernie. Don't go over there again. I'll call you tomorrow."

"Raul?"

"Yeah."

"The young fella," Ernie said. "He had a sign in the window of his truck. For sale. With a phone number."

"His truck's for sale?"

"That's what it looked like. Big orange sign. Make an offer."

I hung up and sat there with the engine running. A young man with no shirt and a lifted pickup he was trying to sell, living in a cabin he thought my wife owned.

I opened my phone, unblocked nothing, and searched Facebook Marketplace for black lifted trucks in Show Low.

He was the third listing. 2021 Ram 2500, Laramie, lifted, thirty-five-inch tires, MUST SELL, BEHIND ON PAYMENTS, NEED GONE BY END OF MONTH. Make an offer. Kaden R.

And underneath it, in the related listings, posted that same morning, was something else.

22-foot Bennington pontoon boat with trailer, 2016, lightly used, garage kept, Pinetop. $19,500 OBO. Cash only. Must sell fast.

The picture was my father's boat, sitting on its trailer beside the cabin. The seller's name was Carmen E.

I drove straight from Brownie's to the office of Rosalia Trevino.

Rosalia does business law out of a stucco building on Fourth Avenue across from the old courthouse, mostly for farmers and packing sheds, the kind of people who need contracts read and companies set up and partnerships taken apart without anybody getting shot. She's in her forties, very small, very fast, and she reads documents the way I read a canal schedule, all at once.

She read Carmen's petition in about four minutes. Then she looked at the photo on my phone.

"Is he old enough to rent a car?" she asked.

"Barely."

"Okay." She set my phone down. "Arizona is a community property state, Mr. Escobar. In general, anything either of you earned or bought during the marriage belongs to both of you, half and half. That includes your salary, your retirement from the irrigation district, your house in Yuma, the money in your joint accounts. Your wife's lawyer is assuming the cabin falls in the same pot."

"It doesn't."

"Tell me why."

I told her. My father building it in 1978. My parents putting it into Escobar Family Ranch LLC in 2005, for the taxes, and to keep it out of probate. My mother dying in 2011 and my father in 2014, and their membership interests passing in equal halves to me and Graciela under their wills. Graciela keeping the box.

Rosalia's pen was moving the whole time.

"So the cabin isn't owned by you," she said. "It's owned by a company. And your half of the company came to you by inheritance."

"Yes."

"In Arizona, property you inherit, even during a marriage, is your separate property. Not community. Your wife has no claim to your membership interest in that LLC, and the LLC owns the cabin, so she has no claim to the cabin. Not one board of it." She looked up. "Unless the company was ever paid for with community money. Did you ever put marital money into that cabin? A new roof? Property taxes out of your joint account?"

"The company pays its own taxes. Graciela and I each put in twelve hundred a year from our own money into the company account for upkeep. My twelve hundred comes out of an account in my name that Dad left me. Carmen's never been on it."

"Good. Is your wife a member of the company? Did she ever sign anything?"

"She signed one thing. In 2005. A spousal consent."

Rosalia's pen stopped.

"Describe it."

"My father's lawyer drew it up. Every spouse signed one. Graciela's husband, Carmen. It says the spouse understands the company interest is the member's separate property, that the spouse has no interest in the company or its property, and that the spouse waives any claim to it in a divorce."

Rosalia set her pen down very carefully.

"Notarized?"

"Graciela says it is."

"Who has the original?"

"Graciela. She's driving it down from Flagstaff tomorrow. With pumpkin pie on the corner."

Rosalia looked at me like I'd said something in a foreign language. Then she shook her head and picked her pen back up.

"Mr. Escobar, I'd like to see the operating agreement before I say anything I'll regret. But if that consent says what you say it says, then your wife isn't living in her house." She tapped the photo on my phone. "She's a guest. In a house owned by a company she has no part in. And a guest can be told to leave."

"Who tells her?"

"Under most operating agreements, a manager or any member can revoke permission for anybody to use company property. In writing. Does yours say that?"

"Dad's says any member can."

"And the house in Yuma?" I asked. "The savings? My retirement?"

"Community," Rosalia said. "Half hers. Half yours. That part's not a fight, Mr. Escobar, that's arithmetic."

"Then I'll split it fair," I said. "Every dollar we built together, she gets half. I don't want anything that's hers. I just don't want her taking what was my father's."

Rosalia looked at me for a moment over her desk.

"Most people in that chair want me to find a way to get more," she said.

"Most people in that chair didn't carry the logs."

She nodded slowly, like she was writing that down somewhere in her head.

"Then get your sister to put it in writing," Rosalia said. "Today. Signed by both of you. Delivered by somebody who isn't you."

I called Graciela from the parking lot. She'd already pulled the operating agreement out of the box and was reading it to me before I finished my first sentence.

"Section eleven," she said. "Use of company property. Any member may grant or revoke permission for any person, including any member's spouse, to occupy or use company property, by written notice. Dad wrote that one himself. He always said in-laws are guests."

"Can you type up a notice?"

"Raul," my sister said, "I typed for a living for thirty years. I'll have it done in ten minutes. I'll email it to Ernie. He can print it at the library in Pinetop."

"And the boat?"

There was a pause.

"What about the boat?"

I told her about the listing. Twenty-two-foot Bennington, nineteen-five, cash only, Carmen E.

Graciela didn't laugh this time.

"That boat is in the company's name," she said quietly. "Dad bought it with company money in 2016. If she sells it, that's not a divorce problem. That's theft."

"I know."

"So what do we do?"

I thought about my canal gates. Water goes where the gates send it.

"Ernie's nephew Wilbert has a truck with a hitch," I said. "And Ernie has a barn."

At about three that afternoon, my phone rang. It was Ernie Begay, and he was out of breath.

"Raul? I'm putting you on speaker. I'm standing in your drive. Wilbert's backing up to the boat trailer."

"Is she there?"

"She's on the deck. With the young fella. They came down when they saw Wilbert's truck. She thinks we're the buyers." I heard a door slam somewhere on his end. "Here she comes."

There was a pause.

"I know who you are," Carmen said, and the brightness went right out of her voice. "Kaden told you not to come back here."

"Yes, ma'am, he did. But he's not the one who pays me." I heard paper rustle. "This is for you. It's from the owners of the cabin."

"I'm the owner of the cabin."

"Babe, I'm not gonna fight an old guy over a boat."

"Kaden!"

In the middle of the morning my phone buzzed with an alert from the credit union. A withdrawal of forty-one thousand dollars from our joint savings, by cashier's check, at the branch in Show Low.

I forwarded it to Rosalia without a word. She wrote back in two minutes.

Community money. She can take it, but she has to account for every dollar of it in the divorce. It'll come off her half. Do NOT respond to her.

It's a three-bedroom ranch off Avenue B that we bought in 1999, community property through and through, half hers, and I knew it. Her reading glasses were on the nightstand. The following Saturday, we met on the deck of my father's cabin at ten in the morning.

It was cool up there, in the sixties, the pines smelling like butterscotch the way ponderosas do in the sun. Ernie had swept the deck and set out the old redwood picnic table and five chairs. He stood off to the side by the woodpile in his Forest Service jacket, with his arms folded, the way he must have stood in the fire lookout for thirty years watching the horizon.

Rosalia came up with me and Graciela in my truck. Corbin Ashe drove up from Phoenix in a silver Mercedes with Carmen in the passenger seat. He got out in a blue suit and loafers and stepped carefully across my father's gravel like it might bite him.

Carmen got out after him.

She looked tired. She was wearing jeans and a sweatshirt from the motel gift shop in Show Low that said WHITE MOUNTAINS ARIZONA with an elk on it. She didn't look at me. She looked at the cabin. At the deck where she'd taken that picture with my father's mug.

Then he turned it around so Carmen could see it.

"Carmen," he said. His billboard voice had gone very quiet. "Is this your signature?"

Carmen looked at it. She looked at the heart over the i.

"I don't..." She looked closer. "That was for taxes. Raul's dad said it was for taxes."

"It's notarized," Corbin said. "November 2005. It says you have no interest in the company or the property and that you waive any claim in a divorce."

"I didn't read it."

"Did anybody stop you from reading it?" Rosalia asked.

"No, but..."

"It was one page, Mrs. Escobar."

"I didn't know it would ever matter!"

"Paragraph three," Graciela said. "I typed it. You asked what it was for. Dad told you. You said okay." She tapped the corner of the plastic sleeve. "That's your pie on it. Mom's pumpkin. You had three pieces."

Carmen stared at the orange stain. Then, slowly, she reached into her purse and took out the photograph I'd left on the table at Brownie's. She'd brought it. She'd been carrying it around for two days, trying to figure out why I'd given it to her.

She laid it on the picnic table next to the plastic sleeve.

Thanksgiving, 2005. Her laughing, with her fork. The paper plate with the pumpkin pie. And under the paper plate, the corner of a white page with a notary's stamp on it.

She looked from the photograph to the page and back. Graciela didn't say anything. Neither did I. Up in the pines, a Steller's jay started scolding somebody.

"I was eating pie on it," Carmen said, very quietly. "I was eating pie on it, and laughing."

"You were," Graciela said. "It was a good pie."

Something in her face gave way. Not all at once. Just a little, the way a canal bank goes when the water's been working on it underneath for a long time.

Corbin Ashe sat back from the picnic table.

"Ms. Trevino," he said, "I'll need some time with my client."

"Take all the time you need," Rosalia said. "Ernie has her things boxed up by the door. Clothes, books, the quilt from her mother, the jewelry box and her good knives. He made an inventory. If there's anything else she thinks is hers, she can point to it now."

Ernie stepped forward from the woodpile.

"Ma'am," he said, "I also found your Kindle under the couch, and the charger. And the earrings you left on the bathroom sink. They're in the box on top. I put the earrings in a sandwich bag so they wouldn't get lost."

Carmen looked at him like she was seeing him for the first time. The old man she'd had a twenty-three-year-old order off the property a week ago, who'd packed her earrings in a sandwich bag.

"Thank you, Ernie," she said, barely above a whisper.

Corbin Ashe was writing on a legal pad. He tore the page off, folded it, and put it in his jacket pocket.

"Ms. Trevino," he said, "we'll be amending the petition to remove any claim to the Navajo County property. I'll have it to you by Wednesday."

"Thank you, Mr. Ashe."

"And the forty-one thousand she took out of your joint savings last Thursday," Rosalia added to me, loud enough for everybody, "we'll be addressing in the dissolution. That one's community. She'll get credit for half of it as her own money. The other half comes off her share."

Carmen turned to me then. For the first time since she'd gotten out of the car, she looked right at me.

"Raul," she said. "Raul, please. I made a mistake. Kaden was a mistake. I don't even know what I was thinking. I was turning fifty-two and I was scared and he made me feel young, and I thought if I had the cabin, I'd have something, I'd be somebody." Her voice broke. "Can we just stop? Can we just go back home and talk? Twenty-seven years. Please don't throw it away over a stupid piece of paper."

I looked at the piece of paper. The little heart over the i. The pumpkin stain.

Behind her, across the gravel, I could see my father's coffee mug sitting on the railing of the deck where Ernie had set it, with the Holbrook gas station logo half worn off from forty-five years of dishwashers.

"You sent me a picture of yourself in my father's chair with his mug," I said. "You wrote that the house was yours now."

"I know. I know I did."

"I asked if you were sure."

She closed her eyes.

"You never read a single thing in your life before you signed it, Carmen," I said. "That gate's been shut since Thanksgiving of 2005. You're the one who closed it."
