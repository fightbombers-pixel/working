"I'm at my grandma's funeral! Don't call, I need space to grieve."

I read my fiancée's text out loud on my own back patio in Evansville, Indiana, because fourteen people at my daughter's twentieth birthday cookout had just asked me where Tina was.

There was a beat of quiet, the kind you get at a cookout when somebody says the word funeral. My sister-in-law put her hand on her heart. Somebody said, "Oh, that poor thing."

I had a spatula in one hand and a plate of bratwurst in the other. Two days earlier, on Thursday night, I'd sent Tina eighteen hundred dollars for "funeral flights and flowers," and she'd sent back a little heart and the words "you're my rock."

Forty minutes later, my daughter Brianna put the Coachella livestream on the patio TV, and there was my grieving fiancée, sitting on another man's shoulders in a cowboy hat, two thousand miles from any funeral, screaming the words to a song I'd never heard.

I set the bratwurst on the picnic table. I took my phone out and typed two words.

"My condolences."

Then I copied the livestream link, timestamp and all, and sent it to her mother.

Thirty-five minutes after that, my phone lit up with a photo from her mother, Faye Wheeler: her grandmother, Opal, ninety-two years old and very much alive, holding a fork over a slice of coconut cream pie at her own kitchen table in Henderson, Kentucky.

What nobody at that cookout knew, not even me yet, was that there was a sympathy card sitting on the other end of that same kitchen table, with eleven signatures inside it and six hundred and forty dollars in cash.

My name is Ralph Kendall. I'm 55 years old, and I run warehouse operations for a medical supply distributor on the north side of Evansville. Three shifts, ninety-two people, forty thousand square feet of shelving. If you run a warehouse long enough, you learn one thing. Inventory doesn't lie. People lie. Paperwork lies. But if the count says there's a pallet missing, there's a pallet missing, and sooner or later you find out who took it.

Remember that.

Forty minutes before that, it had been a normal Saturday. Brianna had come home from Purdue for the weekend, and the cookout was her idea. My brother Glen was there with his wife, a couple of my shift supervisors, Brianna's two roommates, and my neighbor Arlo from across the street, who comes to anything that involves free food. Tina was supposed to be there too. She'd been my fiancée for four months. She'd been living in my house for seven.

On Thursday, she'd come home from work at the hospital billing office crying. Her grandmother had died, she said. Very sudden. A stroke. The funeral was family only, in Henderson, tiny, just the immediate family, and her father wanted it that way. She didn't want me to come. She said I'd only met Grandma Opal once, at Easter, and it would be weird.

"I need to do this alone, Ralph," she said, hugging me in the kitchen. "Can you understand that?"

I understood. I'd buried both my parents. I sent her the money for a flight to her aunt's in Nashville afterward, because she said she needed a few days away to grieve. Eighteen hundred dollars. She left Friday morning with a suitcase bigger than she needed for a funeral, and I didn't think a thing about it.

So I read the text out loud at the cookout, and everybody went quiet, and Arlo said he was sorry for my loss, which was nice of him, even though it wasn't exactly my loss.

Brianna was the one who put Coachella on.

She and her roommates had been talking about it all afternoon. It's a big music festival out in the California desert, in a town called Indio, and they stream it live on YouTube, and every college kid in America watches it on the weekend they can't afford to go. Brianna hooked her phone to the patio TV, and we all half watched while I flipped brats.

There was a band on stage. The camera swung out over the crowd, the way they do, all those thousands of people with their hands up.

Brianna screamed.

Not a little scream. A real one, the kind she used to do on roller coasters. She pointed at the TV with her whole arm.

"Dad! Dad, is that Tina?"

I looked up.

The camera had stopped on a woman in a white cowboy hat and a fringed vest, sitting on a man's shoulders right up near the stage. She was waving both arms. She had big sunglasses on, but I knew that smile. I'd bought that smile a six-thousand-two-hundred-dollar ring.

The ring was on her left hand, waving at the camera.

The man under her was big and bald with a gray goatee and a tank top that said Lucas Bail Bonds, Evansville, Indiana, We Get You Out. I knew him too, sort of. Every billboard on the Lloyd Expressway knew him.

Damon Lucas. Her ex.

The camera stayed on them for maybe four seconds. Then it swung away to some other screaming kid.

The whole patio was dead silent. My brother Glen had a beer halfway to his mouth. Arlo put down his potato salad.

"Dad," Brianna said, very quietly. "Isn't she supposed to be at a funeral?"

"That's what she said."

"Was that... was that her grandma under there?"

My brother snorted beer out of his nose. It broke the spell. Somebody laughed. Then everybody laughed, and I laughed too, standing there at my own grill with a spatula, because what else are you going to do?

"Brianna," I said, "can you find that spot on the stream? The time it was on?"

She already had. She'd scrolled the livestream back and found the exact moment. Three hours and twelve minutes in. You could pause it right on Tina's face.

That's when I typed "My condolences" and sent it to my fiancée.

Then I scrolled down to the F's in my contacts. Faye Wheeler. Tina's mother. She'd put her number in my phone herself at Easter, so I could text her pictures of the ham I smoked for the whole family.

I sent her the link, with the timestamp. I didn't write anything else.

"Dad," Brianna said, reading over my shoulder. "Are you sure?"

"I'm sure."

"What if her grandma really did die?"

"Then her mother's going to be very confused," I said. "And I'll apologize."

It took thirty-five minutes. Every person on that patio was watching my phone like it was a Colts game in the fourth quarter. When it buzzed, Arlo actually stood up.

It was the photo. Grandma Opal at her kitchen table in her blue cardigan, holding a fork over a piece of coconut cream pie, looking straight at the camera with an expression I can only describe as unimpressed.

Underneath it, Faye had typed, "Mother says she feels fine. Ed and I are having lunch with her. What is this, Ralph?"

I wrote back, "I think you'd better ask Tina. She told me Thursday that Opal passed away. She said the funeral was family only and you wanted it that way. I sent her money to fly to Nashville after."

Three dots. They went away. They came back. They went away.

Then, "We are coming to your house when she gets home. Ed is very upset. When does she land?"

"Monday night," I wrote. "Eight-forty. She asked me to pick her up."

"Don't," Faye wrote. "We'll be there."

I put the phone face down on the picnic table and looked at fourteen people staring at me.

"Well," I said. "Who wants another brat?"

Nobody did. But my brother Glen came over and put his arm around my shoulders, and Brianna's roommates started cleaning up without anybody asking, and Arlo took two more brats home anyway.

It was almost dark when my phone buzzed again. Not Faye. Not Tina.

It was a number I didn't know, with a Henderson area code.

"Ralph, this is Darcy, Tina's sister. Mom gave me your number. She just called me screaming. I need to talk to you before Monday. There's stuff you don't know, and I'm done covering for her."

Darcy Wheeler met me Sunday morning at the Waffle House on Green River Road, because she said she couldn't do it on the phone and she couldn't do it anywhere her mother might drive past.

She was 44, four years younger than Tina, and she looked like her sister if her sister had worked night shifts as a respiratory therapist for twenty years, which Darcy had. She ordered coffee and didn't touch it. I ordered hash browns, scattered and smothered, because it was Sunday and my life was falling apart and I wasn't going to let it fall apart hungry.

"Damon's not her ex," Darcy said, before the waitress was even gone. "I mean, he is. They dated for six years. But he's not ex anymore. Not since Christmas."

"Christmas."

"The hospital party at the Tropicana. You had the flu, remember? She went alone. Damon was there with some nurse he's dating, and by the end of the night he wasn't with the nurse anymore."

I remembered the flu. I remembered Tina coming home at two in the morning smelling like cigarettes, saying the party ran late. I'd been too sick to care.

"And you knew," I said.

Darcy looked at her coffee. "I've known since January. She told me. She tells me everything, Ralph, that's the problem. She uses me like a diary that can't say no."

"So the girls' nights."

"Some of them were real. Most of them weren't. If you ever called me looking for her, I was supposed to say she was in the bathroom." She swallowed. "You never called. Not once. That's what made me feel worst. You trusted her so much you never even checked."

That stung. It stung because it was true. I run a warehouse where I count every pallet twice, and I'd never once counted my own fiancée.

"Why'd she say yes?" I said. "When I proposed. If she was already with him."

Darcy let out a long breath.

"Because Damon won't marry her. He never would. Six years, he never would. And you've got a paid-off house, a pension, a good job, and you're nice." She said "nice" the way you'd say "beige." "She called you her retirement plan. Not to your face. To me."

The waitress put my hash browns down. I looked at them for a while.

There's a funny thing that happens when you finally find out the truth about something. All the little moments you didn't count come back, one at a time, and they line up on the shelf.

Like the time Tina introduced me to her coworkers at the billing office as "my fiancé, he runs a warehouse," and one of them said, "Oh, like a forklift?" and Tina laughed and said, "Pretty much."

Like the ring. She'd wanted a bigger one. She'd said so at the jewelry counter, in front of the salesman. "I mean, I'm fifty soon, Ralph. This is probably my last ring. Don't be cheap about it."

Like Brianna's Purdue orientation last summer, when Tina sat in the car the whole time on her phone because, she said, campuses made her feel old.

Like her birthday, when I took her to the steakhouse downtown and she spent the whole dinner checking her phone and then said, "Damon used to take me to Louisville for my birthday. Now that was a night."

I'd laughed. Everybody always laughs.

"Did she ask you to cover the funeral?" I said.

Darcy nodded. She didn't look up.

"Thursday. She called me from the parking garage at work. She said, 'If Ralph calls you, tell him the service was beautiful and I'm too upset to talk. And don't tell Mom. Mom won't find out. Grandma doesn't even use a phone.'"

"Grandma doesn't need a phone," I said. "Her daughter-in-law does."

Darcy laughed then, a little hiccup of a laugh, and covered her mouth.

"Ralph, when Mom called me yesterday, she was screaming so loud I had to hold the phone away. And then Grandma got on. You know what Grandma said?"

"What?"

"She said, 'Tell that girl I'm not dead yet, but if she comes near this house, she's going to wish she was.'"

I laughed so hard the cook looked out of the kitchen. It was the first real laugh I'd had since the cookout. It felt good. It felt like taking a heavy box off a top shelf.

"What are you going to do?" Darcy said.

"Her bags are going on the porch tomorrow," I said. "Your parents are coming. That's all I've got planned."

"That's all?"

"That's plenty."

My phone buzzed on the table between us. We both looked at it.

It was Tina. First word from her since Friday.

"Sorry, signal's terrible here. The service was beautiful. Grandma would have loved the flowers. Pick me up Monday? Love you."

Darcy read it upside down. She put both hands flat on the table.

"She doesn't know," she said. "Ralph. She hasn't checked. She doesn't know Mom saw it."

"She hasn't checked anything," I said. "She's been sitting on a man's shoulders for two days."

I didn't answer the text. I didn't need to.

That afternoon I drove Brianna back up to West Lafayette, two and a half hours on I-69 with her feet on my dashboard, the way she's ridden since she was six. Somewhere past Bloomington she turned the radio down.

"Dad. Can I say something and you won't get mad?"

"I never get mad."

"That's kind of the thing." She picked at her shoelace. "I never liked her. Not because of you. Because of how she talked to you. Like you were the help."

"You never said anything."

"You were happy. Or I thought you were." She looked out the window. "Mom used to say you'd forgive a tornado if it said sorry."

"Your mother says a lot of things."

"She's not wrong about that one, though."

I drove for a while. A semi passed us, and I counted the pallets I could see through the gap in its curtain, out of habit.

"I'm not going to forgive this one, Bri."

"Promise?"

"Promise."

She held out her pinky, like she was eight. I hooked it with mine over the gearshift. Then she turned the radio back up and sang along to something terrible the whole rest of the way.

Monday night at eight-forty, Tina's flight landed in Evansville. I wasn't there.

At nine-thirty-five, an Uber pulled up in front of my house, and Tina got out with her big suitcase and a sunburn on her shoulders that went right up to where her tank top straps had been.

She stopped at the end of the walk.

Her other bags were on the porch. Four of them, the matching set she'd bought when she moved in, plus three cardboard boxes I'd packed and labeled in black marker, the way we label pallets at work. "Tina, Clothes." "Tina, Shoes." "Tina, Bathroom." Her pillow was on top in a garbage bag, because she was particular about her pillow and I didn't want it to get dirty.

And on the porch swing, side by side, sat Ed and Faye Wheeler.

I was standing in the doorway with the screen door shut. I didn't say anything. I'd decided ahead of time that I'd let her talk first.

Tina looked at the bags. She looked at her parents. She looked at me. You could watch her brain trying to put the boxes in order.

"Mom? Dad? What are you... what's going on?"

Ed Wheeler stood up. Ed is 74, a retired lineman for the electric company, the kind of man who still irons his jeans. He had his hands in his pockets.

"How was the funeral, Tina?" he said.

She opened her mouth. Shut it.

"It was... Dad, I don't know what Ralph told you—"

"Ralph didn't tell us anything," Faye said. She had her purse in her lap with both hands on it, like she was holding it shut. "Ralph sent us a link. With a time on it."

"What link?"

"You know what link," Ed said. "Three hours and twelve minutes. Your mother watched it eleven times. Then she showed it to your grandmother."

Tina turned to me. "Ralph. Baby. You need to let me explain."

"Go ahead," I said.

"It's not what it looks like."

"It looks like Coachella," I said.

"I went to Grandma's funeral... I mean, I meant to, I was going to... I was so upset, Ralph, I needed to get away, and Damon had an extra ticket, and he's an old friend, and it was just friends—"

"Who died, Tina?" Ed said.

"What?"

"You told your fiancé your grandmother died. My mother. Who died?"

"Nobody, Dad, I just... it was a figure of speech, I needed time off and I couldn't—"

"A figure of speech," Faye said. Her voice wobbled. "You buried your grandmother as a figure of speech."

That was round one, the denial, and it lasted about three minutes before she ran out of road. I watched her face change. Round two always comes fast with Tina. She pulls her shoulders back and her chin comes up, and she stops being the one who did something and starts being the one it was done to.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. You know what? Fine. I went to Coachella. I'm a grown woman. I'm allowed to go to a concert. And you"—she pointed at me—"you sent that to my mother? You went behind my back to my parents? Do you know how humiliating that is? Do you know what kind of man does that?"

"The kind who sent you eighteen hundred dollars for funeral flights," I said.

"I'll pay you back!"

"Out of what?"

"Out of... I'll pay you back, Ralph, God, it's just money, you're always so obsessed with money—"

"You picked the number," I said. "I just sent it."

"And you put my stuff on the porch? Like trash? In front of the whole street?"

Across the street, Arlo's porch light was on, and Arlo was very definitely sitting on his porch in a lawn chair, in the dark, eating something out of a bowl.

"I folded everything," I said. "Your pillow's in a bag so it doesn't get dirty."

That did something to her. I don't know why it was the pillow, but her eyes filled up and her voice dropped.

"Ralph. Please. I'm sorry. I was confused. I've been so stressed about the wedding and work and money, and Damon just... he showed up, and he was fun, and it was one weekend. One stupid weekend. I swear to God it was just one weekend."

"Since Christmas," I said.

She stopped breathing for a second.

"What?"

"The Tropicana. I had the flu."

She looked at her mother. Her mother looked at her purse. Tina's face did a quick calculation, and I watched her arrive at the answer.

"Darcy," she said. "Darcy told you. That little traitor. I'm going to kill her."

"You already killed your grandmother this week," Ed said. "Give it a rest."

There was a long silence on the porch. Somewhere down the block, a dog barked twice and quit.

That was round three. She'd run out of lies and there was nothing left in the bag. She stood there in her sunburn with her suitcase handle in her hand, and she looked at the ring on her finger like she'd forgotten it was there.

"So that's it," she said to me. "You're just done. After seven months."

"You told me you needed space to grieve," I said. "I'm giving you space."

"Where am I supposed to go?"

"You're coming home with us," Faye said, standing up. "Tonight. Your father has the truck."

Ed was already picking up two of the boxes. He carried them down the porch steps to his pickup without looking at his daughter, and then came back for two more. I helped him. Neither of us said a word.

Tina stood in the yard the whole time, holding her suitcase, watching her life get loaded into the back of a Ford F-150.

When the last box was in, Ed walked back up onto the porch, close to me, and spoke low, so his wife and daughter couldn't hear.

"Ralph," he said. "That eighteen hundred dollars. Did you send it through that phone app?"

"Venmo. Thursday night."

"What did she tell you it was for?"

"Funeral flights. And flowers."

Ed nodded slowly. He looked out at his daughter in the yard.

"She called me Thursday too," he said. "Before she left. She asked me for two thousand. Said it was for your wedding photographer."

I stood on my porch and watched Ed's taillights go down the street, and I did the math. Eighteen hundred from me. Two thousand from her father. Three thousand eight hundred dollars for one weekend in the desert on a bail bondsman's shoulders.

Inventory doesn't lie.

Arlo waved at me from across the street with his spoon. I waved back and went inside and locked the door.

Tuesday morning at six-fifteen, before I'd even finished my first cup of coffee, Ed Wheeler's pickup was back in my driveway.

He didn't come to the door. He just sat in the truck with the engine running until I came out in my socks and knocked on his window. He rolled it down. He had a checkbook open on the steering wheel and a pen in his hand, and his eyes were red around the edges like he hadn't slept.

"Ralph," he said. "I'm writing you a check for eighteen hundred dollars."

"No, you're not."

"She took it under my family's name. She said my mother was dead to get it. That's on the Wheelers." He started writing. "I'm not going to have a man in this town say Ed Wheeler's daughter robbed him and Ed Wheeler sat there."

I put my hand on the pen. Gently.

"Ed. You didn't take my money. She did."

"She's my kid."

"She's forty-eight years old," I said. "If you pay me back, she learns her daddy cleans up after her. Then next time it's somebody else's eighteen hundred, and you're seventy-six, writing another check in somebody else's driveway."

He looked at me for a long time. Then he looked down at the checkbook.

"She took two thousand from me," he said. "For your wedding photographer. You know what I was going to do with that two thousand? I was going to buy my mother a new hearing aid. Her old one whistles."

"Then make Tina buy the hearing aid," I said.

Ed laughed, a short, tired laugh, and closed the checkbook.

"You know," he said, "when she brought you to Easter, I told Faye in the car, that one's too good for her. Faye told me to hush." He rubbed his face. "I should've said it louder."

"Would she have listened?"

"Not one word." He put the truck in reverse. Then he stopped. "Ralph. She's going to call you. She's going to call you a lot. She did it to Damon the first time they broke up. Ninety calls in a day. He changed his number."

"I'm not changing my number," I said. "I'll just stop picking up."

He nodded once, like a man agreeing to a bid, and backed out of my driveway.

Wednesday afternoon, a little after three, my dock supervisor called me on the radio.

"Ralph, there's a guy out here at door nine asking for you by name. Big bald guy. Says he's not a driver."

I knew who it was before I got there.

Damon Lucas was standing on the loading dock in a golf shirt that said Lucas Bail Bonds, with his sunburn peeling off the top of his head. He had his hands up a little when he saw me, palms out, like a man showing a cop he isn't armed.

"Ralph Kendall," he said. "Tina talked about you a lot. Said you ran the warehouse on Kratzville Road. I figured you'd be here."

"I'm here," I said. "You want to step off the dock? There's a forklift coming through."

He stepped off the dock. We stood by the dumpsters, which seemed about right.

"I'm not here to start anything," Damon said. "In my business, I deal with a lot of guys who are mad at me. I know what it looks like. You don't look mad."

"I'm not."

"That's what's bugging me." He rubbed his head and winced when he touched the sunburn. "Look, man. I didn't know."

"Didn't know what?"

"That you two were engaged. That she lived with you." He held up his hand. "I swear to God. She told me you were her roommate. Said she rented a room from a divorced guy to save money while she looked for a condo. She said the ring was her mother's. Said she wore it so guys at the bars would leave her alone."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. Damon looked at me like I'd hit him.

"I'm sorry," I said. "It's just, she told me you were an old friend with an extra ticket."

"An extra ticket." He laughed too, but it came out sour. "I paid for both tickets. Eleven hundred apiece. She said she'd pay me back when her tax refund came in."

"Did her tax refund come in?"

"Not yet."

"It's not coming," I said. "She paid for the flight with my money and her father's money. Your ticket's just gone."

Damon was quiet for a while. A truck backed into door seven behind us, beeping.

"When'd you find out?" he said.

"Saturday. On the livestream. You were wearing your shirt."

He closed his eyes. "My shirt. Oh, man. My shirt. I've had six calls this week from people asking if that was me at Coachella with my 'grieving girlfriend.' Somebody at the courthouse clipped it. It's on TikTok. My sister sent it to me with like fifty laughing faces."

"Is she your girlfriend?" I said.

"Not anymore." He said it flat. "I'm fifty years old, man. I bail people out for lying for a living. I don't need it at home. I told her Monday, after she called me from her parents' house. I told her I'm done."

"What did she say?"

"She said you'd take her back." He looked at me. "Are you going to?"

"No."

He nodded like he respected that. He stuck out his hand. I looked at it for a second. Then I shook it, because he'd driven across town to tell me the truth, and that's more than she ever did.

"For what it's worth," Damon said, "you dodged a bullet."

"I know," I said. "I've been dodging it since Christmas. I just didn't know it was flying."

He walked back to his car. Halfway there my phone buzzed in my pocket. It was a number I didn't know, with the hospital's exchange.

"Mr. Kendall? This is Gwen Talley. I'm the director of patient billing at Deaconess. You're listed as Tina Wheeler's emergency contact. Do you have a few minutes to talk?"

I didn't have to agree to that call. I want to be clear about that. I could've said I wasn't comfortable and hung up, and nobody would've blamed me. I'd never once called Tina's boss, and I didn't intend to start. But she called me, and she asked, and I'm not going to lie to a woman doing her job.

"Mr. Kendall," Gwen said, "I'll be direct. Ms. Wheeler took three days of bereavement leave last week for the death of her grandmother. There's a video going around the hospital that suggests she was in California. I'm not asking you to say anything about your relationship. I just need to know one thing. Did her grandmother pass away?"

"No, ma'am," I said. "I saw a picture of her eating coconut cream pie on Saturday afternoon."

There was a short silence.

"Thank you, Mr. Kendall. That's all I needed."

"Ms. Talley," I said. "For the record, I didn't send that video to anybody at the hospital. I sent it to her mother."

"I know," she said. "Our receptionist's grandson sent it to her. Half the hospital has seen it. Thank you for your time."

She hung up. I stood by the dumpsters for a minute.

That's where Tina's ladder started climbing, and I counted every rung.

Wednesday night: twenty-three missed calls.

Thursday morning, a text. "Gwen suspended me. Without pay. Two weeks. Because of YOU."

Thursday afternoon, thirty-one missed calls and a voicemail that was just her crying for forty seconds.

Thursday night, Faye called me, and I answered, because Faye hadn't done anything wrong.

"Ralph, I'm sorry to bother you," Faye said. "She's not eating. She's just lying in Darcy's old room crying. She keeps saying you'll come around. I told her you won't. She doesn't believe me."

"She's going to have to, Faye."

"I know." Faye sighed. "I just wanted you to know I told her."

Friday at lunch, Tina called from her father's landline, which is how I knew it was her, because nobody else in America still calls from a landline.

I picked up. I'd decided I'd answer once.

"Ralph." Her voice was hoarse. "Please. I need you to do one thing for me. Just one thing, and I'll never ask you for anything again."

"What's that?"

"Call Gwen. Tell her you got confused. Tell her my grandma had a health scare, and I panicked, and I got my words mixed up. She'll believe you. You're my emergency contact. She trusts you."

"No."

"Ralph, I could lose my job. Twenty-two years at that hospital. My pension—"

"No."

"Why not? It's one phone call! You owe me that! You're the one who sent the video to my parents!"

"I sent it to your mother," I said. "Your grandmother's alive. That's three facts. I'm not going to change any of them."

"So you're just going to let me lose everything."

"I'm not letting you do anything, Tina. You bought the ticket."

She hung up. That was the last time she called for nine days.

On the tenth day, a card came in the mail at my house. Not from Tina. The envelope was addressed in shaky blue cursive, and the return address said Opal Wheeler, Henderson, Kentucky.

Inside was a birthday party invitation. A plain one from the dollar store, with balloons on it. In the blank space somebody had written, "Opal is turning 92! Saturday at 2 o'clock. My house. Bring a lawn chair."

Under that, in the same shaky cursive, it said, "Ralph, you come. I have something of Tina's you ought to see. And I want to meet the man who found out I was dead before I did."

Henderson, Kentucky, is just across the Ohio River from Evansville, about a fifteen-minute drive over the twin bridges. Opal Wheeler has lived in the same white frame house on Clay Street since 1961. She buried a husband out of that house and raised three boys in it, and she still mows her own front yard with a push mower, which Ed has been trying to stop for eleven years.

I got there at ten till two with a lawn chair and a coconut cream pie from the bakery, because I figured she'd appreciate the joke.

There were about thirty people in the backyard. Wheeler cousins, Wheeler grandkids, a couple of old ladies from Opal's church, Darcy in a sundress, Ed working the grill like he was mad at it, Faye setting out potato salad. Somebody had hung a banner over the back door that said HAPPY 92ND OPAL in glitter letters. Underneath that, somebody else, probably a teenage grandson, had taped a piece of printer paper that said STILL ALIVE.

Opal was in a lawn chair in the shade in a blue cardigan, same as the picture. She took one look at my pie and cackled like a hen.

"Coconut cream," she said. "Oh, I like you. Sit down."

I sat down next to her.

"You're the warehouse man," she said.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Ed says you count things."

"That's most of the job."

"Faye showed me that video on her phone," Opal said. "Eleven times, she says. I only watched it twice. Once to see Tina, and once to see that big bald man's shirt. 'We Get You Out.'" She snorted. "Well, he didn't get her out of anything, did he?"

"No, ma'am. He got her into Coachella."

"I don't even know what a Coachella is. Ed says it's a hootenanny for rich children."

"That's about right."

"Good." She patted my hand. "Then you'll appreciate this."

Before she could say what, the side gate squeaked open, and the whole backyard went quiet.

Tina came through the gate in a yellow sundress with a gift bag in her hand. Later I found out it was a new hearing aid, the good kind, paid for on a credit card in her own name. Ed had told her the same thing I told him. Her sunburn had peeled and faded. She'd done her hair. She was still wearing the ring.

She saw me sitting next to her grandmother, and she stopped.

Then she came straight across the yard, past her father at the grill, past Darcy, past the old ladies from the church, and stopped right in front of my lawn chair.

"Ralph," she said. "Can I talk to you? Please. Just for a minute."

Thirty people pretended not to listen. None of them were very good at it.

"You can talk," I said.

She knelt down on the grass in front of my chair. In a yellow sundress, in her grandmother's backyard, in front of her whole family.

"I'm so sorry," she said. "I'm sorry for everything. I lied to you, and I lied to my parents, and I lied to my job, and I did the worst thing a person could do to her own grandmother. I know that. I've been going to counseling. Twice a week. I haven't talked to Damon in two weeks. I'm going to pay you back every penny, I swear. I'll pay Dad back. I just want to come home. I want to marry you. I'll do anything. Please, Ralph. Please."

I didn't answer. I looked at Opal.

Opal reached down beside her lawn chair and picked up a square white envelope from her pocketbook. It had been opened along the top. She held it out to me, not to Tina.

"This came to my house the Tuesday after Tina's trip," she said, loud enough for the whole yard. "It's a sympathy card. From her office. Eleven people signed it. Six hundred and forty dollars inside. In cash."

Tina's head came up.

"Grandma—"

"Hush." Opal didn't even look at her. "They mailed it to 'The family of Opal Wheeler.' First sympathy card I ever got for myself. I've been saving it." She tapped the envelope. "But that's not why I wanted you to see it, Ralph. Read the note at the bottom."

I opened the card. It had lilies on the front. Eleven names inside, with little messages. "Thinking of you." "So sorry for your loss." And at the bottom, in neat handwriting, a note signed by somebody named Bonita.

"Tina, we know you asked us to send the collection straight to your grandma's house so your fiancé wouldn't see it and get upset about money. We hope this helps with the funeral costs. Hugs, Bonita and the whole billing team."

I read it twice, the way I read a packing slip. Then I looked down at Tina, kneeling on the grass.

"You told them to send it here," I said. "So I wouldn't see it."

"Ralph—"

"And then you called your grandmother," Opal said, "on the Thursday before you left, and asked me if anybody from your office called, would I please tell them I was too sad to talk. I thought you meant you were sad. I said yes, honey, of course." She shook her head slowly. "Then you called me again the Tuesday you got back and asked me to put any mail that came for you in an envelope and drop it at your mother's. I didn't. Because I'm ninety-two, Tina. I'm old. I'm not stupid."

You could hear the hamburgers sizzling on Ed's grill. Nobody else in that backyard made a sound.

Tina looked up at me, her eyes wet, the ring on her hand shaking.

"Please," she said.

I closed the card. I put it back in its envelope. Then I held it out to her, and she took it, because she didn't know what else to do.

She held it in both hands, the way you hold a bill you can't pay.

"And the ring?" she said, very small. "Do you want the ring back?"

Ed had stopped flipping burgers. Darcy had her hand over her mouth. Opal was watching me with her chin up, like she was waiting to see if I'd pass a test.

"Keep it," I said. "Sell it if you need to. You've got some people to pay back."

"Ralph, please, I'm on my knees—"

"Eleven people at your office paid for a funeral that never happened," I said. "Monday morning, you walk into that billing office, and you give every one of them their money back. In person. Out of your own pocket. Starting with Bonita."
