"I can't believe I have to sleep with him just to keep getting his money."

Two women in the room, one camera on the wall above the coffee maker, and one husband watching a 38-second clip that the doorbell app sends you when it hears voices.

I was parked next to a fresh strip of blacktop on Southwest 29th Street with a cold McDonald's coffee in the cup holder. I'd been married to Kim for 27 years. That very morning I'd wired $11,000 to the Elks Lodge for her parents' 50th anniversary party, because she said it would mean the world to her.

"Helen. Watch this. Call me."

My name is Virgil Haynes. I'm 57, and I own Haynes Paving in Topeka, Kansas, twelve trucks and twenty-two guys, mostly parking lots and county roads. Thirty-five years of asphalt taught me one thing. Helen's office is on the second floor above a vacuum repair shop on Kansas Avenue. I brought two glazed doughnuts and the folder. "Now her lawyer read the prenup," I said. "And you know what it says, because her lawyer has read it by now, and so has she. Proven adultery. No maintenance. No share of the company. She leaves with what she brought in, which in 1998 was a Ford Escort and a makeup kit."

Brady put the sunglasses back on. His hand was shaking.

"I didn't make up any accounts," he said. "I just told her what a guy at the club told me. That paving guys always keep cash on the side."

"Well, this one doesn't. Tell her that. Or better yet, tell the judge. Thursday, nine a.m."

"I'm not going to any court."

"Then don't. But Brady, you should know. Helen's going to ask her under oath who told her about the accounts. And Kim's not the type to go down alone. She keeps score."

He looked at me for a long time. Then he reached under the counter, pulled out his phone, and walked into the back room. I heard him through the door, because he's the kind of guy who talks loud on the phone.

"Kim. It's me. No, listen. Listen to me. I can't do this. I'm out. I'm not going to court for you. No, I'm not... Because there's no money, Kim! There's no money!"

I walked out to the parking lot. Before I even got to the truck, my phone buzzed. Kim. Then again. Then a text, all capital letters.

"WHAT DID YOU SAY TO HIM."

I didn't answer that one either.

Thursday morning in Courtroom 4 of the Shawnee County Courthouse was colder than a meat locker. Judge Theresa Alcott was in her sixties with short silver hair and reading glasses on a chain, and she had our whole file stacked in front of her with yellow sticky notes poking out of it like feathers.

Kim sat at the other table with Stanton Pike, who looked exactly like his billboard, only more tired. Kim wore a navy suit and no jewelry. Not even her ring. She didn't look at me once.

Trish sat in the second row behind me. Lloyd and Bev sat in the back row, together, holding hands, not on anybody's side.

"Mr. Pike," the judge said. "Your motion to suppress the recording. Go ahead."

Pike stood up. "Your Honor, the petitioner secretly recorded my client in her own home, in a private conversation with a friend, without her knowledge or consent."

"Without her knowledge," Judge Alcott said. She held up a sheet of paper. "This is the account information from the camera company. The account holder is your client. The device was purchased with your client's credit card. The device was installed by your client. And according to this activity log, your client watched this exact recording on her own phone two hours after it was made, and then deleted it." She looked over her glasses at Kim. "Is that right, Mrs. Haynes?"

Kim looked at Pike. Pike looked at his legal pad.

"I... I didn't know it recorded sound."

"The box says it records sound. In red letters. Your attorney attached a photo of the box to his own motion." The judge set the paper down. "Motion denied. Next. Your motion for temporary maintenance and exclusive possession of the marital home, based on a history of explosive anger and verbal abuse."

Pike stood again. "Your Honor, my client is in fear—"

"Let me read something into the record, Mr. Pike, and then you can tell me about fear." Judge Alcott picked up a transcript. Helen had attached it, every word of the clip, typed out. The judge read it slowly, in a flat, clear voice, the way you'd read the weather.

"'I can't believe I have to sleep with him just to keep getting his money. Once a week, lights off, I think about Brady. And then Friday the card works. That's the deal.'"

The room was silent. Somebody's phone buzzed in the gallery and they fumbled to turn it off.

"Mrs. Haynes," the judge said. "That doesn't sound like a woman in fear. That sounds like a woman with a business arrangement."

"That's taken out of context."

"Then give me the context."

Kim opened her mouth. Nothing came out.

"Ms. Kincaid," the judge said. "Your exhibit fourteen."

Helen stood up. "Exhibit fourteen is a series of Venmo payments, Your Honor, from the account of Kimberly Haynes, paid from a credit card belonging to Haynes Paving, to a Mr. Brady Sexton. Golf. Rent lol. Our cabin fund. Thirty-one payments since March, totaling $13,850. And exhibit fifteen is a signed statement from Patricia Kellogg, who was present for the recorded conversation and confirms the affair."

Kim turned around in her chair and looked at Trish. It was the first time she'd looked at anyone on our side of the room.

"Trish. You?"

"You called me pathetic, Kimmy," Trish said, quietly. "On my own phone. To him."

The judge tapped her pen.

"Mr. Pike. There's also the matter of the prenuptial agreement, signed in June 1998. Have you read paragraph eleven?"

Pike was quiet a moment. "I have, Your Honor."

"And?"

"We'll be challenging its enforceability at trial."

"You're welcome to try. I'll tell you now, it was signed with independent counsel, it was notarized, and it was signed eleven days before the wedding, not on the courthouse steps. I don't see much daylight there." The judge took off her glasses. "Motion for temporary maintenance and exclusive possession is denied. The petitioner will have possession of the marital residence, which according to these records was purchased by him in 1994, four years before the marriage. Respondent will have thirty days to remove her personal belongings. The parties will not dissipate any marital or business funds pending trial. And Mrs. Haynes, I'd encourage you to have a long talk with your lawyer about the word 'proven.'"

She banged the gavel. It sounded like a door closing.

In the hallway, Kim caught up to me by the elevators. Pike was trying to hold her elbow and she shook him off.

"Virgil. Virgil, please. Just let me come to the house. Let me get my things and talk to you. Just once. Just you and me. Please."

I pushed the elevator button.

"Tomorrow at ten," I said. "Bring boxes."

The next morning at ten sharp, Kim pulled into the driveway in her white SUV, the one with the payments I'd made every month for three years. She got out with a stack of flat U-Haul boxes under her arm and walked to the front door like she was walking into a hospital.

I let her in. She stopped in the kitchen doorway.

The coffee maker. The John Deere calendar. Our son's magnet from the third grade. And up in the corner over the coffee pot, the little black camera with its little blue light.

"You kept it up," she said.

"It's yours. You can take it with you. It's on your account."

She dropped the boxes on the floor. She leaned against the island, right where she'd been standing with the pinot grigio that Tuesday afternoon.

"Virgil, I made a mistake. One stupid mistake. Twenty-seven years, and one stupid afternoon with a bottle of wine."

"Thirty-one Venmo payments."

"Brady's gone. He changed his number. He blocked me on everything. It's over. Isn't that what you wanted?"

"I wanted a wife who didn't need the lights off."

She flinched like I'd slapped her. Her eyes filled up.

"I'll do counseling. I'll do anything. I'll sign anything. I'll sign the cabin over to you, I'll sign the business, whatever you want."

"You can't sign the cabin over to me, Kim. It was always mine. That's what you were trying to fix."

"Please. Where am I supposed to go? Mom and Dad said I can stay in my old room. My old room, Virgil. I'm 52 years old. There's still a Bon Jovi poster on the wall."

"Lloyd and Bev are good people."

"Is that all you're going to say?"

I picked up a roll of packing tape off the counter and handed it to her.

"Your closet's upstairs. Your makeup's in the bathroom. The good towels are your mother's, so take those too."

She took the tape. She stood there holding it, looking at me, and I think she was finally, really seeing me. Not the card. Not the guy in the gray suit. Just me.

"Do you hate me?"

"No."

"Do you love me?"

I didn't answer. I reached up over the coffee maker, unplugged the little black camera, wrapped the cord around it, and set it on top of her stack of empty boxes.

"Don't forget this," I said. "It's the only thing in this house that ever told me the truth."
