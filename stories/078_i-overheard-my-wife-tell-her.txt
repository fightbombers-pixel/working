"Ned will make the perfect ex-husband, Mom. He's too nice to fight for anything in court."

My wife said it in her mother's kitchen in Fargo on a Friday night in January, to her mother and two of her aunts, while I was standing in the back doorway with a nine-by-thirteen pan of tater tot hotdish in my oven mitts. Four people heard it. One of them laughed.

I'd been married to Carmela for twenty-nine years. I had just driven eleven miles on a snow-packed I-29 to bring that hotdish to her mother's freezer, because her mother had a hip replacement in December and couldn't stand at a stove. It was nine below outside. The pan was still warm.

"He'll give me the house," Carmela went on, with her back to me. "He'll give me half the pension and thank me for the casserole dish. You watch. Ned doesn't fight. Ned fixes things."

Her Aunt Peg was the one who saw me first. Her mouth made a little O.

I walked in, set the hotdish down on the counter right in the middle of the four of them, and took off my oven mitts.

"Then you'd better keep the dish," I said.

Carmela turned around so fast she knocked a coffee cup off the counter. It didn't break. It just rolled under the table, and nobody went after it.

Nine days later, at her mother's seventy-sixth birthday dinner, a woman in a Vikings jacket would walk into that same dining room past fourteen people and a sheet cake, and my wife would be holding a white envelope with both hands, and her hands would be shaking so hard the paper rattled.

What she didn't know, standing there in her mother's kitchen with her face going red, was that her younger sister Lavonne had been in the pantry the whole time, looking for the good paper napkins, and had heard every word through the slatted door.

My name is Ned Kenley. I'm fifty-seven years old, and for thirty-two years I've been a signal maintainer for the railroad out of Fargo, North Dakota. I keep the crossing gates and the trackside signals working on about ninety miles of line, in weather that would make you cry. The one thing that job teaches you is this: a signal that fails, fails safe. When something goes wrong, the light goes red and the gate comes down, and nothing moves until somebody fixes it. Nobody argues with a red signal. Remember that.

"Ned," Carmela said. "Honey. That's not… you're taking that out of context."

"What's the context?"

"I was joking. Mom and I were just talking. You know how we talk."

Her mother, Bev Harding, sat at the kitchen table with her walker parked beside her and looked at her hands. Aunt Peg looked at the ceiling. Aunt Doris, who'd laughed, had stopped laughing.

"Okay," I said.

"Okay what?"

"Okay, you were joking." I picked my oven mitts up off the counter. "Bev, the hotdish goes at three-fifty for about forty minutes if you take it out frozen. The tots on top get crispy if you leave the foil off for the last ten."

"Thank you, Ned," Bev said, very quietly.

"Where are you going?" Carmela said.

"Home."

"I'm staying here tonight. Mom needs help with her shower in the morning."

"Then I'll see you when I see you," I said.

I walked out the back door, down the four wooden steps I'd rebuilt for Bev two summers ago, and across the yard in the blue dark to my truck. The engine was still warm. The thermometer on the dash said minus nine. I sat there with my hands on the wheel, not starting it, and I thought about twenty-nine years and a casserole dish.

Somebody knocked on my window.

It was Lavonne. She'd come out the side door without a coat, in a sweatshirt that said Moorhead Spuds and her slippers, and she was hugging herself and stamping her feet in the snow.

I rolled the window down.

"Lavonne, get in, you'll freeze."

She came around and climbed into the passenger seat and put her hands in front of the heater vent while I started the engine.

"I heard it," she said. "I was in the pantry."

"I figured somebody was."

"Ned." She looked at me. Lavonne is fifty, three years younger than Carmela, and the two of them have been fighting about something since the Carter administration. "It's not the first time she's said it."

"Said what?"

"The perfect ex-husband thing. She said it at Thanksgiving. She said it at my house in October when she'd had two glasses of wine. She's been saying it since summer." Lavonne took a breath. "It's not a joke. It's a plan. And there's somebody."

The heater ticked. Outside, snow was blowing sideways under the yard light.

"Who?" I said.

"Not here," Lavonne said. "Mom's going to come to the window in about thirty seconds. Meet me tomorrow. The Grand Diner on Main, nine o'clock. I'll tell you everything." She opened the door and let in a gust of cold. "And Ned? Don't tell her I talked to you. Not yet. I need you to hear the whole thing first, because she's not planning to tell you. She's planning to tell everybody else."

She shut the door and ran back across the yard. In the kitchen window, I saw the curtain move, and Carmela's face looking out, and I saw her see her sister coming back from my truck.

I put it in gear and drove home.

The Grand Diner on Main Avenue has been there since before I was born. It's got a counter with spinning stools, a pie case, and a waitress named Hazel who's been calling me "hon" since I was a signal helper in 1994. At nine o'clock on Saturday morning, Lavonne was already in the back booth with two coffees and her winter coat still on.

"She saw me come back from your truck," Lavonne said before I even sat down. "She texted me eleven times last night. I told her I was asking you to drive me to Target."

"Did she believe you?"

"Carmela only believes things that are about her."

Hazel came by. I ordered eggs. Lavonne ordered toast she didn't eat.

"His name is Thad Hadley," Lavonne said, when Hazel was gone. "He's the choir director at Bethany Lutheran."

I knew Thad Hadley. Not well. Fifty or so, thin, a big voice, nice sweaters, the kind of man who says "Blessings" when he means goodbye. Carmela had joined the choir in March. She'd said it was good for her lungs.

"Since when?" I said.

"Since the Easter cantata. They have practice Wednesdays. Practice hasn't run until eleven at night once in that church's history, Ned. I know because my Kevin sings bass."

I didn't say anything. I put sugar in my coffee, which I don't take, and stirred it.

"Here's what she's going to do," Lavonne said. "She told me in October, when she was drinking. She's going to wait until Mom's birthday dinner next Sunday. The whole family's coming. Uncle Lonnie's driving down from Grand Forks. And at dessert, she's going to stand up and tell everybody that you and her have decided to separate, very amicably, very grown-up, and that you've agreed she should keep the house because you're so generous. In front of everybody. So that by the time you figure out what's going on, the whole family's heard you agreed to it."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"That's the point. She thinks you'll be too embarrassed to say no in front of Mom. She said, 'Ned won't make a scene at Mom's birthday. Ned would rather die.'"

I looked out the window at Main Avenue, at a city plow scraping by with its yellow light turning.

"And then?"

"And then she files in February. After your thirty-two years come up on the pension in the spring, I think, but I'm not sure about that part. And she sells the house, because Thad has a condo in West Fargo but he wants something with a yard." Lavonne pushed her toast plate away. "She told me she's already looked at listings. She showed me one on her phone."

"Why are you telling me?"

Lavonne went quiet for a while.

"Because when Kevin got laid off from the sugar plant in 2016," she said, "and we didn't have the money for Christmas, you showed up at our house with a turkey and an envelope and you never said a word about it to anybody. Not even to her. And Carmela found out anyway and spent two years calling Kevin 'the charity case' at every family dinner." She looked at me. "You were nice to us. She used it as a joke. I'm not going to sit at Mom's table and watch her do it to you too."

Hazel came back with the coffee pot and filled my cup, which I hadn't touched.

"You two look like a funeral," Hazel said.

"Close," Lavonne said.

When Hazel was gone, I took out my phone.

"Who are you calling?" Lavonne said.

"Somebody I should've called about a year ago," I said. "Apparently."

At two o'clock that same Saturday afternoon, I was sitting in a little brick office over a bakery on Broadway, across a desk from a lawyer named Winona Pierce.

Winona is sixty-one, five feet tall, and wears reading glasses on a beaded chain around her neck. Arnie Tolliver, a guy I'd worked the signal crew with for twenty years, had used her when his wife left him in 2019, and Arnie still talked about her like she was a weather event.

She didn't usually see people on Saturdays. She said she'd make an exception, because Arnie had called her first and told her I wasn't the kind of man who'd ask unless the house was on fire.

"So," Winona said. "Tell me what she said. Exact words."

I told her.

She wrote it down on a yellow pad. Then she underlined "too nice to fight."

"Mr. Kenley," she said, "in North Dakota, a divorce doesn't start when somebody files papers at the courthouse. It starts when the other person gets served. The summons gets handed to her. Whoever serves first, starts first. Their lawyer is the one answering, instead of the one asking."

"Does it matter who starts?"

"Practically speaking? It means you set the clock. It means the first thing every judge, every mediator, every relative reads is your version, written down calmly, instead of her version, announced over birthday cake." She took off her glasses and let them hang. "And it means she doesn't get to stand up at her mother's party and tell fourteen people you agreed to something you didn't."

"She's going to be furious."

"Probably. Is she going to be anything else?"

I thought about Carmela's voice in that kitchen. Ned fixes things.

"No," I said.

"Then here's what we do this weekend," Winona said, and she put her glasses back on. "Monday morning, your paycheck goes to an account in your name only. Not hers, not joint. The joint account stays exactly as it is; she can have what she's put in. You don't move out. You don't take anything. You don't argue with her, and you don't tell anybody what we're doing. I draft the summons and complaint over the weekend. We file Monday, and I send a process server to hand it to her as soon as possible."

"She's at her mother's half the time right now."

"Then the server goes where she is," Winona said. "Process servers are like weather, Mr. Kenley. They don't care whose birthday it is."

"How much is this going to cost me?" I said.

"Less than the house," Winona said.

I signed the retainer. I wrote her a check for five thousand dollars out of the little savings account I'd had since before I was married, the one with my mother's name still on the passbook. Carmela had never asked about that account in twenty-nine years, because it only had about nine thousand dollars in it, and nine thousand dollars wasn't interesting to her.

When I got back out to the truck, my phone had three texts from my wife.

"Where are you?"

"Lavonne says you drove her to Target. Target doesn't take five hours."

"Ned, call me. We need to talk about last night like adults."

I put the phone face down on the seat and drove home.

Monday morning at eight o'clock, I walked into the Bell Bank branch on 13th Avenue and opened a checking account in my own name. Then I called the payroll office at the railroad from the parking lot and changed my direct deposit, which took about four minutes and one form. Then I went to work, because the crossing gates on the Dilworth line had iced up overnight and somebody had to get them moving again.

At four-ten that afternoon, Winona texted me. "Filed. Server will try the house tonight."

Carmela wasn't at the house that night. She was at her mother's, or that's what she said. The server, a woman named Dolores Romero who'd been handing people bad news in Cass County for twenty years, tried again Tuesday morning and Tuesday night. Nobody answered either time.

On Wednesday, Carmela came home.

She came in the kitchen door at six-thirty with two bags of groceries and a smile, the smile she used to use on our son's teachers at conferences. She kissed me on the cheek. She put the groceries away. She asked how my day was, which she hadn't done since about 2011.

"Cold," I said.

"I bet." She took out a package of pork chops. "I thought I'd make your mother's recipe. With the mushroom soup."

I leaned against the counter and watched her. Twenty-nine years. I knew every one of her moves the way I know a signal relay. Sweet first. Then the ask.

"Ned," she said, while the chops browned. "About Friday."

"Okay."

"I was upset. Mom's hip, the holidays, all of it. I said something stupid. I'm sorry you heard it."

"You're sorry I heard it."

"I'm sorry I said it." She flipped a chop. "But you know, we have been… drifting. You can't tell me you haven't felt it. You work, you come home, you fall asleep in the recliner. I sing in the choir. We're like roommates."

"Okay."

"So I was thinking." She turned the burner down. "Mom's birthday is Sunday. Everybody'll be there. Maybe it would be nice, and grown-up, if we just told them together. That we're taking some space. That we love each other but it's time. No fighting. No lawyers. We sit down and divide everything up fair, like friends."

"Like friends."

"You've always been so good about that kind of thing."

"About what kind of thing?"

"Being fair," she said. "Being nice."

There it was. I could almost hear the rest of it, in her mother's kitchen, through the pantry door. Too nice to fight.

"And the house?" I said.

She put the lid on the pan.

"Well. I'd need somewhere to live. And you're never home anyway. You practically live on that railroad. You could get something smaller, closer to the shop. We could work it out."

"What about the pension?"

"That's a long way off. We'd split it. Fair."

"Fair," I said.

She smiled at me. Then she reached past me for the pepper, and her hand stopped in midair. On the counter, under the mail, was a white envelope with a green bank logo in the corner. Bell Bank. My new account. They'd mailed the welcome packet to the house, which in hindsight I should have thought about.

She picked it up.

"What's this?"

"My bank."

"We don't bank at Bell."

"I do."

She turned it over. My name. Only my name.

"Ned, why do you have a new bank account?"

"Because I wanted one."

"Since when?"

"Since Monday."

She stared at me. I watched her try to put it together, and I watched her decide she couldn't, because it didn't fit what she knew about me. Then she laughed. A short little laugh, almost relieved.

"You're mad. Okay. You're mad about Friday and you opened a bank account to make a point. That's very you." She put the envelope down. "That's so very you, honey. Fine. Keep your little account. We'll still tell them Sunday?"

"I'll be there Sunday," I said.

"Good." She kissed my cheek again. "Thank you. You're a good man, Ned. You really are."

We ate the pork chops. They were good. She went to bed early, and I sat in the recliner in the dark living room with the TV off, and at eleven-fifteen I heard her in the bedroom, very quietly, on the phone.

"No, he's fine," she was saying. "He's mad but he's fine. He's going to sit there and nod. I told you. He's too nice to fight."

Then, softer: "I miss you too. Sunday. After cake."

I texted Winona from the recliner.

"She'll be at her mother's Sunday at five. Birthday dinner. Fourteen people."

Three dots appeared. Then: "Dolores can be there at six."

Then, a minute later: "Ned, are you sure? You can still pick another day."

I looked at the dark hallway where my wife's voice was coming from.

"She picked the day," I wrote back. "I'm just showing up."

Bev Harding's seventy-sixth birthday dinner was at her house, in the dining room with the extra leaf in the table and two card tables pushed against the end of it, with a bedsheet over them for a tablecloth. That's how the Hardings had done every birthday since 1971.

Fourteen people. Bev at the head with her walker parked behind her chair. Uncle Lonnie and his wife Marge from Grand Forks. Aunt Peg and Aunt Doris. Lavonne and her husband Kevin and their two grown boys, Tyler and Cole, both home from college for the weekend. Carmela's cousin Sheila and her husband. Me. Carmela. And our son, Daniel, who'd driven in from Bismarck that morning because his grandma had asked him to, and who had no idea about anything.

That last part was the one that got me. I'd thought about calling Daniel. Winona had told me not to say anything to anybody until it was served, and Daniel was twenty-six and worked for the state highway department and called his mother every Sunday. So I'd hugged him in the driveway and said nothing, and that was the hardest thing I did all week.

Dinner was roast beef, mashed potatoes, the green bean thing with the crunchy onions on top, and my hotdish from the week before, which Bev had saved for the occasion and announced to the whole table.

"Ned made this," she said. "Ned brought it over on Friday night in nine below. That's the kind of son-in-law I've got."

Carmela was sitting next to me. She squeezed my knee under the table. I looked at her hand.

Across the table, Lavonne caught my eye and looked away.

The cake came out at a quarter to six. Sheet cake from Hornbacher's, white frosting, "Happy 76th Bev" in blue. We sang. Bev cried a little, the way she does every year. Daniel took a video for his cousins who couldn't make it.

Then Carmela tapped her water glass with her fork.

"Everybody," she said, and she stood up. "Before we cut the cake. Ned and I have something we want to share."

The table got quiet. Uncle Lonnie put his fork down. Daniel lowered his phone.

"This is hard to say," Carmela said, "but we love you all and we want you to hear it from us. Ned and I have decided, together, very amicably, that it's time for us to go our separate ways." She put her hand on my shoulder. "We're still best friends. Nobody's the bad guy. And Ned has been so generous. He's agreed that I should keep the house, so Mom can still come over for Sunday dinners, and we're going to divide everything else fairly, without any lawyers or ugliness. Because that's the kind of man he is."

Somebody gasped. I think it was Marge. Daniel said, "Mom, what?"

Carmela squeezed my shoulder and looked down at me.

"Right, honey?"

I put my napkin on the table.

"No," I said.

It wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. The whole table heard it.

Carmela's smile stayed exactly where it was, like a sign somebody forgot to take down.

"Ned," she said.

"We haven't decided anything together," I said. "I haven't agreed you keep the house. I haven't agreed to anything. The first I heard of this was nine days ago, in the kitchen, when I heard you tell your mother I'd make the perfect ex-husband because I'm too nice to fight for anything in court."

Bev made a small noise. Aunt Peg looked at her plate.

"That's not…" Carmela's hand came off my shoulder. "That's a private… Ned, you're making a scene at my mother's birthday."

"You stood up," I said.

That was when the doorbell rang.

Lavonne's boy Cole was closest to the front hall, so he went. We heard the storm door, the cold coming in, a woman's voice, polite and flat. Then Cole came back into the dining room with a strange look on his face, and behind him came a short, solid woman in her sixties in a purple Minnesota Vikings parka with snow on the shoulders and a white envelope in her gloved hand.

"Sorry to interrupt, folks," she said. "Happy birthday, ma'am." She nodded to Bev. Then she looked around the table. "Carmela Kenley?"

Nobody said anything. Everybody looked at my wife, which answered the question.

Dolores Romero walked around the end of the card tables, past Uncle Lonnie and past the sheet cake with the blue letters, and held out the envelope.

"Mrs. Kenley, you've been served," she said. "Summons and complaint for divorce, Cass County District Court. Have a good night."

Carmela didn't take it. So Dolores set it down on the tablecloth in front of her plate, right next to her slice of cake, and walked back out the way she'd come. The storm door banged. A car started in the street.

Carmela picked up the envelope. That was when her hands started shaking. Fourteen people watched the paper rattle.

"You did this," she said to me. "You set this up. At my mother's birthday."

"I filed on Monday," I said. "She tried the house Monday night, Tuesday morning, and Tuesday night. You weren't home. You were at your mother's. Or you said you were."

"So you sent her here? On purpose? In front of everybody?"

"You picked tonight," I said. "You were going to tell fourteen people I'd agreed to give you the house. I didn't want them believing it for a week."

"You humiliated me!"

"You told your mother I'm the perfect ex-husband. I'm trying not to disappoint you."

Daniel stood up so fast his chair hit the wall.

"Dad. Mom. What is going on?"

"Your father," Carmela said, and her voice broke, and the tears came, real ones, "is divorcing me. At Grandma's birthday. Like a coward."

"Daniel," I said, "I'm sorry you had to find out this way. I didn't want you to find out like this either. That's the truth."

Bev was crying now too, holding on to the edge of the table.

"Ned," she said. "Ned, how could you do this to her? Here? Today?"

"Bev, I'm sorry it was today. I really am."

"You were always so good," she said. "You were always the good one."

"I still am," I said. "I'm just done being the one who doesn't say anything."

Then Uncle Lonnie cleared his throat. Lonnie is eighty, Bev's older brother, a retired grain inspector who doesn't say much and hasn't said anything foolish since the Eisenhower administration. Everybody in that family listens when Lonnie talks.

"Ned," he said. "I don't want to pry. But I'd like to know why a man like you hires a lawyer. In thirty years I never once heard you raise your voice."

I looked at Carmela. She was holding the envelope against her chest. I looked at Lavonne, who was looking straight at me, and who gave me the smallest nod.

I'd told myself I'd say it once, if somebody asked. Only once, in plain words, and then never again.

"Because she's been seeing Thad Hadley from the Bethany choir since Easter," I said. "And she planned to stand up tonight and tell you all I'd agreed to give her the house, so she could sell it and buy something with a yard with him. I heard her on the phone Wednesday night. 'Sunday. After cake.' That's why."

Nobody moved.

"That's a lie," Carmela said.

"It isn't, Cor," Lavonne said quietly from across the table. "You told me in October. You showed me the listing."

Carmela turned and looked at her sister like she'd never seen her before.

"You," she said.

"Me," Lavonne said. "Kevin sings bass, Carmela. Choir practice doesn't go till eleven."

Kevin, beside her, put his fork down and stared at his plate.

Carmela opened her mouth and nothing came out. Then she picked up her purse, and the envelope, and walked out of the dining room into the kitchen, and we heard the back door, and the four wooden steps I'd built, and her car door slamming in the driveway.

I stood up. I went around the table and kissed Bev on the cheek, the way I'd done at every birthday for twenty-nine years.

"Happy birthday, Bev," I said. "The hotdish is three-fifty if you want to heat up the rest."

Then I put on my coat and went out the front door, and my son followed me out to the truck in his shirt sleeves in eleven below.

"Dad," Daniel said. "Is it true? About the choir guy?"

"I wouldn't have said it if it wasn't."

He stood there in the snow for a long time.

"She told me last week you two were just in a rut," he said. "She told me you'd probably be relieved."

"I'm not relieved, son. I'm just not lying anymore."

He nodded. He hugged me, hard, the way he hadn't since he was a teenager. Then he went back inside to his grandmother, which was the right thing to do, and I loved him for it.

My phone started buzzing before I was out of the subdivision.

By the time I got home, I had eleven missed calls from Carmela. By midnight it was thirty-one, plus forty-seven text messages, most of them in capital letters. I read the first three. "HOW COULD YOU." "IN FRONT OF MY MOTHER." "YOU ARE NOT NICE YOU ARE A MONSTER." Then I turned the phone face down on the nightstand and went to sleep, which surprised me.

Monday morning there were fifty-two missed calls. And a voicemail from Thad Hadley.

"Ned. Thad Hadley. I think there's been a misunderstanding. I'd like to come by and clear the air, man to man. Blessings."

I didn't call him back. He came by anyway.

He came Tuesday night at seven, on foot, from his car parked down the block, as if that would keep the neighbors from noticing a man in a camel overcoat ringing my doorbell. I opened the inside door and left the storm door shut between us.

"Ned," he said through the glass. "Can I come in?"

"No."

"Okay. That's fair." He put his gloved hands up. "Look, I'm not here to fight. Carmela's a wonderful woman and she's going through a lot. She told me you'd agreed to let her have the house. She told me you two had worked it out. I'd never have… I'm a man of faith, Ned. I just want everybody to be fair."

"She told you I agreed to give her the house?"

"Months ago. She said you were fine with it. She said you were relieved."

"I never agreed to anything," I said. "My lawyer filed for divorce last Monday. North Dakota divides things fair. Fair means half. The house has a mortgage with eighty-one thousand dollars still on it. Half of what's left is what she gets. Half of the pension, when I retire, for the years we were married. Not the house free and clear. Not a down payment on anything with a yard."

He blinked.

"Half," he said.

"Half."

"She said… she said she'd walk away with the house and you'd keep paying for it until it sold. She said you'd never fight for anything."

"Thad," I said, "she said a lot of things about me in a lot of kitchens. You should ask yourself what she says about you in hers."

He stood on my step for a long time, with his breath fogging the glass. Then he said, "Blessings," the way you'd say it to a stranger, and walked back down the block to his car.

On Wednesday night, choir practice ended at eight-thirty. Kevin told Lavonne, and Lavonne told me. Carmela wasn't there. Thad had moved her to the back row of the altos.

On Thursday she called me in the middle of the day, and I answered for the first time, because it was her eighty-ninth call and I figured eighty-eight no's was enough to make the point.

"Ned." Her voice was raw. "The car insurance. The Equinox is on your policy. They're saying I need your signature to change the billing address."

"Change it to where?"

"Mom's. I'm staying at Mom's."

"Not Thad's?"

Silence. Then a sound like she'd put her hand over the phone. Then: "Please just sign it."

"I'll have Winona send it to your lawyer," I said. "That's how it works now."

"I don't have a lawyer! I can't afford a lawyer! You took the money!"

"I didn't take any money. The joint account's exactly where you left it. I just stopped putting my paycheck in it."

"That's the same thing!"

"Then I guess that tells you whose money it was," I said.

She hung up. She called back. I didn't answer.

On Saturday, her family came to my porch.

Not Carmela. Aunt Peg drove, and Bev rode in the passenger seat of Peg's Buick with her walker folded in the back, and Aunt Doris came along for moral support, the way Aunt Doris comes along to everything. I saw the Buick pull up and went out on the porch in my shirt sleeves, because I wasn't going to make a seventy-six-year-old woman with a new hip climb my steps.

Bev rolled the window down.

"Ned," she said. "We need to talk to you about Carmela."

"Okay."

"She's not eating," Peg said from the driver's seat. "She cries all day. She's sleeping in Lavonne's old room in her clothes."

"I'm sorry to hear that."

"Then fix it," Doris said from the back. "You can fix anything. You fixed Mom's steps. You fixed my garage door."

"Doris, I can fix a garage door because a garage door wants to go up and down. I can't fix something that wants to be broken."

"She made one mistake," Bev said.

"Bev," I said, as gently as I could, "she made a plan. She made it in your kitchen, and she made it in Lavonne's kitchen, and she made it on the phone in our bedroom at eleven o'clock at night. The only mistake she made was saying it with the pantry door open."

Bev looked at me through the open window for a long moment. Her eyes were wet. Then she said something I didn't expect.

"I laughed," she said. "When she said it. That night. Doris laughed and I smiled. I didn't stop her."

"No," I said. "You didn't."

"I'm sorry for that, Ned."

"Thank you, Bev."

She rolled the window back up. Peg pulled away from the curb. Doris, in the back seat, didn't look at me at all.

On Monday, Carmela came to my work.

I was at the 12th Avenue crossing on the south side, the one by the grain elevator, replacing a cracked gate arm with my helper, a kid named Marco Velez who's been on the crew a year and asks a lot of questions. It was five below and the wind was coming straight across the Red River from Minnesota. Carmela's Equinox pulled up on the shoulder and she got out in her long wool coat and walked right up to the tracks.

"Ma'am, you can't be there," Marco said.

"I need to talk to my husband."

"Marco," I said, "give us a minute."

He went and sat in the truck. Carmela stood about ten feet from me, on the gravel, with her arms crossed against the wind. Her eyes were swollen. She looked smaller than I'd ever seen her.

"You won't answer the phone," she said.

"I answered on Thursday."

"You answered to tell me to call my lawyer. I don't have a lawyer, Ned. I have a kid from Legal Services who calls me 'ma'am.'" Her voice cracked. "Everybody hates me. Mom won't hold my hand. Lavonne won't speak to me. Thad won't speak to me. Daniel won't speak to me. You did that."

"I didn't do any of that, Cor."

"You stood up at Mom's birthday and told everybody!"

"You stood up first. Uncle Lonnie asked me why. I told him once. I haven't said it to anybody since, and I'm not going to."

Behind me, the signal bells started. Ding ding ding ding. The red lights on the crossing mast began to flash, left right left right, and the good gate arm on the other side came down across the road with a hum. Far off to the north, a train horn sounded. Two longs, a short, a long.

Carmela flinched at the noise.

"Can we please just go back?" she said over it. "Please? I'll leave the choir. I'll do counseling. I'll do whatever you want."

The train came through. A BNSF freight, a hundred and ten cars of grain, the wind off it hitting us both like a wall. We stood there and waited for it, because there wasn't anything else to do. You can't talk over a grain train.

When the last car went by, the gate went up and the lights went dark.

"You know what I've done for thirty-two years?" I said.

She didn't answer.

"I keep those lights working. And the whole point of them is, when something goes wrong, they go red. They don't try to keep going. They don't pretend. The gate comes down, and nothing crosses, and nobody argues with it, until somebody fixes what's broken." I picked up my wrench off the gravel. "Carmela, you broke it on purpose. And you were counting on me to keep the gate up anyway."

"That's not fair."

"That's half," I said. "That's what fair is."

She went back to her car. Marco got out of the truck and came over, rubbing his hands together.

"Your wife?" he said.

"For a few more weeks."

He thought about that.

"She was crying pretty bad, Ned."

"She's been crying since her mother's birthday," I said. "She'll be crying when she signs."

"You okay?"

"Hand me that bolt bag," I said. "Gate's not going to fix itself."

Three weeks later, on a gray Friday morning in February, I sat in a conference room at the county's family mediation office on 2nd Avenue North with Winona on my left and a mediator named Mrs. Gutierrez at the head of a table with a box of tissues on it. Across from me sat Carmela and a young lawyer from Legal Services named Brent, who looked like he'd been out of school about eleven minutes.

It took four hours. Nobody yelled. Mrs. Gutierrez went room to room with offers like a waitress taking orders. At the end of it, we had a piece of paper. The house would be sold in the spring and the money split down the middle after the mortgage. She'd get her half of the pension for the twenty-nine years. I kept my truck, my tools, and the little savings account with my mother's name on the passbook, because I'd had it before I ever met her. She kept the Equinox and the furniture, and she kept the casserole dish. I made a point of that.

Out in the hallway, by the elevators, Carmela caught up with me.

Her mascara had run, the way it had been running for three weeks, Lavonne told me. She'd cried at her mother's. She'd cried at choir, in the back row of the altos. She'd cried in the parking lot at Bethany when Thad Hadley told her he thought they both needed "a season of discernment," which, Lavonne explained, was Lutheran for "don't call me."

"Ned," Carmela said. "Wait. Please."

I waited. Winona stood a little way off, looking at her phone, pretending not to listen.

"I'm sorry," Carmela said. "I'm so sorry. I said a terrible thing in that kitchen and I've been paying for it every day since. Thad's gone. Mom won't look at me. Daniel won't call me back. Lavonne… Lavonne." She wiped her face with the back of her hand. "I made a mistake. People make mistakes. You've always forgiven me. You've always been so nice to me. Can't you just… be nice, one more time? We could stop this. We could tear up the paper and go home."

I pushed the elevator button.

"Carmela," I said. "You told your mother I'd make the perfect ex-husband."

"I know. I know I did."

The elevator doors opened. I stepped in and held the door with my hand.

"I'm trying," I said, and let it close.
