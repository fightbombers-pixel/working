"Nathan, why on earth are you holding her arm?"

My wife gasped it more than said it, standing at the top of the ballroom stairs at the Coastal Burn Foundation gala, in front of three hundred guests in black ties and long dresses, about ten feet from a cardboard easel with my own face on it. The easel said, "Tonight's Honoree: Captain Nathan Bolton, Savannah Fire, Station 7."

I'd paid $350 for the rented tux, and the woman on my arm had paid $25,000 for her table, and her husband was the chairman of the whole foundation.

Her husband was also the man my wife had been sleeping with since February.

So I didn't let go of Meredith Sutton's arm. I walked my wife's lover's wife right down the center aisle, past the silent auction and the ice sculpture shaped like a fire helmet, pulled out a chair at table one, and sat her down in the seat with her husband's name card on it.

"Evening, Adriana," I said. "You look nice."

Six minutes later, the fourteen-foot screen behind the stage lit up, and three hundred people stopped chewing at the same time.

What my wife didn't know was what was on the little red thumb drive sitting in the AV booth at the back of the room, next to a kid named Dominic who owed me a favor.

My name is Nathan Bolton. I'm fifty years old, I've been a firefighter in Savannah, Georgia, for twenty-six years, and I'm the captain at Station 7 off Waters Avenue. The first thing they teach you about a burned building is that every fire leaves a pattern on the walls, and if you read the pattern right, it tells you exactly where the fire started and who was lying about it.

Hold on to that.

Adriana came down the stairs fast, in a green dress she'd told me cost "practically nothing" and that I later found on our Visa for $1,100. She's forty-six, pretty in that careful way where everything is planned, and she's the events director of the Coastal Burn Foundation. This whole gala was hers. The flowers, the band, the fire helmet made of ice.

She got to the table and bent down close to me, still smiling for the room.

"What is this?" she whispered. "What are you doing?"

"Sitting down. It's my table, right? I'm the honoree."

"Why is she with you?"

"Her husband stood her up." I unfolded my napkin. "Seemed rude to leave her in the parking lot."

Meredith Sutton didn't say a word. She's forty-nine, small, with silver hair cut short and a black dress buttoned up to her collarbone. She just reached across the table, picked up her husband's name card, and turned it face down.

Adriana looked at the card, and then at Meredith, and I watched her figure out about half of it. Not all of it. Half.

"I'm sorry," she said, which is what Adriana says about everything, the traffic, the weather, the price of shrimp. "I'm sorry, I just wasn't expecting... Meredith, it's so good to see you."

"Is it?" Meredith said.

That was all she said. Two words. Adriana straightened up like somebody had poked her with a fork.

And then a hand came down on my shoulder, heavy, and a man's voice said, "Captain. A word, please. Outside."

I looked up. Dr. Rafael Sutton was standing over me in a tux that fit him better than mine fit me, fifty-two, tan, with teeth you pay for and a pair of sunglasses hooked in his breast pocket. The man wears sunglasses indoors. I'd always thought that told you everything.

"After my award, Doc," I said. "I don't want to miss it."

"Now, Nathan."

His fingers dug into my shoulder. Up on the stage, the emcee tapped the microphone, the lights went down to half, and a voice from the speakers said, "Ladies and gentlemen, please take your seats. It's time to honor the man who has carried forty-one people out of burning buildings."

Rafael's hand stayed right where it was. He leaned down by my ear.

"Whatever you think you're doing," he said, "you're going to stand up and walk out of here with me, or I swear to God I'll—"

The lights went all the way down.

Nine days earlier, on a Thursday morning, I'd been in the bay at Station 7 checking the air packs when Lou Cardenas, my engineer, came around the side of the engine with a look on his face like he'd seen a ghost in a pantsuit.

"Cap," he said. "There's a lady here to see you. Rich lady. She's got a folder."

"Is she on fire?"

"No."

"Then she can wait till I finish the packs."

"Cap, I think you want to stop doing the packs."

Lou has been my engineer for nine years. He tells terrible jokes, he eats everything in the station fridge whether it's his or not, and he has never once in all that time told me to stop doing something. So I stopped.

Meredith Sutton was standing in the doorway of the bay in a navy coat, holding a blue folder against her chest with both arms. I knew who she was. I'd met her at two foundation Christmas parties. She'd been nice, quiet, and she'd asked me real questions about the job, which most of those people don't.

"Mrs. Sutton," I said. "Everything okay?"

"No, Captain. Is there somewhere we can talk?"

I took her into the office and shut the door. Lou hung around outside it pretending to read a clipboard, which I let slide.

Meredith sat down, opened the folder, and laid out the first sheet on my desk without saying a word. It was a printout of a text message. The sender was saved as "A," with a little green heart next to it.

"Thursday can't come fast enough. He's on a 24 again. Park on Habersham like last time."

I read it twice. I know what my schedule is, and I know where Habersham Street is. It runs about a block from my house.

"Where'd you get this?" I asked. My voice came out fine. That surprised me.

"Our family iPad. It sits in my kitchen. Rafael's phone backs up to it, and he's never understood how any of it works." She laid down another sheet. Then another. "I pay the bill, Captain. The account is in my name. I wasn't snooping. I was looking up a recipe, and his messages started popping up on the screen one after another, like a slot machine."

There were eleven pages. Texts back to February. A photo of my wife in a hotel bathrobe at the Westin on Hutchinson Island, holding a glass of champagne, smiling at whoever held the camera. A photo of Rafael's hand on her knee in a car I recognized, because it was my car.

"How long have you known?" I asked.

"Three weeks." Meredith folded her hands. "I've spent three weeks deciding whether to tell you. I'm not a brave woman, Captain. I've been married to him twenty-four years and I've never once raised my voice."

"Why tell me now?"

"Because of this."

She took out one more page. It wasn't a text. It was a bank statement, the foundation's operating account at a bank downtown, with a line highlighted in yellow.

"I'm the treasurer of the foundation," she said. "Unpaid. It's a title they give the chairman's wife. Every big transfer needs two signatures, and one of them is supposed to be mine. Last week the bank called me to confirm a scheduled wire. Two hundred and twelve thousand dollars, to a consulting firm I've never heard of." She tapped the page, where it said N.B. Coastal Safety Consulting, LLC. "I never authorized it. Somebody signed my name."

"Who?"

"The form came from the events office." She looked at me. "Your wife's office, Captain."

I sat back in my chair. Outside the door, I heard Lou's clipboard hit the floor.

"When does the wire go out?" I asked.

Meredith put her finger on the date at the bottom of the form.

"The fourteenth," she said. "At six o'clock in the evening."

The fourteenth was a Saturday. At six o'clock that Saturday evening, three hundred people would be sitting down to dinner in the ballroom of the DeSoto hotel, and I'd be standing on a stage holding a glass plaque with my name on it.

It was the night of the gala.

The night my wife had nominated me to be honored.

That afternoon, with Meredith's folder on the seat of my truck, I drove downtown to see a lawyer.

Lou picked her. His ex-wife had used her in their divorce, and the only nice thing Lou has ever said about that whole year was that Faye Holcomb scared him so bad he'd paid his support on time for eleven straight years.

Faye was about sixty, with reading glasses on a chain and an office over a bank on Bull Street that smelled like old paper and peppermints. She read all eleven pages without a word. Then she read the bank statement twice.

"Well," she said. "Your wife has been busy."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Let's take it in order. Georgia is a fault state, and adultery is a ground for divorce. In plain English, if we prove she was sleeping with the doctor, she can't get alimony out of you. Not a dime." She tapped the photo of Adriana in the bathrobe. "This proves it. And Mrs. Sutton got it off her own device, on her own account, so nobody stole anything."

"And the money?"

"The money is not yours, and it's not your problem, and you're not going to touch it." She took her glasses off. "Mrs. Sutton reports the forged signature to the bank in writing, today. The bank's fraud people will put a quiet hold on that wire. Quiet means nobody gets a phone call. The people who sent it will think it's still going out Saturday."

"And then what?"

"Then she goes to the police with the forged form, and they do their job." Faye looked at me over the top of the glasses. "Now tell me what you want, Captain. Because a man doesn't bring me a folder like this unless he's already got a plan."

"I'm the honoree Saturday night," I said. "Three hundred people. Her boss. His board. Half the doctors in this town."

"And?"

"And there's a screen."

Faye sat back. She didn't smile, exactly, but something around her mouth moved.

"Truth is a defense to defamation," she said. "If every word and every picture on that screen is true, and you got it legally, there's not a judge in Chatham County who'll punish you for showing it. Put nothing on that screen you can't prove. Not one guess. Not one word about the money until the police have it." She pointed a pen at me. "And Captain, whatever happens in that room, you don't throw the first punch."

"I never do."

"I've heard that before."

"Not from me, you haven't."

Back at the station, Lou was waiting in the office with his feet up on my desk, eating a sleeve of Ritz crackers that belonged to B shift. I told him the plan. He listened to the whole thing with his mouth full, then swallowed.

"Okay, three things," he said. "One, I love it. Two, you need somebody in the AV booth. Three, N.B. Coastal Safety Consulting."

"What about it?"

"N.B., Cap." He pointed a cracker at me. "Nathan Bolton. Coastal Safety. You run fire safety talks at every elementary school in the county. If I didn't know you, I'd think that was your company."

I laughed. I remember laughing, because it sounded crazy, and because it was Lou.

"It's a coincidence," I said.

"Sure." He ate the cracker. "I'm just saying, if I was a crook, I'd pick a name that made a fire captain look like a crook."

I didn't laugh at that one. I wrote it down on a sticky note and put it in my wallet, and then I asked him about the booth.

"Dominic Reyes," Lou said. "Kid runs sound and lights at the DeSoto. Twenty-four years old. You pulled his grandmother out of that kitchen fire on Wheaton Street three years ago."

"I remember. Mrs. Reyes. She made us a pound cake every week for a year."

"Well, her grandson does every event in that ballroom. And he told me at her birthday that if Captain Bolton ever needed anything, ever, he'd do it."

I met Dominic the next night at the Waffle House on Abercorn, because that's where all the truth in Savannah comes out after ten o'clock. He was a skinny kid with a ponytail and a hoodie, and he ate a pecan waffle while I explained. When I finished, he put his fork down.

"The honoree video is five minutes," he said. "Mrs. Bolton sent it to me Monday. Photos of you, kids you saved, the mayor talking. It plays off my laptop."

"Can you play something else instead?"

"I can play whatever you hand me." He wiped syrup off his chin. "But I'm not losing my job for this, Captain. If the foundation tells me to cut it, I cut it."

"That's fair. I'll need about ninety seconds."

"Ninety seconds is a long time on a fourteen-foot screen." He picked his fork back up. "What's on it?"

"The truth."

"Okay." Dominic nodded slowly. "My grandmother says you don't lie. So okay."

Meredith and I built the ninety seconds together at her kitchen table on Wednesday, while Rafael was at a surgical conference in Atlanta. We used only what we could prove. Seven texts with the dates showing. Two photos. One line from Rafael to my wife, dated the week of my birthday, that Meredith wanted at the end.

"He doesn't know the difference between a fireman and a doorman. He'll smile for the pictures and never see it coming."

"Are you sure you want this?" I asked her. "Your name's on this as much as mine."

"Captain, I've been the woman who smiles for the pictures for twenty-four years." She clicked save. "I'd like to try the other thing."

Friday night, the night before the gala, I came home to find Adriana in our bedroom with my rented tux laid out on the bed, picking lint off the jacket with a piece of tape. She'd been happy all week. Humming. Making shrimp and grits. Hugging me in the kitchen for no reason. I know now what that happiness was. It was a woman counting down.

"Try it on," she said. "I want to see you in it."

"I tried it on at the store."

"Humor me, honey."

I put the jacket on. She fussed with the shoulders, then slid her hand down into the inside pocket like she was checking the lining, and my whole body went cold, because twenty minutes earlier that's exactly where I'd put the little red thumb drive.

Her fingers stopped.

"What's this?"

I didn't move. I didn't breathe. She pulled her hand out, and it was holding a folded index card.

"Oh, your speech," she said. "Look at you, you wrote a speech." She read the top line out loud. "'I want to thank my crew at Station 7, who make me look a lot better than I am.' Nathan, that's sweet."

"It's short."

"Short is good. Nobody wants a long speech." She tucked it back in and kissed my cheek. "I'm so proud of you. I'm sorry I've been so busy. After tomorrow, I promise, things will be different."

"Yeah," I said. "I bet they will."

The thumb drive was in my sock drawer, inside a birthday card my son Brandon gave me when he was nine. I'd moved it an hour before, without knowing why. Twenty-six years of fires teaches you to trust a bad feeling.

At the bedroom door she turned around.

"Oh, and wear the blue tie tomorrow, not the black," she said. "I want you in all the pictures."

Back in the ballroom, the lights were all the way down, and Rafael Sutton's hand was still clamped on my shoulder.

Then the screen behind the stage lit up, bright white, and the first text came up in letters three feet tall.

"Thursday can't come fast enough. He's on a 24 again. Park on Habersham like last time."

Under it, in smaller letters, the date in February, and the sender. "A."

A woman at table four said, "Oh my God," loud enough to carry. Rafael's hand came off my shoulder like my jacket was on fire.

The next text came up. Then the photo of my wife at the Westin in the bathrobe, holding champagne, fourteen feet tall. Somebody's fork hit a plate. A man in the back stood up to see better.

I looked at Adriana. She was standing by the stage steps with her clipboard against her chest, and she'd gone the color of the ice sculpture. Her mouth was open, but nothing was coming out.

"Cut it!" Rafael shouted toward the back of the room. "Cut that! Turn it off!"

Nobody turned it off. Dominic was doing exactly what we'd agreed. Ninety seconds.

The photo of Rafael's hand on Adriana's knee in my car. Two more texts. And then the last one, white letters on black, all by itself.

"He doesn't know the difference between a fireman and a doorman. He'll smile for the pictures and never see it coming."

The screen went dark. For a few seconds, the only sound in the ballroom of the DeSoto hotel was the band's drummer nervously tapping a cymbal with one finger.

Then Meredith Sutton stood up. She walked to the stage, past my wife, up the three steps, and took the microphone out of the emcee's hand like it was a coffee cup.

"Good evening," she said. "I'm Meredith Sutton, the foundation's treasurer, and the chairman's wife." She looked straight down at table one. "Rafael, those were your words. I thought everyone should hear them."

She handed the microphone back and walked down the steps.

The emcee, God bless him, looked at his card, then at me, and said into the mic, "And, uh, our honoree, Captain Nathan Bolton."

I stood up, buttoned my $350 jacket, and walked to the stage. I didn't take the plaque. I just leaned into the microphone.

"I want to thank my crew at Station 7," I said, "who make me look a lot better than I am. And I want to thank my wife for nominating me. She told me she wanted me in all the pictures."

I walked out of the ballroom through the side door, and I didn't look back.

The parking garage under the DeSoto was half empty. I'd made it about halfway to my truck when I heard shoes slapping concrete behind me, fast, and Rafael Sutton yelling my name.

"Bolton! Bolton, you son of a witch, you don't get to walk away from that!"

I turned around. He came at me with his tie hanging loose and his face red under the tan, and he didn't slow down. He put both hands on my chest and shoved me hard into the side of somebody's Tahoe. The alarm went off, whooping through the whole garage.

"You think that's funny?" he shouted. "You think you humiliated me? You have no idea who you just—"

He pulled his right hand back to swing.

I've been hit before. Drunks, mostly, on medical calls, and once a guy on PCP who thought I was the devil. You learn to see it coming. I stepped inside the punch, grabbed his wrist and his collar, turned him, and laid him face down across the hood of his own black Mercedes, which was parked right there with a little sign that said Reserved, Foundation Chairman.

I held him there. I didn't hit him. I didn't need to.

"You swung first, Doc," I said, right by his ear. "There's a camera on that pillar. Smile for the pictures."

I let him go and stepped back with my hands open. Rafael rolled off the hood, breathing hard, holding his wrist. Behind me, two hotel security guards were running down the ramp, and behind them, a whole crowd of tuxedos with their phones up.

Rafael pointed at me with his good hand.

"This isn't over," he said. "You don't know what you've done."

"I know exactly what I've done."

"No," he said, and he smiled, and that smile bothered me more than the punch. "You don't."

I got in my truck and drove out of that garage with my heart going a hundred miles an hour. I was sitting at the red light on Liberty Street when my phone buzzed on the seat. It was a text from Adriana. One line.

"You don't know what you've done. The money goes tonight. Now it's all on you."

I pulled into the lot of a closed Goodwill and called Meredith. She picked up on the first ring.

"She says the money goes tonight," I said. "She says it's all on me."

"It doesn't go anywhere, Nathan. The bank's fraud man called me at five. The wire is on hold. They just haven't told the events office." She was quiet a second. "What does she mean, on you?"

I took the sticky note out of my wallet and looked at Lou's handwriting. N.B.

"I think I'm about to find out," I said.

I found out at nine the next morning. I was sitting at the kitchen table in my undershirt, with a cup of coffee I hadn't touched, when somebody knocked on the front door like they were serving a warrant. Adriana hadn't come home. She'd texted at midnight that she was at her sister Teresa's and that I should "think very hard about what kind of man I am."

The woman on my porch was in her forties, in a blazer, with a badge on her belt. Behind her stood a uniformed officer about Brandon's age.

"Captain Bolton? Detective Annie Kessinger, Savannah police, financial crimes." She held up a sheet of paper. "Can we come in? I'd like to ask you about your company."

"I don't have a company."

"That's funny." She stepped inside without waiting. "Because the Georgia Secretary of State says you do."

She laid the paper on my kitchen table, right next to my coffee. It was a registration for a limited liability company. N.B. Coastal Safety Consulting, LLC. Filed online on January ninth. Organizer and registered agent, Nathan Bolton. My name. My address. My cell phone number.

"I didn't file that," I said.

"Somebody did. And last night, somebody at the Coastal Burn Foundation tried to wire two hundred and twelve thousand dollars into a bank account owned by that company." Kessinger folded her arms. "Dr. Rafael Sutton called us at seven this morning. He says he'd suspected for weeks that you were stealing from the foundation through your wife's office, and that last night's stunt was you trying to destroy his reputation before he could expose you."

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me like a cough.

"Something funny, Captain?"

"Yes, ma'am. My engineer called this two weeks ago, over a sleeve of crackers." I stood up and got my wallet off the counter and handed her the sticky note. "That's his handwriting. N.B. Nathan Bolton. He said if he was a crook, he'd pick a name that made a fire captain look like a crook."

Kessinger looked at the sticky note a long time. Then she looked at me.

"That's a good story," she said. "It's also exactly what a smart crook would say. Don't leave the county."

At noon, my battalion chief called and told me, very politely, that I was on administrative leave until the police were done. Paid. Nothing personal. The foundation had made a formal complaint, and a man accused of stealing from a burn charity can't be the face of the fire department.

At one, Adriana walked in the front door with Teresa behind her, carrying two empty suitcases.

"I'm here for my things," she said. "Teresa's my witness, so don't try anything."

"Try what? I'm sitting at my own kitchen table."

"You know what. You attacked Rafael in a parking garage. Half of Savannah has it on video."

"Half of Savannah has him swinging first on video."

"He was defending himself!"

"From what? My napkin?"

Teresa snorted, then covered it with a cough. Adriana shot her a look and marched past me toward the bedroom. I followed her down the hall.

"You put my name on a company," I said to her back.

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"N.B. Coastal Safety Consulting. My name, my address, my phone. The detective was in our kitchen this morning."

She yanked open a dresser drawer and started throwing sweaters in a suitcase.

"Then maybe you should ask yourself why a detective is in your kitchen, Nathan."

"I'm asking you."

"And I'm telling you, I'm sorry, but I don't know anything about any company. I plan parties. I order flowers." She threw a sweater so hard it missed the suitcase. "You're the one who showed my private messages to three hundred people. You're the one who stole them off Meredith's iPad. Do you know what Rafael's lawyer says? He says that's a crime."

"It's her iPad. She pays for it. It sits in her kitchen."

"It's private!"

"So was the Westin, I guess."

That stopped her for about two seconds. Then her chin came up.

"You want to know why I did it? Fine. Because you're boring, Nathan. Because you come home smelling like smoke and fall asleep in your chair at eight-thirty watching the Braves. Because for twenty-two years I planned every party in this town and nobody ever once planned anything for me. Rafael noticed me. Rafael took me places."

"With whose money?"

She zipped the suitcase.

"Ask your company," she said. "I'm sure it'll come out in court."

She dragged the suitcase down the hall. At the front door, Teresa stopped, looked back at me, and said something quiet I'll never forget.

"Nathan, she asked me in January if I knew how to set up an LLC online. I told her no." She shook her head. "I swear to God I thought it was for a catering business."

Then she was gone too.

That night I couldn't sleep. A little after two in the morning, I was lying on the couch with the TV off when I heard it. A soft scrape, metal on metal, from the side of the house where the garage is.

I've spent twenty-six years listening for sounds that don't belong in a building. I was up and moving before I even decided to be. I didn't grab a gun. I don't own one. I grabbed the big Maglite I keep by the back door, the long one, and went out through the kitchen into the dark garage.

There was a man in there. Big, in a black hoodie, with a little penlight in his teeth, bent over my workbench. He had a manila envelope in one hand and he was pulling open the bottom drawer, the one where I keep my tax papers, with the other.

I hit the overhead light.

He spun around. He was in his thirties, thick neck, a face I'd seen before and couldn't place. He spit the penlight out and came straight at me, and he had something in his hand that flashed.

He swung it at my face. I got the Maglite up and the box cutter skidded off the barrel with a screech. He swung again, and I stepped in and drove my shoulder into his chest and we both went into the shelving, and about forty pounds of paint cans and Christmas lights came down on top of us.

He was strong, but he was slow, and he'd never been in a fire. I got hold of his wrist and slammed it against the edge of the workbench, once, twice, and the box cutter went skittering under my truck. He yelled and yanked his hand back, and when he did, he dragged it across the jagged end of a broken shelf bracket. Blood went everywhere.

He shoved me off him, hit the side door with his shoulder, and was gone into the dark, holding his hand against his chest. I heard a car start somewhere down the block and peel away.

I stood there in my garage, breathing hard, with paint all over my bare feet. The manila envelope was lying on the floor where he'd dropped it. I didn't touch it. I called 911, and then I called Detective Kessinger, who had given me her card and probably regretted it.

She was there in thirty minutes, in sweatpants, with her hair up. She looked at the blood on the shelf bracket, the blood on the side door, the blood in a trail down my driveway. Then she put on gloves, picked up the envelope, and opened it.

Inside were bank papers for N.B. Coastal Safety Consulting. A debit card with my name printed on it. A checkbook. All of it brand new.

"He wasn't here to steal anything," Kessinger said slowly. "He was here to leave it."

"For you to find when you came with a search warrant."

"Which I was planning to ask a judge for on Monday." She looked at the blood on her glove. "Well. Now I've got a lot of DNA and a very nervous judge."

My doorbell camera had him walking up the driveway at two-oh-six, hood up, but his face clear when he looked at the porch light. Kessinger stared at it on my phone for a long time.

"I know him," I said. "I've seen him somewhere."

"Captain, you saw him Saturday night." She turned the phone around to face me. "That's Curtis Pugh. He's the foundation's head of security. He was standing at the ballroom door when you walked in with Mrs. Sutton."

Meredith called me at seven that morning, before I could call her. Her voice was steady, but fast.

"The bank's fraud man called back," she said. "He traced where the money was going to go after it landed in your LLC. There was a standing transfer set up. Every dollar that hit that account was supposed to move the same day to an account in Charleston."

"Whose account?"

"Rafael's. Only Rafael's. Opened in his name alone, in March." She paused. "Nathan, your wife's name isn't on it anywhere. He was going to take all of it."

I sat down on the edge of the tub. I thought about Adriana in the bedroom, throwing sweaters, saying Rafael took her places.

"Does she know?"

"Not unless somebody tells her."

"The board's having an emergency meeting this afternoon, isn't it? About me."

"Four o'clock. The hospital conference room. Rafael called it." Meredith was quiet. "He wants you formally referred to the district attorney. Adriana will be there. She's giving a statement."

"Then I'll be there too," I said. "And so will you. Bring the bank man's papers."

I hung up and called my wife for the first time since the gala. She let it ring four times.

"What," she said.

"Adriana, meet me in the lobby of Memorial at three forty-five. Fifteen minutes before the board."

"Why would I do that?"

"Because I've got one thing you need to see before you walk into that room and say anything about me. One piece of paper. After that, you can say whatever you want."

The hospital conference room was on the fourth floor, with a long table, bad coffee and a window looking at the parking deck. When I walked in at four o'clock, eleven people were already sitting around that table. Board members, two hospital vice presidents, a lawyer for the foundation. Rafael sat at the head, with a soft cast on his right wrist and his sunglasses in his pocket. Curtis Pugh stood behind him by the wall, in a blazer, with his left hand in his pocket.

Detective Kessinger sat in a chair in the corner like she was waiting for a bus. Faye Holcomb sat next to her with her reading glasses on their chain. Meredith sat halfway down the table with a folder in front of her.

Adriana came in last. Her eyes were red. She'd been crying in the lobby for fifteen minutes after I showed her the bank man's paper, the one with the Charleston account in Rafael's name and nobody else's. She didn't sit down next to Rafael. She sat down at the far end of the table, across from me.

Rafael didn't notice. He was too busy being the chairman.

"Thank you all for coming on short notice," he said. "As you know, Saturday night, this foundation was the victim of a vicious, illegal attack by Captain Bolton, who had every reason to want to silence me. Because I had discovered that he was using his wife's office to steal from this foundation through a shell company in his own name. Detective Kessinger is here, and I'd ask her to—"

"Rafael," Adriana said.

He kept going. "—to take the board's formal referral, and Mrs. Bolton has bravely agreed to give a statement about her husband's—"

"Rafael." Louder. "Who's Curtis's left hand for?"

The room went quiet. Every head turned toward the wall. Curtis Pugh took his hand out of his pocket without thinking, and it was wrapped in a thick white bandage from the knuckles to the wrist, spotted brown where it had bled through.

"Kitchen accident," Rafael said.

"In my husband's kitchen?" Adriana said. "At two in the morning?"

Kessinger stood up out of her chair.

"Mr. Pugh, I have a warrant for a DNA swab," she said, "and I'd be happy to do it here, or downtown. Your call."

Pugh looked at the door, then at Rafael. Rafael wouldn't look at him.

"He told me to," Pugh said. "He told me to put the envelope in the drawer. He said nobody would get hurt. He said the captain sleeps like the dead."

"Shut up, Curtis," Rafael said.

"Not for a box cutter charge, Doc. Not for fifteen hundred bucks."

The uniformed officer from my porch came in from the hallway, and Kessinger cuffed Curtis Pugh right there against the window, with the parking deck behind him.

Rafael stood up. He put his good hand flat on the table and turned to Adriana, and his voice went soft and smooth, the way I imagine it went on all those Thursdays.

"Adriana. Sweetheart. Whatever he showed you, he's lying. You know me."

"I know the Charleston account," she said. "The one with only your name on it. Opened in March. Every dollar, Rafael. You were going to take every dollar and leave Nathan holding the company and me holding nothing."

"That account was for us. For our future."

"Then why isn't my name on it?"

Rafael opened his mouth and nothing came out. For the first time since I'd known him, he didn't have a line ready.

Adriana turned to Kessinger. Her hands were shaking.

"He made me do it," she said. "The transfer form, the signature, all of it. It was his plan. He said Nathan would be on stage and nobody would notice until Monday. He said if anything went wrong, it'd be Nathan's name on the company and not mine. I didn't want to. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry."

"You're going to want a lawyer before you say anything else, Mrs. Bolton," Kessinger said.

"I don't care. I'll tell you everything. It was all Rafael."

Rafael came around the table fast toward her, and the uniformed officer stepped into his path. Rafael tried to shove past him with his good arm, and that was the end of it. The officer turned him into the wall, and Kessinger came over with a second pair of cuffs.

"Rafael Sutton, you're under arrest for conspiracy to commit theft, forgery, and burglary," she said, "and you'll be getting a few more by Friday."

"You can't," he said into the wall. "I'm the chairman."

"Not as of this vote." It was the foundation's lawyer, a gray-haired man who hadn't said a word yet. "All in favor of removing Dr. Sutton as chairman, effective immediately?"

Eleven hands went up around the table. Meredith raised hers last, slowly, and kept it up the longest.

They walked Rafael out. He didn't look at Meredith. He didn't look at Adriana. At the door he looked at me, and I gave him the same thing he'd given me in the garage. A smile for the pictures.

Adriana was crying at the end of the table. I stood up and walked down to her, and she looked up at me with her mascara running.

"Nathan, I swear, it was him. I was in love with him. He used me. You have to believe me. It was all his idea."

"Adriana, when did you meet Rafael?"

"What?"

"The first text. The first time. When was it?"

"February. Valentine's week. You've seen them."

I took two sheets of paper out of my jacket pocket and laid them down on the table in front of her, side by side. Faye had pulled the first one from the Secretary of State on Monday morning. Meredith had pulled the second one from the foundation's own board minutes.

The first was the LLC registration. N.B. Coastal Safety Consulting. Filed online on January ninth.

The second was the honoree nomination form for Captain Nathan Bolton. Submitted by the events director, Adriana Bolton. January ninth.

Same day. A month before the first text. A month before Rafael ever parked on Habersham Street.

Adriana looked down at the two pages. She didn't touch them. Her lips moved a little, like she was counting.

"You picked me first," I said. "Before him. You picked my name for the company and my face for the stage on the same afternoon, and then you went and found a man to help you carry it."

"Nathan—"

"I read burn patterns for a living. That's where this one started."

I slid both pages across the table to Detective Kessinger. She picked them up and read them, and then she looked at my wife for a long moment, and reached for her notebook.

"Mrs. Bolton," she said, "I'm going to need you to tell me about January ninth."

I picked up my jacket off the back of the chair.

"Wear something nice, Adriana," I said. "They'll want you in all the pictures."
