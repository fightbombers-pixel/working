"Yes, my love, I'm marrying him tomorrow. But you are my true love. I'm only doing it for his money."

My fiancée said that in a whisper, behind the bathroom door upstairs, while twenty-six of our rehearsal dinner guests were still drinking margaritas on my patio below.

She'd told me she was going up "to call her mom." I'd come up a minute later to get my reading glasses for the toast, and I was standing in the laundry room next door, where the old vent shares a wall with that bathroom. Rosa used to complain you could hear a sneeze through that vent. Rosa was right.

The ring I'd bought Leticia cost fourteen thousand dollars. I'd been a widower for six years, and I'd waited five of them before I let anybody past my front door.

I didn't kick the door in. I took my phone out of my shirt pocket, pressed the red button, and held it up to the vent.

"How much longer, baby?" she whispered. "A year, maybe less. Texas is community property, Reid. Anything he puts my name on, half is mine. And he's going to put my name on the house. He already said so."

I let it run for three minutes and forty seconds.

Eighteen hours later, a hundred and forty wedding guests were staring at an empty spot under an arch of white roses, and Leticia was the one holding the microphone, and her hands were shaking so badly the feedback squealed.

What she didn't know was that her "true love" was sitting at our wedding, at table nine, under a name she'd given me herself.

My name is Oscar Fuentes. I'm fifty-two years old, and I'm a fire captain in Corpus Christi, Texas, Station 9, twenty-six years on the job. The one thing the job teaches you, more than anything else, is that smoke always comes from somewhere. You don't argue with smoke. You find the source.

Hold on to that.

The toilet flushed. The faucet ran. I stopped the recording and stepped back into the hallway, and I had my reading glasses in my hand by the time the bathroom door opened.

Leticia came out smiling. She's forty-six, she has the kind of laugh that fills a restaurant, and that night she was wearing a sage green dress and the diamond I'd put on her finger last Christmas.

"There you are," she said. "Come on, everybody's waiting for your toast."

"How's your mom?" I said.

"Oh, she's fine, she's just tired. She says good night."

I walked to the window at the end of the hall and looked down at the patio. There, under the string lights, in a lawn chair by the grill, holding a margarita the size of a fishbowl, sat her mother, Delia Ramos. She saw me in the window and waved.

I waved back.

"Huh," I said.

Leticia came and stood beside me and looked down at her mother, and for about one second her face did nothing at all. Then she laughed.

"I meant my aunt. My aunt in Laredo. I call her Mom, I always have."

"Sure," I said. "Let's go give that toast."

I gave it, too. I stood on my own patio and raised a glass of iced tea, because I don't drink the night before anything important, and I said, "To tomorrow. May everybody get exactly what they came for."

People clapped. Leticia kissed my cheek. And over by the cooler, a tall man in a pressed guayabera shirt clapped a little slower than everybody else and smiled into his beer.

Her cousin Reid. Up from Houston. Forty-nine years old, a car salesman by trade, with a silver watch he kept turning on his wrist. She'd introduced me to him at the rehearsal that afternoon and said, "Reid's like a brother to me. He's going to sit with Mom at table nine."

He shook my hand on his way out that night and held it a little too long.

"Big day tomorrow, Captain," he said. "You're a lucky man."

"I'm starting to feel that way," I said.

Everybody was gone by eleven. Leticia went to bed in the guest room, because her mother had told her it was bad luck to sleep with the groom the night before. Somebody up there has a sense of humor.

I sat at my kitchen table for an hour and listened to that recording twice with one earbud in. Then I went to the garage, found the box I'd been saving for the honeymoon, and took out the little velvet case for the wedding bands.

I took her band out and left it on my dresser. I tore a corner off the back of the seating chart, the one with table nine on it, and I wrote four words with a Sharpie.

"I heard your call."

I put the note in the ring box, snapped it shut, and set it on the kitchen counter next to the coffee maker, where she'd find it before anything else. Then I packed a duffel bag, got in my truck, and drove to the firehouse.

My phone buzzed at a quarter after one. "Where are you?? Nervous? Lol."

I didn't answer.

At six forty in the morning it started buzzing and didn't stop. By seven, there were nineteen missed calls on the screen. Abel Guerra, my lieutenant, came into the kitchen in his socks with his hair standing up and looked at my phone dancing across the table.

"Cap," he said. "Isn't today your wedding?"

"It was," I said.

Abel is forty-four, he's been on my crew for eleven years, and he reads every true crime book in the county library. He poured himself a coffee, sat down across from me, and didn't say a word while I played him the recording.

When it ended, he set his mug down very carefully.

"Who's Reid?"

"Her cousin from Houston," I said. "The guy at table nine."

"Her cousin."

"That's what she told me."

Abel rubbed his face with both hands. "Man, I knew there was something about that guy. He tipped the bartender at the rehearsal with a fifty. Car salesmen don't tip like that unless it's somebody else's money."

The phone buzzed again. Twentieth call. The screen said "Leti" with a little heart she'd put there herself.

"You going to answer?"

"Once," I said, and I picked it up.

"Oscar!" She was crying, or doing something that sounded like crying. "What is this? What is this note? What call? I was talking to my aunt, I told you!"

"You told your aunt she was your true love?"

"It's an expression! It's how we talk in my family, 'my love,' 'mi amor,' everybody says it!"

"And the part about Texas being community property? Is that an expression too?"

That stopped her. I could hear her breathing.

"You recorded me," she said, and the crying was gone like somebody had turned off a faucet.

"I recorded what came through my own wall, in my own house."

"That's illegal!"

"Then call the cops," I said. "I'm at the fire station. They know where it is."

"Oscar, listen to me. There are a hundred and forty people coming to that venue at four o'clock. My mother paid for the dress. My whole family drove up from Laredo. If you don't show up, I swear to God, I'll tell every single one of them what you are."

"What am I, Leticia?"

"A sad old man who sees burned bodies for a living and finally cracked," she said. "Everybody knows firemen get PTSD. Everybody will believe me."

"Maybe," I said. "Tell Reid I said good luck."

I hung up. Abel was staring at me.

"You're not going?"

"No," I said. "But Isabel is."

My daughter is twenty-two, she's a nursing student at A&M Corpus Christi, and she had not liked Leticia from the first dinner. She never said it out loud. She just called her "your friend" for eight months, even after the engagement, and every time she did it I pretended not to notice.

I called her and told her everything. When I was done, she was quiet for so long I thought the call had dropped.

"Dad," she said finally, "I'm so sorry."

"Don't be sorry. I need you to go to that venue this afternoon."

"Why on earth would I go?"

"Because she's going to stand up there and tell a hundred and forty people a story about me," I said. "And I want somebody I trust to hear exactly what she says. Word for word."

"You want me to record her?"

"I want you to watch her. And watch table nine."

The Bayfront Pavilion had cost me a nine-thousand-dollar deposit. I didn't go near it. I spent the afternoon on the apparatus floor polishing the chrome on Engine 9, which is what I do when I can't think, while Abel pretended to do inventory and walked past me every ten minutes.

At four twenty, my phone buzzed. It was a video from Isabel.

There was Leticia in her white dress at the front of the pavilion, under the arch of roses I'd paid for, holding the DJ's microphone. The guests were in their chairs, a sea of pastel and seersucker, and a couple of my own crew's wives were in the third row with their mouths open.

"I'm so sorry, everybody," Leticia said into the mic, and her voice cracked right on cue. "Oscar isn't coming. He... he's not well. He's been struggling for a long time with what he sees at work. Last night he had some kind of episode, and he left me a crazy note, and he disappeared. I've been so scared for him."

A woman in the front row put her hand over her heart.

"Please pray for him," Leticia said. "And please don't believe anything he says right now. He isn't himself."

Then she lowered the mic and let her mother hug her, and the whole room went "aww."

I watched it twice. Abel watched it over my shoulder.

"PTSD," he said. "She's going full PTSD on you."

"She told me she would," I said. "At least she's honest about something."

A text from Isabel came in right behind the video.

"Dad. Everybody here is crying except one guy. Her cousin Reid. He's at table nine eating cake. He cut it himself. Nobody even cut it yet and he cut himself a piece."

A second text came in a second later.

"He just kissed her mom on the cheek and said 'We'll get him next time, Delia.' Next time, Dad. What does he mean, next time?"

Next time. I read those two words maybe ten times standing there on the apparatus floor.

Then I did what you do when there's smoke coming out of a wall. I started opening things up.

Monday morning I sat across a desk from Miriam Contreras, the meanest divorce and family lawyer in Nueces County. Miriam is fifty-five, she wears her reading glasses on a beaded chain, and she has the bedside manner of a cinder block. Six years ago my crew pulled her mother out of a kitchen fire, and at the hospital Miriam told me, "Captain, if you ever need a lawyer, my first hour is free, and after that I'm expensive and worth it."

She listened to the recording with her eyes closed. When it ended, she opened them.

"Well," she said. "She's a charmer."

"Can I use it?"

"Carefully. Texas lets you record a conversation you're part of. You weren't part of that call. You were a man standing in his own laundry room listening to a voice come through his own wall, which is a gray area, and I don't like gray areas in front of a judge." She tapped her pen on the desk. "So we don't lead with it. We keep it in the drawer. We let her tell her story under oath first, and if she lies, the recording comes out to show she's lying. That's a much easier door to walk through."

"You think she'll sue me?"

Miriam slid a sheet of paper across the desk. "She already did. Well, her lawyer sent this over at eight o'clock this morning. That's how I knew you were coming in."

It was a demand letter on the stationery of a Houston firm I'd never heard of. It said that Leticia Ramos had quit her job at the dental office in reliance on my promise of marriage. It said she had moved her belongings into my home. It said I had promised, in front of witnesses, to put her name on the deed to my house. And it demanded forty-one thousand dollars for the cost of the wedding, her lost wages, and "emotional distress caused by Mr. Fuentes' erratic abandonment."

At the bottom, it said she would be keeping the engagement ring "as a gift."

"Forty-one thousand," I said. "I paid for that wedding. Every dime of it."

"Did you?" Miriam said. "Whose name is on the venue contract?"

I opened my mouth and closed it again. Leticia had handled the venue. Leticia had handled the caterer. I'd handed her the money in cashier's checks because she said the vendors liked it better that way, and she'd signed everything.

"I'll want every receipt you have," Miriam said. "And Captain, the ring. In Texas, an engagement ring is what the courts call a conditional gift. In plain words, it's a gift on the condition that the wedding happens. Who called it off usually matters. She'll say you did. You'll say she called it off the minute she whispered into that vent."

"And the house? I never promised her the house."

"Did you ever say anything that sounds like it?"

I thought about it. I thought about a night in May, on the patio, when she'd said she felt like a guest in Rosa's house, and I'd said, "It'll be ours, Leti. I'll figure it out."

"Maybe," I said.

"That's what she's counting on," Miriam said. "And here's something you didn't know you needed to tell me. Who owns that house right now?"

"Rosa's trust. For Isabel. Rosa set it up when she got sick. I live there for life, and then it's Isabel's."

Miriam smiled for the first time. It wasn't a nice smile. It was the smile of a woman who'd just found a twenty in an old coat.

"So you couldn't have put her name on that deed if you tried."

"I guess not."

"Does she know that?"

"I never told her. It never came up."

"Then don't tell her now," Miriam said. "Let's see how much she builds on a house she can't have."

On the way out to my truck, my phone buzzed. It was Abel.

"Cap. Where are you?"

"Leaving the lawyer's."

"Come by the station. I did some reading." I could hear him grinning. "Your fiancée's cousin isn't her cousin."

Abel had been busy. He had his laptop open on the kitchen table at Station 9, between a jar of jalapeños and a stack of training manuals, and he spun it around when I walked in.

"Galveston County marriage records are public," he said. "You can search them online for free. I put in Leticia Ramos."

"And?"

"And in 2009, Leticia Ramos married a Reid Kessler." He clicked to the next screen. "Divorced in 2015. Uncontested. No kids."

I sat down hard in the chair.

"Her ex-husband," I said.

"Her ex-husband, who drove up from Houston to sit at table nine and eat your cake." Abel clicked again. "And that's not even the interesting part. In 2017, Leticia Ramos married again. A man named Danny Cervantes, Pasadena, Texas. Divorced in 2019. Eighteen months."

"Eighteen months."

"I found him on Facebook," Abel said. "Retired refinery operator. Widower. Wife died of cancer in 2016." He paused. "Cap, he looks like you. Not the face. The situation."

He turned the laptop toward me. There was a photo on Danny Cervantes' page from 2017. A backyard wedding, string lights, a sheet cake. Leticia in a white sundress. And standing behind the bride and groom with his arm around Delia Ramos, in a pressed guayabera shirt, was a younger, thinner Reid Kessler, holding a beer and smiling into it.

The caption said, "Me and my beautiful bride, and her cousin Reid who drove all the way from Houston!"

"Next time," I said.

"What?"

"That's what he told her mother at the wedding. 'We'll get him next time.'" I stood up. "Abel, what are you doing Wednesday?"

"Whatever you're doing Wednesday."

Pasadena is about three and a half hours from Corpus if you drive like a sane person, and a little under three if Abel drives. We took his pickup. Danny Cervantes had answered my Facebook message in eleven minutes with one line: "I been waiting seven years for somebody to call me."

He lived in a brick ranch house off Fairmont Parkway with a smoker in the side yard and a porch light that stayed on in the daytime because, he said, he forgot it was on. He was sixty, heavyset, with big scarred refinery hands, and he made us coffee so strong you could stand a spoon in it.

"She took half this house," he said, before I'd even sat down. "Eighteen months of marriage, and she walked away with a hundred and twelve thousand dollars of Rosemary's and my equity. Twenty-two years we paid on this house. She paid on it for one."

"How?"

"She got me to refinance. Put her name on the new loan, so she was on the title. Said it was for her credit, so we could buy a lake house someday." He laughed, short and bitter. "There was no lake house. There's never a lake house. Then about a year in, she started saying I was cold. Said I was still married to my dead wife. Then one day she filed, and her lawyer said Texas is community property and she had a right to half of what she was on."

Abel glanced at me. I didn't say anything.

"And Reid?" I said.

Danny's jaw tightened. "Reid was her cousin. He came to the wedding. He came to Thanksgiving. He came to my birthday. He helped me move a deep freezer." He shook his head. "I found out after the divorce that they were married before. A guy at the courthouse told me. By then it was done. I went to a lawyer, and he said lying about a cousin isn't a crime, and nobody was going to dig through a finished divorce for one lie, so there was nothing to do."

"Did she ever lie on a paper?" I asked.

Danny looked at me for a second. Then he got up, went to a hall closet, and came back with a banker's box. He set it on the table and pulled out a thick stapled document.

"My lawyer took her deposition," he said. "During the divorce. Sworn testimony. I never read the whole thing. I couldn't stand to." He flipped through it and put his thick finger on a line on page fourteen. "But I remember this part."

I read it out loud.

"Question: Mrs. Cervantes, have you been married before? Answer: No. This is my first marriage. I waited for the right man."

Abel let out a long whistle.

"She lied under oath," I said.

"Seven years ago," Danny said. "My lawyer said it didn't matter, the divorce was over, nobody was going to do anything about it."

"It matters now," I said. "Danny, can I borrow this?"

"Captain, you can have the whole box."

Right then my phone buzzed on the table. The screen said "Leti" with the little heart.

Danny saw it, and his face went still. "That her?"

"That's her." I put it on speaker. "Hello, Leticia."

"Oscar," she said, all sweetness, like the last four days hadn't happened. "I've been thinking. This is so ugly. Neither of us wants a lawsuit. Let's just make it go away."

"How?"

"Twenty-five thousand. I keep the ring, you pay me twenty-five, and we never have to see each other again. That's less than half what my lawyer says I'll get. I'm being generous because I loved you."

Danny Cervantes made a small noise in his throat. I looked at him. He was staring at my phone like it was a snake on his kitchen table.

"That's generous, all right," I said.

"So we have a deal?"

"No."

"No?" Her voice went up a notch. "Oscar, I'm offering you a way out."

"I know what you're offering me," I said. "The answer is no."

"Then I hope you have a good lawyer," she said, "because you're going to need one. And I hope your fire chief likes reading about crazy captains."

She hung up.

Danny looked at me. "She's going to go after your job."

"She told me she would," I said. "At least she's honest about something."

We got back to Corpus a little after nine that night. Abel dropped me off at the curb, and I stood there with Danny's banker's box under my arm and looked up my driveway.

There was a black Chevy Tahoe parked behind my truck, with Harris County dealer plates, and a tall man in a guayabera shirt sitting on my front porch steps, turning a silver watch on his wrist.

"Captain," Reid Kessler said. "We need to talk. Man to man."

I set the box down on the hood of my truck.

"Sure," I said. "You go first."

He stood up slowly. He was taller than me by a couple of inches, and he made sure I noticed it.

"Leticia's upset," he said.

"She tends to be."

"She loved you, you know. She really did."

"She told you that on the phone the other night? Right before she told you you were her true love?"

He smiled, but only with his mouth. "I'm her cousin, Oscar."

"Funny," I said. "The Galveston County Clerk says you're her ex-husband."

The smile stayed on his face, but something behind it slipped, the way a ladder slips on wet pavement before it falls.

Across the street, Mrs. Ochoa's porch light came on. Mrs. Ochoa is seventy-three, she prays the rosary every evening in a rocking chair, and nothing on this block happens without her knowing it. She sat down in her chair, crossed her ankles, and watched us like we were the evening news.

"You don't know what you're talking about," Reid said.

"I know you married her in 2009. I know you divorced in 2015. I know in 2017 you went to a wedding in Pasadena as her cousin and helped a widower move a deep freezer." I watched his face. "I know you told her mother at my wedding, 'We'll get him next time.'"

"So what? It's a free country. People get married, people get divorced. People stay friends."

"Friends," I said. "Does a friend help her lie under oath?"

Reid took a step toward me. "Here's what's going to happen, Captain. You're going to pay her. Because if you don't, she's going to walk into your fire chief's office tomorrow morning and tell him you're unstable. She's going to tell him you've been drinking on shift. She's going to tell him you scared her, and she's going to cry, and she's good at crying, you've seen it. And once that's in your file, it never comes out."

"You'd better hurry," I said. "Chief Benavides gets in at seven."

"You think this is funny?"

"I think it's familiar," I said. "Danny Cervantes says hi."

He froze. Across the street, Mrs. Ochoa stopped rocking.

For a second Reid Kessler didn't look like a car salesman anymore. He looked like what he was, a forty-nine-year-old man standing in a stranger's driveway after dark with nothing in his hands.

"Danny," he said, very quietly, "doesn't have anything."

"Danny had a hundred and twelve thousand dollars," I said. "Now he has a banker's box. And I have the banker's box."

Reid looked at the box on the hood of my truck. Then he looked at me. Then he turned around, walked to his Tahoe, got in, and slammed the door so hard the side mirror shook.

He rolled the window down before he backed out.

"She'll take your job, Captain," he said. "You watch. She's done it before."

The Tahoe pulled away. Mrs. Ochoa called across the street, "Oscar, mijo, do you need me to call somebody?"

"No, ma'am," I said. "But thank you."

"That man has bad shoes," she said, and went inside.

At six fifty-eight the next morning, before I'd even finished my first cup of coffee, my phone rang. It was Chief Benavides himself.

"Oscar," he said. "I need you in my office at noon. A woman just filed a formal complaint against you. She says you were drunk on duty the night of your rehearsal dinner."

Chief Ramon Benavides has a mustache like a push broom and a desk covered in so many plaques you can't see the wood. He's sixty-one, he's been my boss for nine years, and he does not like surprises.

He slid a printed email across the desk.

"Read it."

It was from Leticia, sent at six forty that morning. It said that on the night of our rehearsal dinner I had been "visibly intoxicated," that I had "screamed at her and frightened her," and that I had then "driven drunk to the fire station and reported for duty in that condition, endangering the public."

"Was any of this true?" the chief asked.

"Not one word," I said. "I drank iced tea all night. Twenty-six people saw me do it. I drove to the station a little after midnight. I wasn't on duty, Chief. I slept in the bunk room because I didn't want to sleep under the same roof as her."

"Who saw you come in?"

"Abel Guerra. Torres. Whoever was on watch."

Chief Benavides picked up his desk phone and called down to the watch desk. He asked for the night log from that Friday. Three minutes later a probie knocked on the door with a clipboard.

The chief ran his finger down the page. "'Captain Fuentes, off duty, arrived 0012, bunk room. Advised watch he would be leaving in a.m.'" He looked up. "Who wrote this?"

"Torres," the probie said. "He said the captain made coffee and asked him how his mother's surgery went."

"Did he seem drunk to Torres?"

The probie grinned. "Torres said the captain corrected his spelling on the log, sir."

The chief set the clipboard down and looked at me for a long moment.

"She filed a false report with my department," he said. "In writing. With her name on it."

"Yes, sir."

"You know what happens to that, Oscar? It goes in a file. Not your file. A file. And if anybody ever asks me about it, I'll tell them the truth, under oath if I have to."

"I might take you up on that, Chief."

"I figured," he said. He pushed the email back toward me. "Make a copy for your lawyer. And Oscar? Next time you get engaged, bring her by the station first. Torres can spell-check her."

Miriam Contreras took the copy, the night log and Danny Cervantes' banker's box, and she read every page of the box over a weekend. On Monday she called me.

"Captain," she said, "she's not going to stop."

"I didn't think so."

"Her Houston lawyer filed suit on Friday. County Court at Law Number Three. Forty-one thousand dollars, plus the ring, plus what they're calling 'the promised interest in the marital residence.' She wants a jury."

"There is no marital residence. We never got married."

"Which is why I want her deposition before anybody sees a jury," Miriam said. "Sworn testimony. Her sitting across the table from me, under oath, with a court reporter typing every word. I want her to tell her story in her own words."

"And then?"

"And then," Miriam said, "we find out what her words are worth."

The deposition took place three weeks after the wedding that didn't happen, in Miriam's conference room on the fourth floor of a building on Shoreline Boulevard, with a window that looked out over the marina. Leticia came in a navy suit with her hair pulled back, and her Houston lawyer, a young man named Felipe Lara who kept clicking his pen, sat beside her.

Reid Kessler wasn't there. I'd have bet my pension he was waiting in a Tahoe downstairs.

I sat at the end of the table. Miriam had told me I could be there as a party to the case, and that I wasn't to say a single word. "Not a cough, Captain. Not a sigh. You're furniture."

Leticia wouldn't look at me. She looked at the court reporter, at the window, at her lawyer's pen.

Miriam swore her in and started slow, the way you start up a ladder.

"Ms. Ramos, you quit your job at the dental office in reliance on the marriage?"

"Yes. Oscar asked me to. He said I'd never have to work again."

"And you moved your belongings into his home?"

"Yes."

"And he promised to put your name on the deed?"

"Yes. On the patio, in May. He said, 'It'll be ours.' He said it more than once."

"Did you know who owned the house?"

"Oscar owns it."

Miriam slid a single sheet across the table. "This is a certified copy of the Rosa Fuentes Living Trust. The house has been owned by that trust since 2018. Mr. Fuentes has the right to live in it. He doesn't own it, and he couldn't put your name on it if he wanted to. It goes to his daughter."

Leticia looked at the paper. For about one second her face did nothing at all.

"He never told me that," she said.

"No," Miriam agreed. "He didn't. Let's move on. The night before the wedding, Ms. Ramos, you went upstairs to make a phone call. Who were you calling?"

"My aunt. In Laredo. I call her Mom."

"What's your aunt's name?"

"Esperanza."

"Did you call her anything else during that call?"

"No."

"Did you call her 'my love'?"

Leticia's lawyer stopped clicking his pen.

"Maybe," Leticia said. "It's how we talk in my family."

"Did you tell her you were marrying Mr. Fuentes for his money?"

"No. Absolutely not. I loved Oscar. I'd never say anything like that."

"Did you speak to Reid Kessler that night?"

"Reid was at the dinner. He's my cousin."

"Is he?" Miriam said.

"Yes."

Miriam opened a folder and took out two pieces of paper. She slid the first one across the table.

"This is a marriage record from Galveston County. Leticia Ramos and Reid Kessler, 2009." She slid the second one. "This is the decree of divorce, 2015."

Felipe Lara leaned over to look at it. He looked at it for a long time.

"Ms. Ramos," Miriam said, "is Reid Kessler your cousin, or your ex-husband?"

Leticia's lawyer put his hand on her arm. "Let's take a break."

"We can," Miriam said pleasantly. "But first, one more question, so we don't lose our place. Ms. Ramos, other than Mr. Kessler, have you ever been married to anybody else?"

Leticia looked at the papers. Then, for the first time all morning, she looked at me. I've seen that look before. I've seen it on people standing in the front yard while their house burns, trying to decide what to run back in for.

"No," she said.

Miriam reached into Danny Cervantes' banker's box, took out a stapled deposition, and turned it to page fourteen. She slid it across the table so that it lay right on top of the Galveston papers.

"Then who is Danny Cervantes?"

Felipe Lara read page fourteen. Then he read it again. Then he turned to his client and said, very quietly, "Ms. Ramos, I need to speak with you in the hallway. Right now."

They were in the hallway for twenty-two minutes. I counted.

When they came back, Felipe Lara didn't sit down. He gathered his papers and said, "My client will be seeking other counsel. I'm withdrawing from this matter."

Leticia looked at him like he'd slapped her.

"You can't quit," she said. "I paid you."

"Ma'am," he said, "you paid me a retainer. You didn't pay me to sit next to perjury."

He walked out. Leticia stood there in her navy suit with her purse clutched against her chest, and then she turned to me, and her voice dropped to that whisper I'd heard through the vent.

"You think you're so smart. Reid says no judge will ever hear that tape. It's illegal. You're the one who'll be in trouble."

"Maybe," I said.

It was the only word I said all day. Miriam gave me a look anyway.

Leticia didn't drop the suit. I'll give her that. She went and found a new lawyer, and when he quit too, she decided to represent herself, which Miriam said was like deciding to do your own appendectomy because you watched a video once.

The hearing was in County Court at Law Number Three, in the Nueces County Courthouse, seven weeks after the wedding that never happened. Miriam had filed what she called a motion for summary judgment. In plain words, it meant: Judge, look at the papers, there's nothing here for a jury, throw it out.

Judge Teresa Galvan was sixty-four, small, silver-haired, and had a reputation for reading every page you gave her and remembering where you hid the bad ones. The courtroom was mostly empty. Abel sat in the second row in his only tie. Isabel sat next to him with her arms crossed. Danny Cervantes had driven down from Pasadena that morning and sat behind them in a shirt that still had the fold lines from the package.

Across the aisle, Leticia sat alone at the plaintiff's table with a stack of papers held together with a binder clip. Behind her, in the first row, in a pressed guayabera shirt, sat Reid Kessler, turning his silver watch.

Judge Galvan let Leticia go first. She stood up and told it the way she'd told it at the pavilion. The promises. The job she gave up. The crazy note. The scared, sick man who disappeared on her wedding day.

"He had an episode, Your Honor," she said. "It's not his fault. Firemen see terrible things. But I'm the one who paid for it."

"Thank you, Ms. Ramos," the judge said. "Ms. Contreras."

Miriam stood up. She didn't play the tape. She didn't need to yet.

She handed up the trust. She handed up the Galveston marriage and divorce records. She handed up Leticia's deposition, with the line where she swore Reid was her cousin and the line where she swore she'd never married anybody else. Then she handed up page fourteen of the Cervantes deposition, where she swore she'd never married anybody at all.

Then she handed up Chief Benavides' sworn statement and the night log in Torres' handwriting.

"Your Honor," Miriam said, "the plaintiff told this court, under oath, that she was calling her aunt that night. She also told my client's employer, in writing, that he was drunk on duty when he was off duty and drinking iced tea in front of twenty-six people. We have a recording of what she actually said that night. We wouldn't need it except to show that her sworn testimony isn't true. She's put her testimony in front of you. I ask permission to impeach it."

Judge Galvan looked over her glasses at Leticia. "Ms. Ramos, do you have an objection?"

"Yes! That tape is illegal! He recorded me in a bathroom!"

"Through a wall in his own house," Miriam said.

The judge thought about it for a moment. Then she said, "I'll hear it for the limited purpose of credibility. Go ahead."

Miriam pressed play on a little speaker she'd set on the table.

"Yes, my love, I'm marrying him tomorrow. But you are my true love. I'm only doing it for his money."

The courtroom was so quiet you could hear the air conditioning. Leticia's whisper filled it. "How much longer, baby? A year, maybe less. Texas is community property, Reid."

In the first row, Reid Kessler stopped turning his watch.

When it ended, Judge Galvan sat back in her chair. She didn't say anything for a long moment. Then she looked at Leticia.

"Ms. Ramos," she said, "who is Reid?"

Leticia opened her mouth. She looked back over her shoulder at the first row.

Reid Kessler was already standing up. He buttoned his jacket, didn't look at her, and walked down the aisle and out the double doors without a word.

"Reid," Leticia said. Then louder, "Reid!"

The doors swung shut behind him.

Judge Galvan picked up her pen.

"The motion for summary judgment is granted. The plaintiff's claims are dismissed with prejudice. In plain English, Ms. Ramos, that means you may not file them again. The engagement ring is a conditional gift, the condition was not met, and the ring will be returned to the defendant within ten days." She paused and looked at the stack of depositions in front of her. "And I am sending a copy of this file, including your sworn statements in this case and in the Cervantes divorce, to the District Attorney of Nueces County for review."

Leticia sat down slowly, like her legs had stopped working.

"You can't do that," she said.

"I just did," the judge said. "Ms. Contreras, anything else?"

"Just one thing, Your Honor," Miriam said. She turned to me. "Captain?"

I stood up. I took the little velvet ring box out of my jacket pocket, the same one I'd left on the counter next to the coffee maker seven weeks before, and I walked over and set it on the plaintiff's table in front of Leticia.

"For the ring," I said.

She looked at it. Then she opened it.

Inside was the scrap of seating chart, table nine on one side, and on the other, in my handwriting, four words.

"I heard your call."

Leticia stared at it. Then she slowly took the diamond off her finger and dropped it in the box on top of the note, and her hand was shaking so badly it bounced off the velvet and rolled to the edge of the table.

I picked it up, put it in the box, snapped it shut, and put it in my pocket.

"Court is adjourned," Judge Galvan said.

Isabel was out of her seat before the gavel finished. Abel and Danny were right behind her. We walked down the hall together, past the clerk's window and the metal detectors, and out through the big glass doors onto the courthouse steps, into the hot wind coming off the bay.

At the bottom of the steps, Reid Kessler was sitting in his Tahoe with the engine running, and he wasn't waiting for anybody. He pulled out into traffic without his blinker and was gone.

Danny Cervantes watched him go. Then he put out his big scarred hand.

"Captain," he said, "seven years I waited for somebody to call me."

I shook it.

"Danny," I said, "smoke always comes from somewhere."
