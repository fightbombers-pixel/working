"I'm finalizing the divorce, Josiah. Just so you know."

I was standing on the roof of a Lutheran church in Hillyard, forty feet up, with three of my guys and about two hundred and sixty bundles of shingles. My phone was on speaker too, clipped to my tool belt, because my hands were full of a nail gun. My crew heard every word. Twenty-four years of marriage, announced to a roofing crew and a brunch table at the same time.

I set the nail gun down on the ridge. I took the phone off my belt and held it up so I could hear every laugh clearly. I picked out one in particular, a high little giggle I'd heard at every Fourth of July barbecue for twenty years.

"Oh?" I said. "Emily's laughing? Guess she didn't hear you're dating her husband."

The table went dead quiet. Not a fork, not a glass.

I'm Josiah Barnett. I'm 52, and I own Barnett Roofing in Spokane, Washington. Fourteen guys, nine trucks, mostly commercial work, churches and schools and warehouses. Thirty years on roofs teaches you that water always finds the one bad seam. You can have the best shingles money can buy, but if one flashing's not sealed, it'll find it, and it'll rot the whole deck from underneath while the top looks perfect.

Hold on to that.

"Well," he said finally, "it's been a while since we had any of that around here."

Vanessa had a glass of wine and a little microphone that somebody's nephew had set up for toasts. She tapped it and it squealed, and people winced.

"Everybody? Everybody, can I have your attention?" She smiled her brunch smile. "Before we do cake, I just want to say something. I've made a big decision, and I want my family to hear it from me..."

"I'll save you the trouble," I said.

I didn't raise my voice. I didn't need to. The Grange hall has old wooden walls, and it carries.

"Josiah, this is my father's party."

"I know. That's why I'm going to keep it short. One fact for each of you." I looked at Curtis by the coffee urn. "Curtis. Two F-550s, ninety-six thousand dollars, Barnett Roofing on the paperwork, my name forged on the bottom. The lender sent the files to a detective yesterday. The trucks don't exist."

The room went quiet the way a roof goes quiet right before it rains.

Curtis laughed. "That's insane. Josiah, you're upset, I get it, but..."

I turned to Vanessa.

"Vanessa. Hanley Auto has a floor-plan loan for a hundred and forty thousand dollars. You signed as personal guarantor. If Curtis doesn't pay, the bank comes for you. Not for him. For you."

She blinked.

"That's not..." She turned to Curtis. "That's the divorce paper. You said that was the community property paper. You said I had to sign it for my lawyer."

Curtis's smile slid off his face like snow off a metal roof.

"Babe, let's talk about this outside."

"Did I sign for your cars?"

"Vanessa..."

"Did I sign for your CARS, Curtis?"

People were standing up now. Roy's old linemen were looking at each other. Somebody's grandmother whispered, "Who's Curtis?" and somebody else whispered back, "The car dealer, Mom, keep up."

And then Emily walked to the middle of the floor in her red dress.

"Curtis," she said, clear as a bell. "We don't have a pool."

Curtis looked at her like he'd been slapped.

"I went to the bank yesterday," Emily said. "The refinance on our house. A hundred and ten thousand dollars cash-out. It went into Hanley Auto's account the same week you told me it was for a pool. I froze our joint accounts at nine o'clock this morning. And I filed for divorce at ten."

Curtis's mouth opened and closed.

I turned to the last one. Dale, against the wall, with his raccoon eyes.

"Dale," I said. "The police have your plate, the ER report, and my statement. And my son heard your brother tell you to come get the video Wednesday. 'I don't care how,' he said."

Every head in the room swung to Curtis.

"I never said that," Curtis said. "Dale, tell them. I never..."

Dale came off the wall.

"You never said that?" Dale's voice was thick through the bandage. "You stood in my shop and said, 'Go get it, I don't care how!' You said Barnett was a soft old roofer who'd hand it over! I got my nose broke for you!"

"Shut up, Dale!"

"You told me the trucks were a sure thing! You told me nobody'd check!"

"I SAID SHUT UP!"

Curtis grabbed his brother by the polo. Dale shoved him off, and Curtis stumbled back into the cake table, and the sheet cake with the frosting power pole slid off the end and went face down on the Grange hall floor.

Then Curtis turned and came at me.

He didn't get there. Roy Whitlock, 70 years old that day, stepped into his path, took Curtis's wrist in one big lineman's hand, and turned it over, and Curtis went down to one knee on the floor next to the cake with a squeak like a sneaker on a gym floor.

"Not at my party," Roy said, very calm. "Not in my hall."

The front doors opened.

Detective Rosalind Ames came in with two uniformed Spokane Valley officers behind her. She took in the room in about one second: the cake on the floor, Curtis on one knee with Roy holding his wrist, Dale against the wall with blood starting to show through his bandage again.

"Curtis Hanley?" she said.

Roy let go. Curtis stood up slowly. He tried to straighten his blazer. He couldn't find the button.

"Mr. Hanley, you're under arrest for forgery and first-degree theft." She looked past him. "Dale Hanley. Assault in the second degree. Turn around, please."

Dale actually laughed, a high cracked sound through his broken nose. Then he turned around and put his hands behind his back like he'd done it before, because he had.

The handcuffs clicked on Curtis in front of Roy Whitlock's seventieth birthday slideshow. On the screen behind him, Roy was nineteen years old in a hard hat, grinning on top of a power pole.

As they walked him past me, Curtis stopped and looked me in the face. His eyes were wet and furious.

"You couldn't just take the divorce," he said. "You had to do this."

"I didn't do this, Curtis. You forged my name. I just checked."

They took him out.

Vanessa was standing by the empty cake table with her wine glass. Her sunglasses had fallen out of her hair somewhere and nobody had picked them up. She came over to me, and her voice was very small.

"Josiah," she said. "A hundred and forty thousand dollars. They'll take the house. My dad's house too, maybe. Please. You know lawyers. You know banks. Please help me."

"Get a good lawyer," I said. "Not mine."

"I'm the mother of your son."

"You're the mother of my son," I said. "He's the one who found out first. He's been carrying it since June."

She turned and looked at Caleb. Caleb looked back at her. He didn't say anything for a long moment.

"Caleb," she whispered. "Baby. You knew?"

"I sent Dad the video, Mom," Caleb said. "Somebody had to check the roof."

Roy Whitlock bent down, picked the ruined cake up off the floor with both hands, looked at the frosting power pole smeared across the paper tablecloth, and set it back on the table anyway.

"Well," Roy said to the whole quiet room, "I'm seventy years old, and I'm eating my cake. Josiah, Caleb, get over here and grab a fork."
