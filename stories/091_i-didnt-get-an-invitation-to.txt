"Sorry, dear, this event is only for the closest people."

My wife said it at her mother's dining room table on a Sunday afternoon, eight days before her brother's wedding, with nine people sitting around the pot roast and every one of them holding a cream-colored envelope with a gold border. Every one of them except me.

I looked at the envelope in Wanda's hand. It said "Mrs. Wanda Ortega" in fancy calligraphy, and that was all it said.

The groom was her little brother, Ricky. Four years ago I'd lent Ricky forty-eight thousand dollars to keep his boat-detailing shop from going under. He hadn't paid back a single dollar of it, and he was sitting across from me now, buttering a roll and not looking up.

"Only for the closest people," I said.

"It's a small wedding, Larry," Wanda said. "Chelsea's family is very particular about the numbers."

"How many?"

"Two hundred and ten," my mother-in-law said, before anybody could stop her.

I wiped my mouth with my napkin, folded it, and set it on my plate. Then I took my phone out of my shirt pocket, right there at the table, and pulled up the Bar Harbor lobster weekend I'd had saved on my phone since 2009, and I pressed the button that said Book Now.

"Then I'll be in Maine," I said, and I turned the screen around so the whole table could read the confirmation.

Six days later, my mother-in-law was calling me from a ballroom full of folded chairs, and she was the one crying.

What she didn't know was what was sitting in the gray fire safe in my basement, under my father's Purple Heart, with her son's signature on the bottom of it.

My name is Lawrence Ortega. I'm fifty-eight, and I've driven a propane truck for Bay Country Propane in Traverse City, Michigan, for nineteen years. I fill tanks at farmhouses and lake cottages from Kingsley up to Elk Rapids, and the first thing they teach you in this job is the last thing you forget: no signature, no gas. You don't fill a tank, you don't hand over a ticket, you don't do anything until the right person signs the right paper. Hold on to that.

Nobody at the table said a word while I stood up and put on my jacket. Bev, my mother-in-law, was seventy-nine and had never once in thirty-one years been short of something to say, and even she just sat there with the gravy boat in her hand.

"Larry, sit down," Wanda said. "You're making a scene."

"I'm leaving one," I said. "Thank you for dinner, Bev. The roast was good."

I was halfway across the gravel to my truck when the screen door banged behind me.

"Larry! Hey, Larry, hold up."

It was Ricky. Thirty-eight years old, in a golf shirt with his shop's logo on it, the one with the cartoon sailboat I'd paid to have embroidered. He jogged up to my truck with his hands out, like he was calming down a horse.

"Hey, man. Don't take it personal. It's Chelsea's mom, she's got a whole spreadsheet."

"Sure, Ricky."

"Seriously. It's nothing against you." He scratched the back of his neck. "Listen, though. While I got you. Wanda says you'll sign that thing for me by Friday."

"What thing?"

He blinked. Then he laughed, too loud.

"She didn't tell you yet? Oh, man. It's nothing. It's a paper. For Chelsea's dad. She'll bring it home." He slapped the hood of my truck twice. "Friday noon, okay? It's real important, Larry. Like, real important."

He jogged back to the house. I sat behind the wheel and watched him go, and through the dining room window I could see my wife watching me, holding that cream-colored envelope against her chest.

Wanda came home about an hour after me. She put her purse on the counter and her invitation on the fridge, under the magnet from the Cherry Festival, right at eye level where I'd see it every time I got a beer.

Then she sat down across from me at the kitchen table and slid a piece of paper over.

"Before you start," she said.

"I wasn't going to start."

"Just read it, Larry."

It was one page, typed, with a line for my signature at the bottom. It had a heading in bold that said GIFT LETTER AND RELEASE, and under it, it said that I, Lawrence Ortega, confirmed that the forty-eight thousand dollars given to Richard Dorn in March of 2021 was a gift and not a loan, that no money was owed to me, and that I released any claim, lien or security interest on his business equipment.

I read it twice. Then I set it down.

"This says it was a gift."

"It basically was a gift."

"It was a loan. There's a note. You were sitting next to me when he signed it. You picked the pen."

"Larry, he's my baby brother."

"He's thirty-eight."

"He's getting married on Saturday to a girl whose father owns half the cherry orchards on Old Mission and sits on the board of a bank." She leaned across the table. "Bill Ambrose wants a prenup. With financial disclosures. Ricky has to list his debts by Friday noon."

"So list it."

"If he lists it, Bill sees a forty-eight thousand dollar lien with your name on it, and he starts asking questions."

"What kind of questions?"

"The kind that ruin weddings." She tapped the paper with one finger. "It's a formality. Nobody will ever look at it again. You weren't using that money anyway."

"It was our retirement money, Wanda. Half of it was my father's."

"And you'll get it back someday."

"Which part of this paper says I get it back someday?"

She didn't answer that. She got up and ran water in the sink, which she does when she doesn't want to look at me.

"So that's why," I said.

"That's why what?"

"Why I'm not invited. Bill Ambrose is going to be at that wedding, and so is the guy whose name is on the lien. You didn't want us at the same table."

Her shoulders went up a little.

"That's not why."

"Then why?"

"Because you'd stick out, Larry. Those people are lawyers and bankers. You'd stand by the bar in your good shoes and talk about propane prices. You'd smell like the truck." She turned the water off. "I'm trying to protect you."

"From what?"

"From being embarrassing."

There it was. Thirty-one years, and it was nice to finally hear it said plain.

I picked up the paper, folded it once and put it in my shirt pocket.

"Friday noon," I said.

She turned around fast, and I watched her face change. The tight look went away and something softer came in, the look she used to give me at the Cherry Festival in 1992, when she wanted a second elephant ear.

"So you'll sign it?"

"Okay," I said.

She came around the table and kissed the top of my head, which she hadn't done since the Obama administration.

"I knew you'd do the right thing," she said. "You always do the right thing."

She went upstairs humming. I sat at the table with the paper in my pocket and her invitation staring at me from the fridge.

I had said okay. I never said I'd sign.

Now, I want you to understand something before we go any further, because some people hear this kind of story and think the husband's just being stubborn.

It wasn't one dinner. It was the last one.

At our twenty-fifth anniversary party, Wanda told her friends from the dental office that she'd "married down so she'd always be the smart one." When my father died, she skipped the funeral luncheon because Ricky's shop had a grand reopening the same afternoon, a grand reopening I was paying for. And in March of 2021, when Ricky came to our kitchen crying about his creditors, it was Wanda who said, "Larry, you have that money from your dad just sitting there," and it was Wanda who drove us to the lawyer to write up the note, because, as she put it then, "We're not stupid, we want it on paper."

We wanted it on paper. Hold on to that, too.

The next morning, Monday, I was at the yard at six, and at ten I drove my route out past Williamsburg, and at every stop I hooked up the hose and watched the dial and wrote the ticket and got the customer's signature, because no signature, no gas.

Around noon my phone buzzed. It was a text from Wanda.

"Chelsea's mom says there's no plus one available but if you want to drop off a gift card at the hall Friday that would be nice. Don't forget the paper!!"

Two exclamation points. I put the phone back in my pocket and filled the Hendersons' tank.

Tuesday morning I went down to the basement, opened the gray fire safe, and took out the note from under my father's Purple Heart. One page, March of 2021, Ricky's signature at the bottom in that blue pen Wanda picked out. I put it in a folder with the gift letter and drove both papers to a lawyer.

Her name was Rita Galloway. She was sixty, small, with a gray braid and an office above a bike shop on Front Street. She'd written the original note for us in 2021, and she still had the file. She pulled it out of a cabinet without me asking.

"Richard Dorn," she said. "Forty-eight thousand at four percent, payable in monthly installments starting January 2022, secured by his equipment. I filed the lien with the state myself." She flipped a page. "Has he made any payments?"

"Not one."

"Not one in almost four years." She looked at me over her glasses. "Why are you here, Mr. Ortega?"

I unfolded the paper and laid it on her desk.

She read it. She read it again, slower. Then she put it down and pushed it back toward me with one finger, the way you'd push away a plate of something that had gone bad.

"Who wrote this?"

"I don't know. My wife brought it home."

"Do you know what this is for?"

"Her brother's getting married Saturday. The bride's father wants a prenup with financial disclosures. Ricky has to list his debts by Friday noon."

Rita leaned back.

"So in plain words, they want you to sign a paper saying a loan was a gift, so that a man who's about to sign a prenup believes his future son-in-law doesn't owe anybody money."

"That's about it."

"Mr. Ortega, that's a lie on paper, handed to a third party to get him to sign a legal contract. If you sign that, and it ever comes out, you're the man who helped them do it." She tapped the desk. "Don't sign it."

"I wasn't going to."

"Good. And you don't owe anybody an explanation. Not your wife, not Ricky, not the bride's father. If somebody asks you a direct question, you tell the truth. Otherwise you say nothing."

"What about the forty-eight thousand?"

"The note says it's due on demand if he misses three payments. He's missed forty-five." She almost smiled. "You can call it any time you want. But you don't have to call it this week."

"I don't want to wreck a wedding, Rita."

"You're not wrecking anything. You're declining to sign a lie." She handed the paper back. "There's a difference. Your brother-in-law is about to find out what it is."

When I got back to the yard, Sid Ferrante was standing in the door of the office with a cup of coffee. Sid's sixty-three. He started Bay Country with one truck in 1988, and he still drives a route when somebody's out with the flu.

"You booked a vacation," he said.

"How do you know that?"

"Shirley saw it on your phone Sunday night when you came in to get your thermos. She said you were grinning at it like a kid." He sipped his coffee. "Bar Harbor, Maine. In September."

"Lobster weekend. Thursday to Monday. I've got the days, Sid. I've got about forty days."

"You've got fifty-one. I checked." He stepped aside to let me in. "In nineteen years, Larry, you've taken exactly one vacation, and it was to help Ricky Dorn move a boat to Cheboygan."

"Two days."

"Two days. And you came back with a sunburn and a bill for the trailer." He set his cup down. "Go to Maine. Eat a lobster the size of a dog. I'll cover your Thursday."

Shirley Ames, who's run dispatch since before I was hired, didn't look up from her screen.

"Bring me back a shirt," she said. "Something tacky."

I was signing the vacation sheet when a white Lexus pulled into the gravel lot, which you don't see much at a propane yard. Vicky Dorn got out. She's Wanda's younger sister, fifty-one, sells real estate up in Suttons Bay, and she drives that Lexus like it's a parade float.

She came to the office door and didn't come in.

"Lawrence. Can I talk to you outside?"

Vicky is the only person alive who calls me Lawrence. We walked out to the bulk tanks, where nobody could hear us over the compressor.

"Wanda sent you," I said.

"Wanda doesn't know I'm here." She pushed her sunglasses up into her hair. "She told Mom you're sulking. She's telling everybody you'll come around by Friday. She says you always come around."

"I said okay."

"She knows you said okay. She's been bragging about it for two days." Vicky looked at the tanks for a while. "Did you read the paper?"

"I read it."

"Are you going to sign it?"

"Did you come all the way down from Suttons Bay to ask me that?"

"No." She took a breath. "I came because I'm sick of it. Bill Ambrose is giving them a hundred thousand dollars after the wedding. For a house. It's a wedding gift, but only if the prenup is signed and the disclosures are clean. That's Bill. Everything's on paper with him."

"Okay."

"And Ricky promised Wanda fifteen thousand of it. For helping. For getting your signature." She laughed, short and ugly. "She's already spent it, Lawrence. In her head. She's been looking at a condo in Naples with me on Zillow. A one-bedroom."

"A one-bedroom."

"A one-bedroom." She let that sit. "She was going to tell you after the wedding. That's why you're not invited. She doesn't want Bill Ambrose shaking your hand and asking how you two know each other. And she didn't want you there when she started looking happy."

The compressor kicked off, and it got very quiet in the yard.

"Why are you telling me this, Vicky?"

"Because she called me Sunday night and asked me to come down here and lean on you. And I've been leaning on people for my big sister since high school, and I'm fifty-one years old, and I'm tired." She put her sunglasses back on. "Don't sign it. That's all I came to say."

She got back into the Lexus and drove away slowly, so the gravel wouldn't chip her paint.

A one-bedroom condo in Naples, paid for with fifteen thousand dollars of a wedding gift that only existed if I said a loan was a gift. I went back into the office and signed the vacation sheet, and Sid didn't ask, and I didn't tell him.

Wednesday afternoon, Ricky came to the yard.

He parked the shop truck with the cartoon sailboat right across the entrance, where the bobtails pull in, and Shirley had to get on the radio and tell Gus to wait on the road with eighteen hundred gallons in his tank.

"Larry. Two minutes."

I was hosing down my truck. I didn't turn the water off.

"You're blocking the gate, Ricky."

"I'll move it. Two minutes." He came around the puddle in his good sneakers. "Wanda says you said okay."

"I did say okay."

"So you're signing it."

"I said okay, Ricky."

He squinted at me like I was a sign in a foreign language.

"Look. I know how it looks. But you gotta understand, Bill Ambrose is old school. He sees a lien, he thinks I'm a deadbeat."

"Are you?"

"Come on, man." He laughed. "I'm gonna pay you back. After the wedding, I'm gonna be in a whole different situation. A house, Chelsea's money, Bill's connections. I'll pay you back with interest. Double. I swear on Mom."

"So you've got the money after the wedding."

"After. That's the thing. After."

"And to get the money after, I have to sign a paper today that says you don't owe me any."

He opened his mouth. He closed it. You could almost watch the gears try to turn and slip.

"It's just how it has to go, Larry," he said finally. "It was Wanda's idea. She said you'd never read it that close."

"I read everything close, Ricky. I've been reading gauges for nineteen years."

Shirley leaned out the office door.

"Ricky Dorn, move that truck or I'm calling Benny's Towing, and Benny owes me a favor."

Ricky moved the truck. He backed out so fast he clipped our mailbox, and he didn't stop.

It was Wanda's idea. She said you'd never read it that close. I turned the hose off and stood there in the wet gravel for a minute, and then I went in and asked Shirley for a new mailbox post.

Thursday morning Wanda made breakfast.

Wanda doesn't make breakfast. In thirty-one years she's made breakfast twice, once on her mother's seventieth birthday and once in 2016 when she needed me to co-sign Vicky's car. So when I came downstairs at five-thirty and smelled bacon, I knew exactly what time it was.

There was a plate at my spot. Eggs, bacon, wheat toast cut in triangles. And next to the plate, squared up with the edge of the table, was the paper, and on top of the paper was a blue pen. Uncapped.

"Morning," she said, very sweet.

"Morning."

"I made your eggs the way you like them."

"I see that."

I sat down and ate a triangle of toast. She sat down across from me with her coffee and watched me chew.

"So," she said.

"So."

"Chelsea and I talked last night. About you." She smiled. "She said if it's that important to you, she can squeeze you in at the reception. Not the ceremony, the church only holds so many. But the reception. Table nineteen, by the kitchen. With the photographer and the DJ's girlfriend."

"Table nineteen."

"It's a good table. You'll be close to the food."

"That's real generous."

"I thought so." She nudged the pen half an inch closer to my plate. "You just sign that and I'll call her back right now."

"And if I don't sign it, I'm not at table nineteen?"

Her smile stayed, but it got thinner.

"Larry. Don't make this about the paper."

"You put the paper next to my eggs, Wanda."

"Because it's due tomorrow at noon!" She caught herself, put her coffee down, and lowered her voice. "Please. For me. For my family. It's one signature."

I finished the eggs. They were good eggs. Then I wiped my mouth, picked up the paper and the pen, and I could see her lean forward. I put the cap back on the pen and set it down on top of the paper, right in the middle of the table.

"Leave it right there," I said.

"What does that mean?"

"It means leave it right there."

"So you'll sign it tonight?"

"My flight's at six."

"Your flight's today?"

"I told the whole family on Sunday. Thursday to Monday."

"I thought you were bluffing!"

"I don't bluff, Wanda. I drive a propane truck."

I stood up, rinsed my plate and put it in the dishwasher, because I'm not an animal. My suitcase was already by the door. I'd packed it Tuesday night while she was on the phone with Bev.

She followed me into the hall.

"If you leave that paper on the table and get on a plane, I swear to God, Larry..."

"Swear what?"

She didn't have an end for that sentence. I picked up the suitcase.

"Have a beautiful wedding," I said. "Tell Ricky congratulations. I mean that."

I went to work, ran a half day for Sid, and at four o'clock Shirley drove me to Cherry Capital Airport in her Buick, because she said she wanted to see me actually get on the plane.

I was in the security line when my phone buzzed.

"Did you sign it? It's still on the table."

Then another one.

"Larry answer me. Did you sign it somewhere else?? Did you mail it to Ricky??"

I typed back four words, and then I turned the phone off and put it in the gray tray with my belt.

"It's on the table."

I landed in Bangor a little after midnight and drove a rented Malibu down to Bar Harbor in the dark, and I slept like a dead man in a little inn on West Street where the lady at the desk called me "dear" and meant it.

When I turned the phone back on at seven the next morning, it said fourteen missed calls.

I said the number out loud in that little room. Fourteen. Then I took a shower and went down to breakfast.

By nine it was twenty-two. I know because I looked at it on the dock, waiting for the lobster boat tour, and said it again. A man next to me in a Red Sox cap asked if I was counting seagulls.

"Phone calls," I said.

"Bad news?"

"My brother-in-law's getting married tomorrow."

"Ah," he said, like that explained everything. Maybe it did.

The voicemails came in a stack. I listened to three of them on the boat, with the wind trying to take my hat.

The first was Ricky. "Larry, buddy. It's Ricky. Hey, Wanda says the paper's just sitting there. Are you gonna, like, call somebody and say it's okay? Can you do like a text? Call me."

The second was Ricky an hour later. "Larry, it's eleven, it's due at noon, man. Chelsea's dad's lawyer is literally sitting in his office. Please. Please call me back."

The third was Wanda, and she wasn't sweet anymore. "You son of a witch. You did this on purpose. You planned this whole thing. You're going to ruin my brother's life over money you didn't even need. Call me back right now."

I deleted the third one and kept the other two. Rita told me to keep everything.

Noon came and went. I was eating a lobster roll at a picnic table on the pier, and it was the best thing I'd put in my mouth in about four years.

At two, the phone rang, and it wasn't a number I knew. Area code 231, though. Northern Michigan.

I almost didn't answer. Then I thought about Rita saying, if someone asks you a direct question, you tell the truth.

"Hello?"

"Is this Lawrence Ortega?" A man's voice, older, slow and careful, like a man reading a contract out loud.

"It is."

"Mr. Ortega, my name is Bill Ambrose. My daughter is supposed to marry your brother-in-law tomorrow at two o'clock."

"Yes, sir."

"I got your number off the lien paperwork. My lawyer pulled a search on Richard this morning when his disclosure didn't come in. It lists you as the secured party, with your address and your phone number, filed in 2021 by a Rita Galloway."

"That's right."

"I'm sorry to bother you. I understand you're not at the wedding."

"I'm in Maine, sir."

"Maine." He paused. "I'm going to ask you one question, Mr. Ortega, and I'd appreciate a straight answer, because Richard's given me about four different ones today. Ricky says the forty-eight thousand was a gift from you and he's just waiting for the paperwork to clear. Was it a gift?"

The boats were coming in across the harbor, and a gull was screaming at another gull over a french fry. I thought about the paper on my kitchen table with the blue pen sitting on top of it.

"No, sir," I said. "It was a loan. He signed a note for it in March of 2021, at four percent. He's never made a payment."

The line was quiet for a long time.

"Never?"

"Not one."

"And your wife knows this."

"My wife picked the pen he signed it with."

I heard him breathe out, slow, like a man letting air out of a tire.

"Thank you, Mr. Ortega," Bill Ambrose said. "That's all I needed."

He hung up. I sat on that picnic table with the phone in my hand for a while, and then I finished my lobster roll, because it was eighteen dollars and I'm not a wasteful man.

At four o'clock, my phone started ringing again, and this time it didn't stop.

I let it ring the first nine times. I counted them out loud, walking up Main Street past the fudge shops, and the tenth time I saw the name on the screen was Wanda, and I picked up, because Rita had said answer once, give three facts and say goodbye.

"What did you do?" She was screaming so loud I had to hold the phone away from my ear. A woman with a stroller looked at me and crossed the street. "What did you tell him? What did you tell Bill?"

"He called me," I said. "He asked me if it was a gift. I said it was a loan."

"You could have said anything!"

"I said the truth."

"The truth! He walked out of the rehearsal hall, Larry! He walked out in front of the priest and Chelsea's grandmother and the whole wedding party, and he took Chelsea with him!"

In the background I could hear people. Somebody was crying. Somebody else was saying, "Ricky, sit down, sit down."

"Wanda."

"He found everything! His lawyer found everything! The credit cards, the boat loan, the truck, the thing with the guy in Petoskey! Thirty-one thousand on cards, Larry! Ricky didn't list any of it! And then he called you and you...you..."

"I didn't know about the credit cards."

"That's not the point!"

"Then what's the point?"

"The point is you could have signed the paper and none of this would have happened!"

"Three things," I said, as calm as I could, which was pretty calm, because I was standing outside a fudge shop in Maine. "I didn't write that paper. I didn't lie to Bill Ambrose. And I'm on vacation. Goodbye, Wanda."

"Don't you hang up on..."

I hung up. The phone rang again right away. I turned it face down in my jacket pocket and went into the fudge shop and bought Shirley a pound of maple walnut and a T-shirt with a cartoon lobster on it that said "I Got Crabby In Bar Harbor."

That night, the count was forty-one missed calls and sixty-two texts. I said the number out loud in my room at the inn, and then I slept nine hours.

Saturday was supposed to be the wedding. Two o'clock at St. Francis, reception at the Grand Traverse Resort, two hundred and ten guests, table nineteen by the kitchen.

At two-fifteen, Maine time, my phone rang again, and it was my mother-in-law.

I'll be honest with you. I almost didn't pick up. Bev Dorn and I have had a cold war going since 1994, when I said her potato salad needed salt at the Fourth of July picnic and she didn't speak to me until Labor Day. But she was seventy-nine years old, and she'd never once called my phone in thirty-one years.

"Bev."

"Larry." Her voice was small and wet. "Larry, I'm at the hotel."

"Okay."

"Nobody canceled the ballroom. Chelsea's mother was supposed to cancel it and she didn't, she just took her daughter home. So the hotel set it all up anyway. All the tables. All the chairs with the bows on them." She sniffed. "They're folding them up now, Larry. I'm watching them fold up the chairs."

I sat down on a bench by the harbor.

"I'm sorry, Bev."

"Are you?"

"I'm sorry you're sitting there. I'm not sorry I didn't sign a lie."

She was quiet for a while. I could hear the chairs, clack, clack, clack.

"Ricky's here," she said. "He wants to talk to you."

"Bev..."

"Please."

There was a rustle, and then Ricky was on the phone, and he'd been crying, and he'd probably been drinking, too.

"Larry, man. Larry. Listen. Listen to me. Bill says he might reconsider. He said it to Chelsea last night. He said if I come clean on everything and the money's handled, maybe in the spring, maybe."

"Okay."

"So if you sign it now. If you just sign the paper now and we send it to him, it shows him it's handled. It shows him the family's behind me."

"Ricky. The paper says it was a gift. Bill Ambrose already knows it wasn't. I told him."

"So tell him you made a mistake!"

"I didn't make a mistake."

"Oh my God." He made a sound like a kid falling off a bike. "It's forty-eight grand, man. You've got a pension. You've got a house that's paid off. What do you need it for?"

"That's not the question you should be asking."

"What's the question?"

"The question is why a man getting married forgets he owes his sister's husband forty-eight thousand dollars for four years, and then remembers the week of his wedding, when he needs it to disappear."

He didn't say anything to that. Then Wanda's voice came from behind him, sharp, and the phone got grabbed.

"Put it on speaker," she said to somebody. "Everybody needs to hear this."

There was a click and the sound got bigger and hollow, and I could tell I was on speaker in a room with a lot of people in it, in a ballroom with no wedding.

"Larry," Wanda said, loud, for the room. "Your whole family is standing here. My mother. My brother. My aunt Pauline. Chelsea's aunt, who is still here, by the way, and wants to know what kind of man does this. Tell them. Tell them why you'd rather see Ricky ruined than sign one piece of paper."

So I told them.

"Hello, everybody. This is Larry. I'm sorry about today, I really am. Here's the whole thing. In 2021 I lent Ricky forty-eight thousand dollars from my father's money. Wanda was there. Wanda drove us to the lawyer to write the note, because she said, and I'm quoting her, 'we're not stupid, we want it on paper.' Ricky's never made a payment. Last Sunday, Wanda told me I wasn't invited to this wedding because it was only for the closest people. On Monday, she put a paper in front of me that said the loan was a gift. On Thursday she offered me table nineteen by the kitchen if I signed it. I didn't sign it. Yesterday Bill Ambrose called me and asked me a straight question, and I gave him a straight answer. That's it. That's all I did."

The ballroom was so quiet I could hear the hum of the hotel's air conditioning through the phone.

Then a woman's voice I didn't know, older, very clear, said, "Wanda, is that true? About the table by the kitchen?"

"Aunt Pauline, it's not..."

"Is it true?"

"It's more complicated than..."

"Oh, honey," Aunt Pauline said, and that was all she said, but the way she said it, I knew the room had turned.

Somebody took the phone off speaker. It was Ricky again, quieter.

"Larry. Please. I'm begging you. Just... help me fix it."

"It's too late, Ricky."

"It's not too late, it's never too late, you can..."

"It was too late on Sunday," I said, "when your sister told me I wasn't close enough to come. Close people don't get handed a lie to sign next to their eggs. Goodbye, Ricky. I'm sorry about Chelsea. I mean that."

I hung up and turned off the phone, and I walked out to the end of the town pier and watched a schooner come in with its sails down, and I didn't turn the phone back on until Monday.

I flew home Monday afternoon. Shirley picked me up at Cherry Capital in her Buick, put on the lobster shirt right there in the pickup lane over her blouse, and ate a piece of the maple walnut fudge before she even got the car in drive.

"Bev Dorn called the yard," she said, chewing. "Twice. She wants you at her house for dinner tonight. Six o'clock. She says the whole family will be there."

"I bet."

"She said please, Larry. Bev Dorn said please to me. I almost drove off the road."

"Did Wanda call?"

Shirley looked at me sideways.

"Wanda's been staying at her mother's since Saturday night. Vicky told me at the gas station. She said Wanda won't go home because the paper's still on your kitchen table and she can't stand to look at it."

I thought about that paper, still lying there in the middle of the table with the blue pen on top, all weekend, in my empty house.

"Drop me at the house," I said. "Then I'll take my truck to Bev's."

"You're going?"

"I'm going. I've got something for Ricky."

Bev's house is a yellow ranch on the west side with a lawn she pays a neighbor kid to mow and then complains about. When I pulled into the gravel at six o'clock, there were five cars in the driveway. Wanda's Equinox. Ricky's shop truck with the cartoon sailboat. Vicky's white Lexus. And two I didn't know, which turned out to be Aunt Pauline and her husband.

The dining room table was set exactly like it had been eight days before. Same tablecloth. Same pot roast. Same gravy boat. There was a plate at my old spot, and next to it, sitting there like a bad joke, was a cream-colored envelope with a gold border.

I didn't sit down.

"Larry," Bev said, standing up. "Thank you for coming. Please. Sit."

"I'll stand, Bev. Thank you."

Wanda was at the end of the table, and she looked like she hadn't slept since Friday. She had her hands flat on the tablecloth, the way she sits at the dental office when a patient is yelling about a bill.

"We wanted to do this right," she said. "As a family."

"Okay."

"That's for you." She nodded at the envelope by my plate. "Chelsea's mother sent over the leftover invitations. We want you there. When it's rescheduled. In the spring. Front row."

I looked at the envelope. Then I looked at Ricky, who was staring at his roll like it had betrayed him.

"Is it rescheduled?"

Nobody answered.

"Ricky," I said. "Is it rescheduled?"

"Not exactly," Ricky said.

"Bill hasn't returned anybody's calls," Vicky said from her end of the table, flat, not looking at her sister. "Chelsea changed her number Sunday. Her mother sent the invitations back in a box with no note."

"Vicky," Wanda snapped.

"What? He asked."

Bev sat back down slowly.

"Larry, what we're asking," she said, "is that you help us fix it. Ricky can't get married with that debt hanging over him. Bill will never come around. You can make the debt go away. You're the only one who can."

"Sign the release," Ricky said. "Not the gift thing. Just the release. Just let it go. As a wedding present. Please."

"Please, Larry," Bev said.

"Please," said Aunt Pauline's husband, who I'd met twice in my life.

I reached into my jacket. Every eye in the room went to my hand. I took out a plain white envelope, letter-size, with Rita Galloway's address printed in the corner, and I set it down on the tablecloth in front of Ricky, right next to his roll.

"What's that?" he said.

"That's from my lawyer. It says the note's in default and the balance is due. Forty-eight thousand, plus interest. It also says she'll take a payment plan. Two hundred dollars a month, starting November first. The first payment you've ever made."

Ricky stared at it.

"You're calling the loan?"

"I'm asking you to pay it back. Which is what you promised to do in writing four years ago, with your sister sitting right next to you."

"Larry," Wanda said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Larry, please. Don't do this to my family. I'll make it up to you. I'll do whatever you want. I'll stop. I'll stop all of it."

"Stop what?"

She opened her mouth and nothing came out.

"The condo," Vicky said, from the end of the table.

"Vicky, shut up."

"The one-bedroom condo in Naples," Vicky said to the room, very calm, like she was reading a listing. "That's what she'd stop. She was going to buy it with fifteen thousand dollars of Bill Ambrose's wedding gift. Ricky promised it to her for getting Larry to sign. I sent her the Zillow links myself." She looked at her sister. "I'm done lying for you, Wanda. I told you that on Saturday."

Bev turned around in her chair and looked at her oldest daughter like she'd never seen her before.

"A one-bedroom," Bev said.

"Mom, it was just an idea, it was going to be a rental..."

"A one-bedroom," Bev said again, and put her napkin down on her plate, the same way I'd done eight days before.

Wanda turned back to me. Her eyes were wet, and for one second she looked like the girl at the Cherry Festival in 1992, the one who wanted a second elephant ear.

"Larry, please. Come home. We'll start over. It'll just be us. I'll make breakfast every day."

I picked up the cream-colored envelope from beside my plate, the one with the gold border, and I set it down gently in front of her, on top of her hands. Then I went to the front door.

I had my hand on the screen when she said my name one more time.

"Larry."

I didn't turn around.

"Sorry, dear," I said. "From now on, my life is only for the closest people."
