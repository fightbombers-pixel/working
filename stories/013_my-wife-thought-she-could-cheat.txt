"Then let's help her remember," Rosalie said. "Your Honor, Respondent's Exhibit 2 is surveillance footage from the office of Lamb Iron Works, Tuesday, March fourth, a little after two in the morning. Detective Garza of the Pueblo Police Department is present to authenticate it."

In the back row, a heavy man in a corduroy sport coat raised his hand.

"Objection!" Gale said. "Your Honor, this is a domestic matter, not a criminal..."

"It's both, counselor," Detective Garza said, from the back.

"Overruled," Judge Pacheco said. "I'll see it."

They rolled a TV on a cart up to the front, the old kind with a DVD player built in, because the Pueblo County Judicial Building isn't exactly NASA. The bailiff put in the disc.

My shop office came up on the screen. The green chair with the duct tape. My father's picture on the wall.

And there was my wife, kneeling at my safe in her Christmas coat, dialing our anniversary without looking. Opening the CORPORATE folder. Pulling out a long brown envelope from the law office of Harold J. Medina. Holding it up so you could read the return address, clear as day.

"Half of all of it's mine," Yolanda said on the tape. "Once this is gone."

Then Rex Duffy fanning the money. Then Rex's hand on her back. Then the kiss, long and slow, under my father's picture.

Somebody in the gallery said, "Ay, Dios mío," very quietly.

I didn't watch the screen. I'd seen it. I watched my wife.

She sat in the witness chair with both hands gripping the rail, and her face went through about every color a face can go through, and then it went through them again. She looked at Preston Gale. Preston Gale was looking at the TV with his mouth slightly open, like a man watching his house slide down a hill.

The tape ended. The bailiff turned it off. The screen went black, and in it I could see the whole courtroom reflected, small and gray.

Judge Pacheco put her glasses back on.

"Mrs. Lamb," she said. "You're still under oath. I'm going to ask you one question, and I'd advise you to think carefully before you answer it. What was in the envelope?"

Yolanda opened her mouth. Nothing came out.

"Your Honor," Gale said, standing, "I need to confer with my client."

"I'm sure you do, Mr. Gale. You'll have time." The judge looked down at her notes, then up. "The request for temporary orders is denied in its entirety. Mrs. Lamb, you will not have exclusive use of the Mesa house. You will not receive temporary maintenance. And I am referring today's testimony, and that exhibit, to the District Attorney for review as possible perjury." She paused. "Detective Garza, I believe you had something else."

"Yes, Your Honor." Garza stood up in the back. "Just a heads up to the court. We picked up Mr. Rex Duffy about forty minutes ago in the parking lot of this building on a warrant for felony theft from Lamb Iron Works. He was sitting in his truck, waiting for Mrs. Lamb. He's downstairs in a holding cell now. He asked me to tell her he's sorry." Garza scratched his ear. "Then he asked me to tell her it was her idea."

Yolanda made a sound like a door slamming in her chest.

The bailiff helped her down from the witness chair. She came past our table on her way back to her own, and she stopped. Her mascara had gone. Her hand was on the gold cross. She looked down at me, sitting there in my one good suit with my welder's hands folded on the table, and her voice came out small and cracked.

"Sergio. I'm begging you. Twenty-eight years. Please. Don't let them put me in jail for one stupid night. I'll sign whatever you want. I'll walk away with nothing. Just tell them you'll drop it. Tell them it was a misunderstanding."

I looked up at her. I thought about the yellow dress, and the two plates of enchiladas at the Mill Stop, and a long brown envelope held up in the lamplight so Rex Duffy could laugh at it.

"Yoli," I said, "you told the judge there was never any prenup, so I guess there was never any us to drop it for."
